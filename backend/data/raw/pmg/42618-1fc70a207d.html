SAHRC Investigative Report: State of service delivery in selected municipalities in North West

The virtual meeting was convened in two parts, the first session was for the Committee to be briefed by the Public Service Commission and Parliamentary Legal Services on responses received to Negotiating Mandates on the Public Service Commission Bill.

A consistent theme in Members' concerns was the expansion of the PSC's powers and the need for clear reporting obligations. Members further considered the report on the Water and Sanitation Amendment Bill; all provinces present were in support of this Bill and adopted the report.

In the second session Members were further briefed by the South African Human Rights Commission (SAHRC) on the state of water service delivery in selected North West municipalities. The SAHRC had, through its North West Provincial Office (NWPO) over the years, been inundated with service delivery complaints, which spanned a wide range of critical issues: access to water challenges, electricity challenges, housing challenges, sanitation challenges and sewage spillages, refuse collection challenges and dilapidated roads.

This was a violation of the right of residents to an environment that is not harmful to health or well-being as enshrined in section 24 of the Constitution. The SAHRC further highlighted the failure by the municipalities to promote a safe and healthy environment, which was one of the objects of local government listed in section 152(1)(d) of the Constitution. It highlighted there were high water losses and poor water quality in some municipalities in the North West. The SAHRC further highlighted that the systemic failure by the municipalities in the North West Province was also a failure on the part of the provincial government of North West. Sections 139 and 154 of the Constitution mandate the provincial government with the duty to support, strengthen, monitor and intervene where municipalities fail to fulfil their constitutional obligations.

Members expressed grave concern with the violations of residents’ rights to human dignity and the lack of action from the province. The sheer volume of unresolved complaints, confirmed by site inspections and corroborated by oversight reports, showed that the province’s support and intervention had been insufficient or ineffective, perpetuating systemic non-compliance at the local level.

Public Service Commission Bill: written responses to negotiating mandates

Ms Ebrahim, Legal Advisor, Parliamentary Legal Services (PLS), said Adv Makinde would display the responses and all responses were captured in one document for ease of reference, indicating responses from the PSC as well as PLS. She highlighted that the Committee conducted its own public participation process, and the report from that process was shared with Members and the relevant provinces. The report would have been taken into consideration at the time the mandates were prepared. Further, provinces also conducted their own public participation and that culminated in the negotiating mandates which were received over the last two weeks. She proceeded to highlight each province's responses.

The Free State Legislature voted in favour of the Bill, but they requested that the Committee consider the public participation inputs they had made and include them in their report. The first of these being the issue concerning the general constitutionality of the Bill, the concern being that the PSC had no constitutional mandate to deal with local government. They quoted various constitutional provisions in support thereof.

Ms Ebrahim reminded Members that when she made her presentation in response to public submissions and the public participation process, she highlighted the Constitutional issues, indicating this was unfortunately not a black and white issue, it was still very much a grey area in law.

She further indicated that the PSC/the Commission had a legal opinion from legal counsel, where counsel indicated that they did not have a concern about the Constitutionality of the Bill. However, she said there was some risk in this regard, and Members would have to determine whether they would take such a risk or not. She highlighted the possibility of the Bill being litigated upon once passed. She asked Members to think about whether they wanted to err on the side of caution and remove all references to municipalities and other entities. She added that this would, however, require a complete rework of the Bill. She handed over to the PSC to respond briefly.

Commissioner Adv Searle began by apologising on behalf of Prof Fikeni who had planned to join but unfortunately was no longer able to due to another engagement starting at 08:30 in Cape Town. He tried to shift the start time but unfortunately the programme was rigid.

He said, as indicated by Ms Ebrahim, all issues related to constitutional matters were responded to by the PSC, and the Commission further dealt extensively with the opinion provided by legal counsel on the submission made on section 196 of the Constitution, which specifically does not permit the Commission to enter into the sphere of local government. He added that the Commission did not share this view; it called for a contextual and purposeful interpretation of the entire Constitution and not only chapter 10, but all the surrounding provisions. He highlighted that when read conclusively, these led to the understanding that the function and powers of the Commission were not ring-fenced and limited to the public service.

The Commission heard during various submissions that the summary lines which were placed on section 196 and reference made to the utilisation of phrases such as public service and the argument that because 196 only referred to public service, the jurisdiction of the Commission was somewhat ring-fenced and limited to public service. He emphasised that the Commission's view was completely different and opposite to that. He said the PSC submitted that it was not only section 196 which must be analysed, but the entire chapter 10, which was notably titled public administration, and even within section 196, it did not only refer to public service. There were also a few provisions which included public administration, he said, public administration included all organs of state, including municipalities and state-owned entities.

In addition to this, the PSC referred the Committee in its last engagement to two Constitutional Court cases where the court dealt with the issue of Chapter 10, and the analysis from those judgments was very clear. The Constitutional Court said one must not misunderstand the utilisation of terminology such as public service and public administration when deciding on what the intent and purpose of Chapter 10 was. The court said one should rather focus on what the true purpose of chapter 10 is, that is, to ensure that there is a constitutional body that is responsible for performing oversight, monitoring and ensuring that accountability takes place across all spheres of government, not only within the public service but within every sphere of government.

Therefore, while it might be argued that this was a grey area, the PSC contended it was more black than grey because of the provisions of chapter 10, which spoke not only to the public service but also to public administration.

The PSC further referenced specific issues with regard to section 196 (4) (g) which allowed Parliament to extend the powers and functions of the Commission. The PSC made the submission that if one analyses the entire Chapter 10 and looks at s196 (4) (g), it allowed Parliament to enable the Commission to perform additional functions which were not limited only to section 196. Further, when looking at section 195 of the Constitution, which deals with Constitutional values and principles, it was very clear that there's a single PSC and it was, amongst others, responsible for promoting the Constitutional values and principles across all spheres of government. Therefore, with section 195 in mind and additional powers and functions which Parliament could assign to the Commission, it must by its very nature include those specific issues that are listed in section 195 of the Constitution. Adv Searle added that the PSC also submitted other arguments around this, most notably highlighting that there was nothing within the text of section 196 which limited the additional powers and functions to those specific areas of 196 (4) (a-f). If one looks at section 196 it not only referred to public service but by its very definition it included public administration. He added the document before Members provided Members with responses to these very issues.

Adv Makinde said the Commission had tried to explain the principle and logic of its responses to Members in the document and in prior engagements. Eight provinces had supported the Bill. Others made a few comments which the Commission attempted to clarify. The main issue being the extension to local government. She added that the essence of chapter 10 was public administration, and the values of section 195 applied to every organ of state.

She described the resolution of Parliament in 2011, which said the Commission must implement this full mandate as the beginning of everything the Commission had gone through until now. All issues were extensively discussed, and there was agreement that sections 196 and 195 should be read together. She added that section 196 (2) was clear, the Commission was to be independent, must be impartial, exercise its powers and perform its functions without fear, favour or prejudice, in the interest of effective and efficient public administration and a high standard of professional ethics in the public service. She further highlighted that, in public hearings, there was overwhelming support for the Bill, and everyone understood the need for oversight to improve local government.

She further highlighted the provinces in support of the Bill, namely the Free State, Limpopo, Northern Cape, North West, KwaZulu-Natal, Eastern Cape, Gauteng and Mpumalanga. She proceeded to take Members through the Western Cape’s concerns, the province was the only province not in support of the Bill.

Western Cape Provincial Parliament

Adv Makinde said the Western Cape said it did not support the Bill in its current form. The province made the following proposals:

1: The legislature notes that the PSC has a fair amount of discretion as to which departments and entities should be subject to inspections or enquiries. It proposes that a clause be inserted to compel the PSC to report on the same as follows: "must report on and keep an official record of all inspections and enquiries and reasons should be given for why it occurred or alternatively why it did not occur".

2: Proposal to insert a clause to deal with the prevention of cadre-deployment (the appointment of government officials through the political influence of political parties or their members outside the parameters of the law and due process) by any political party in PSC inspections and enquiries.

3: Recommend outlining or defining the process for inspections and enquiries in the Bill itself to ensure due process, as court challenges were both expensive for the taxpayer and may cause bottlenecks in implementation.

4: The legislature notes that the PSC has a fair amount of discretion as to which grievances should be investigated and proposes that a clause must be inserted as follows: "The Commission must report on and keep an official record of all grievances and investigations and reasons should be given for why it occurred or alternatively why it did not occur”

5: The Committee notes the laudable objective for the Commission to be independent and impartial. However, we believe a clause needs to be inserted in Section 14 or alternatively Section 15 that outlines minimum criminal penalties, like other clauses in the Bill, for Commission members or their employees who abuse office.

6: The employees' right, after a collective bargaining process, to approach the Public Service Commission should also be contained in the legislation.

Adv Makinde said inspections were done as part of the PSC's routine mandate. Investigations occurred when there was a reason to investigate. It would conduct a fact-finding mission where it verified facts and issued reports to the relevant departments. Once such reports were issued, the PSC expected the Department to take such recommendations seriously and to report back to the PSC on how these were being implemented. The PSC required that recommendations be implemented within a period of 60 days. She added that departments were by law expected to implement directives, and where they were not going to implement these, they could then take the matter on review in court.

Regarding the keeping of records in the first proposal she said the PSC already did this and emphasised public administration must be governed by democratic values and principles enshrined in the Constitution. The grievance rules further highlighted how to fill out the form and everything required.

Regarding cadre deployment, Adv Makinde said this was a political issue, the PSC did not play politics. She added that the process of appointing commissioners was done through the legislature, and this was an open and democratic process where all Members of Parliament were involved with this, and the process was advertised openly.

Adv Makhinde said if somebody committed fraud or was found wanting of misconduct, they were automatically disqualified from applying for the post of a commissioner. She added that to now say the PSC must monitor how the criminal process works would be outside its scope. The PSC reported to the police and the police conducted their investigations. The responsibility of the PSC was to report crimes to the police and for their processes to lead investigations. In terms of fines for those who were obstructing the PSC in the line of their duty, she said fines were already proposed. This was all included in the Bill and the PSC felt that it was adequate enough in terms of what the Bill could cover.

Further, the collective agreement was monitored by the Labour Relations Act. Bargaining councils such as the GPSSBC and PSCBC were there to deal with collective bargaining issues, and this was already legislated. It did not fall within the mandate of the PSC to deal with collective bargaining issues.

She concluded by requesting Members note all the responses and adopt the Bill accordingly.

The Chairperson requested Ms Ebrahim continue with her presentations. Ms Ebrahim highlighted the second issue raised by the Free State:

The submission raised section 15 as unlawful because it did not mirror the Constitution. Section 196(3) of the Constitution states that, “No person or organ of state may interfere with the functioning of the Commission.” The Bill uses slightly different wording and creates a criminal offence — clause 15(1) states that “No person may hinder or obstruct the Commission in the exercise of its powers or the performance of its functions under the Constitution, this Act, the Public Service Act, or any law relating to local government or any public entity.”

Ms Ebrahim said the PLS was of the view that the term interference would include hindrance and obstruction, which were forms of interference, meaning not to interfere with the performance of something. She added she did not believe it was an extension of the constitutional obligation not to interfere, but rather it was included. Therefore, the PLS and PSC did not require any changes; further, there was no problem with it being made a criminal offence.

Regarding the Limpopo Legislature, she highlighted that the delegation in Limpopo voted in favour of the Bill, and no comments were made. The same applied to Mpumalanga, which was also in support with no proposed amendments. The Northern Cape also supported the Bill with no comments.

The North West was in favour of the Bill and included comments. The first two comments related to clause 4, which dealt with the appointment of commissioners. She noted an error, it seemed the North West may have inadvertently looked at the introduced Bill rather than the B version of the Bill. She said their first comment seeking amendment of the term 'recognised bachelor's degree' to just any recognised degree does not need to change any longer because the B Bill catered to that concern, and the B Bill now merely refers to a level 8 NQF qualification — it no longer describes a bachelor's degree.

The next comment dealt with clause 4 dealing with the years of experience. Ms Ebrahim explained that in the original Bill, it referred to an eight to ten-year experience period, and the Committee had amended this just to read eight years rather than ten years. The North West legislature proposed that it be made ten years. She indicated this was a policy decision and the PSC had indicated it was satisfied with eight years — this was of no legal consequence.

As long as the Committee was satisfied that eight years was a sufficient number of years for the type of experience a commissioner would need to have, there would be no change that would need to be made.

The North West Legislature further highlighted clause 13 which dealt with the investigations and implementation of decisions of the Commission. The legislature questioned whether these clauses were consistent with the constitutional autonomy of municipalities. In their view the role of the PSC was a supervisory role and that their powers and directives should not be binding. The legislature felt that the PSC was only mandated to advise, investigate and report. By allowing the PSC to make these directives would mean encroaching on the autonomy of municipalities. Ms Ebrahim said this issue was dealt with in her presentations last week where she too expressed her concerns with the manner in which the implementation of decisions was dealt with. In her view it was a very limited direction that would be binding everything else would be recommendations. The PSC could not make binding remedial action. She further said the PSC also provided a detailed response in the last meeting, and invited them to add to this if they felt necessary.

The Committee Chairperson asked whether there were any corrections to the Bill which would be effected.

Ms Ebrahim said there was one cross-referencing error which needed to be corrected, and Adv Makinde also indicated a spelling error she picked up in one of the schedules to the Bill; thus, these would need to be corrected.

She explained that both changes were not substantive, and changes thereto would be made by the Bills office, it would not be necessary to draft a C list to send back to the Committee.

The Chairperson invited Members to make comments or pose any questions they might have.

Discussion

Mr E Nzimande (MKP, Gauteng) said it was important to appreciate that public service and public administration did not mean the same thing. He heard the arguments put forward by the PSC however felt it was not prudent, it was important to curb possible future disputes on Constitutional matters. He asked why was the PSC was not considering getting a declaratory order and merely relying on its own interpretation.

Mr Badenhorst said that in reading the responses, these did not read as considerate engagement with provinces but rather as brushing aside inconvenient inputs. He was concerned that real concerns were reduced to policy matters and that the substance of provincial oversights was being ignored. He asked the Department to point to a single instance where a provisional concern had led to a substantive amendment or reconsideration in the Bill.

Mr Badenhorst further pointed to a pattern in the Department's responses where accountability safeguards were proposed and these were dismissed as policy matters where transparency was required - Members were told to rely on internal rules. He emphasised that internal rules were difficult for Parliament to exercise oversight on. Further, where real risks such as political interference were raised, it was simply ignored. The result was that the Bill then expanded the power of the PSC without the necessary checks that you normally would need with no clear reporting obligations. He emphasised there was not sufficient protection against abuse or selective enforcement. He asked why the Department refused to include basic transparency safeguards in the Bill. Specifically a requirement for the PSC to justify why it investigated some matters while it ignored others.

Further, why were critical governance mechanisms being left to internal rules instead of being codified in law, where Parliament could then exercise proper oversight? He highlighted that the Western Cape raised the risk of cadre deployment influencing investigations — why did the Department not engage with this concern at all? What safeguards exist to prevent political interference? How did the Department justify granting the PSC wide discretionary powers without corresponding statutory accountability mechanisms to prevent selective or biased enforcement?

He added that if there were legal opinions out there already warning about encroachment of municipal autonomy, what concrete provisions in this Bill prevented the PSC from overstepping into executive decision making at local government level. Further, what was the purpose of negotiating mandates and the whole process thereof if provincial inputs were neither really meaningfully engaged nor reflected at all in the Department's responses?

Ms Ebrahim addressed the first question by Mr Nzimande on why a declarative order was not sought from the Constitutional Court considering this grey area and disagreement on its constitutionality. She highlighted that the Constitution did provide a process for Parliament to approach a court, and that probably had to do with the separation of powers, because it would mean that every time Parliament was unsure, it would then go to the Constitutional Court and expect them to do the work of Parliament.

She highlighted that what the Constitution does allow for is when a Bill is sent to the President after it has been adopted, and the President has constitutional concerns, the President may then send it back to Parliament for reconsideration. Parliament would then be duty-bound (both Houses) to reconsider those matters raised by the President. Where Parliament felt, notwithstanding the President's concerns, it was still satisfied that the Bill passed constitutional muster, the Constitution then provides that the President may approach the Constitutional Court to get clarity.

An example of this would be the Copyright Amendment Bill and the Performance Protection Amendment Bill which the President had sent to the Constitutional Court in 2024 because he had concerns over property rights. At this stage, Parliament would not be able to approach a court for a declaratory, but certainly if the President had concerns, he may send the Bill back to Parliament. Should that not satisfy he could then take it to the Constitutional Court.

Adv Searle began by addressing the allegation made that the PSC was skirting around provincial inputs and concerns that have been raised by provincial legislatures both at the meeting and during the public hearing process. He said he did not think the PSC could be accused of this, it had thorough engagement on issues raised. Today, they were merely required to respond to particular issues that were raised by provincial legislators, and that was done. He added that the PSC was more than willing to go through each and every province if this was what Members would like. He emphasised extensive engagement was had with provinces through public participation processes, and questions raised by various stakeholders were responded to.

Regarding the question on selective enforcement and bias, he said even if someone looked at the current version of the 1997 Act, one of the obligations on commissioners and the Commission as a whole was to serve and honour the obligations of the Constitution. The PSC served without fear, favour or prejudice. Therefore irrespective of what issues were raised with the PSC and who was involved in particular issues this was not the benchmark for the Commission to decide on which particular matters they would engage in. The PSC's decisions on what matters would be investigated were shaped by its Constitutional mandate. This was the guiding yardstick for the PSC and nothing else.

He said he could not think of anything specific that could be levelled at the PSC for selective enforcement and/or bias. He added the current version of the Bill further entrenched what the Constitution obligated the PSC to do and its constitutional mandate was. Regarding checks and balances, he highlighted that the Commission reported on an annual basis to the National Assembly, and provincial commissioners would report on an annual basis to the provincial legislature. He said these reports included a range of specific issues, including investigations that were done, and if there were any issues with regard to investigations concluded or areas that might be raised about non-investigation of an issue that would have been raised with the Commission. This also served as a platform through which those dialogues and discussions could be held at the National Assembly and with provincial legislatures. He highlighted that, besides the constitutional obligation to report on an annual basis, the PSC engaged regularly with legislatures on a variety of issues. Whether it was the reports they concluded on the financial disclosure framework or the financial misconduct framework within a particular province, these issues were also reported regularly. Therefore there were platforms and mechanisms currently in place and Constitutional mechanisms which allowed the PSC to be open and transparent with not only the legislators but all interested parties with regard to the work done by the Commission.

Regarding the issue of cadre deployment, he was uncertain in what context this was raised, but if one looked at it as a concern regarding the deployment of commissioners that might have political allegiances, there was a provision in the Bill that was inserted by the portfolio committee, which specified that there must be a cooling-off period. Any individual who had been involved in a political sphere would be on a cooling-off period of two years, and the intention behind that was to address the potential for possible cadre deployment.

In addition, commissioners were not appointed in a vacuum. When it came to provincial commissioners and national commissioners, the provincial legislatures and the national assembly, respectively, played a key and vital role during the appointment process of such commissioners. The process entailed that those committees assess, analyse and make recommendations with regard to a suitable candidate. The Bill, in its current form, replicates those constitutional provisions and ensures that there is a measure of independence by virtue of the involvement of the legislators in the appointment process.

Adv Makinde began responses by reminding Members that the Bill was certified by the Chief State Law Advisor, and the essence of certifying it was to ensure that it aligned with the Constitution. She added that the Chief State Law Advisor participated 100% in the parliamentary process. They were there throughout all the discussions and made input thereon. The Bill was not only just put together by the Commission. There was a lot of input that had gone into it and all of these questions had already been addressed. She emphasised that the PSC went to the extent of obtaining a legal opinion from Adv Tembeka Ngcukaitobi, which was also submitted for the record, and the Bill was aligned.

She said the PSC was not allowed to approach the court for a declarative order; it had to go through this process to address issues. The PSC had done everything to seek other opinions and the Bill was constitutional. She further put on record that looking at the purposive interpretation even the legal advisor for the Western Cape itself supported this Bill.

The Chairperson appealed to Members in making their comments not to reopen the process. He reminded Members of the session the Committee had with the commissioners and PLS teams. Hereto, issues were raised and those issues were responded to including the issues which they've tabled today responded to and provinces received such responses. He cautioned against reopening the initial discussion that Members had.

Mr K Ceza (EFF, Mpumalanga) said he did not seek to reopen discussions which were already ventilated but rather wished to thank the PSC for their responses. He asked the SAHRC, when looking at violations documented in its report on the North West, what was an ideal public service administration in terms of ethics and efficiency, accountability, transparency and responsiveness to the people.

He expressed concern with a lot of municipal officials across provinces coming from the national government and said there was a clear issue of capacity in this regard. He asked how the PSC would be empowered to enforce section 195's Constitutional principles of public administration, particularly in municipalities where the Auditor-General’s (AGs) findings reveal persistent Municipal Finance Management Act (MFMA) violations, irregular expenditure and service delivery. Further where would the Commission even begin in this endeavour because there were so many municipalities across South Africa in the same and sometimes worse positions than the North West municipalities.

Dr IS Scheurkogel (DA) said in one of the presentations he noted that the PSC worked closely with the South African Police Services (SAPS). He asked how then were some appointments not criminalised. Further, why did the Bill not criminalise political interference in recruitment? He asked this because he believed it was the core mechanism being used for cadre deployment. He called for the devolution of power to a single accounting officer. Regarding the Head of Department (HoD) power checks, he asked what built-in checks, such as external observers on panels, mandatory reasons, publications, or short listing, would be implemented to prevent this internalised patronage.

Mr Mmoiemang said he took in the fact that the Chief State Law Advisor considered the Bill constitutional and that it met the constitutional test. He said the Bill would be a good platform at the disposal of municipalities to follow directives by the PSC. Therefore he was quite comfortable with the views as expressed by both the PSC and PLS while exercising caution as advised by Ms Ebrahim.

He made the example of the Public Protector which took the South African Broadcasting Commission (SABC) to the Supreme Court of Appeal for the court to say the recommendations of the Public Protector were binding. And if the SABC did not agree with those recommendations, they were to apply for a review in terms of the law. He believed the PSC had also reached that point, the directives of the Commission were binding and there were consequences if they were ignored. If not taken on review then they must be implemented.

Adv Searle responded to the question of what an ideal public administration was and what the Commission perceived as such an ideal. He said the answer could be found in section 195 of the Constitution, that is, in the basic Constitutional values and principles across all spheres of government. The section called for a high degree of accountability, ethical considerations, good governance, listening to citizens and responding to their needs amongst others. The PSC sought to ensure that within all spheres of government, whether local authorities or state-owned entities, these constitutional values and principles formed the benchmark of what drives public office bearers. For public officials to actually implement the mandate of either the provincial government or the national government to ensure that the lives of citizens were improved and constitutional obligations honoured.

He added directive powers essentially gave the PSC more bite in the execution of these by government across all levels. Further, he said Mr Mmoiemang was spot on when he said that the recommendations of the Commission must not be seen as something that can be treated with kid gloves and be ignored and decided upon whether on a whim to agree to it or not. South Africa's courts were now also saying that the recommendations of a constitutional body, and not only the PSC, had authority, and an institution that does not honour those recommendations or provide good reasons why they failed to implement them would be held liable, therefore.

Regarding the question of criminalising political appointments, he asked Adv Makinde to respond to this.

Regarding internal patronage issues and how this would be dealt with, he said in addition to what was in the Bill, there were also other mandates and areas of work that the Commission was currently busy with, most notably the professionalisation framework, which was endorsed by Cabinet in 2022. In such a process, the Commission played both an oversight role and an implementer role to a certain degree. The Commission was clear on it not being involved in the actual appointment of senior officials because there might be a conflict of interest but it sought to ensure that there was a measure of accountability and independence that filters through the selection and recruitment process to amongst others ensure that issues such as deployment of unsuitable and unskilled individuals could be detected with the involvement of perhaps an expert. The framework asks the Commission to actually facilitate the creation of a panel of experts that would, amongst others, assist departments across national, provincial, as well as local government spheres where these experts were utilised. This was to ensure the most suitable, most skilled and most equipped candidate was eventually appointed.

Commissioner Ms Yasim Bacus added that the directives of the Commission were merely discretionary, South Africa accepted the enforceable remedial powers from institutions such as the AG of South Africa, including at the municipal level. Therefore the same principle of strengthening accountability should apply here. The Bill merely seeks to ensure that those constitutional principles are upheld consistently across the entire state, across all three spheres of government. Municipalities would still retain their executive authority but accountability for ethical and professional administration must apply uniformly to all spheres of government.

At the South African Local Government Association (SALGA) convention held in December 2025 at the International Convention Centre in Durban, the Deputy President and Minister, Mr V Hlabisa, called for the support of the Bill in its current form.

Regarding criminalising cadre deployment, she said the PSC did not have powers over the executive, it only dealt with administrative issues. What it could assist in was helping build the Human Resources checks and balances based on its powers in the Constitution as it relates to recruitment selection, etc. She said the PSC wrote guidebooks and manuals for provincial and national governments to help professionalise the public service and enforce and entrench ethics and integrity. She highlighted the PSC's consultation process with municipal structures, labour organisations and municipalities. The PSC went through an extensive process last year, with a lot of the municipalities and even metros calling upon them to help them deal with interference in their administration and to deal with the administrative executive divide.

Adv Makinde added on the question raised by Mr Ceza on what the PSC would like to see happen in local government, she reiterated the importance and actualisation of constitutional values. These must be adhered to across the board. She said the PSC would like to see consistent application thereof and accountability to be improved. Further it was important that professionalisation extends to all three spheres of government to further ensure improved service delivery.

Further, regarding cadre deployment, she said irregular appointment was taken very seriously by the PSC. The Commission issued many manuals on this and would further improve to ensure minimal political interference. Should it be established that such an irregular appointment has happened, it would be taken under review. She highlighted the PSC had done a lot of work on this and further established a panel of experts to ensure the process of hiring HoDs and all high-level positions was done correctly, and those regular appointments were avoided as much as possible.

The Chairperson thanked the PSC delegation for their responses and asked Members to place their votes on the record. He was aware that Members were not allowed to contradict their respective provincial mandates, but for the purposes of the record, it was important for their votes to be placed on record. He asked Members to support Mr Mmoiemang's motion for the support of the responses provided. Once this was done the next step would be to make the proposal to the National Council of Provinces (NCOP) for approval. He requested Members register their support or non-support of the Bill.

Dr Boshoff began presentations by highlighting the rationale for the report. The Commission had through its North West Provincial Office (NWPO) over the years been inundated with service delivery complaints, which spanned a wide range of critical issues:

- access to water challenges;

- electricity challenges;

- housing challenges;

- sanitation challenges and sewage spillages;

- refuse collection challenges; and

- Dilapidated roads.

Living in dams of sewage

Residents in municipalities such as Matlosana, JB Marks, Kgetlengrivier, Ngaka Modiri and Rustenburg were frequently exposed to raw sewage overflowing into streets, rivers and even private yards.

This was a violation of the right of residents to an environment that is not harmful to health or well-being as enshrined in section 24 of the Constitution.

This was further a violation of the National Environmental Management Act, which obliges every organ of state to prevent pollution and ecological degradation and remedy it where it has occurred.

It is also a failure by the municipalities to promote a safe and healthy environment, which was one of the objects of local government listed in section 152(1)(d) of the Constitution.

Living in thirst

Several municipalities were failing to supply households with clean and sufficient water, as mandated by section 27(1)(b) of the Constitution and given content by the Water Services Act.

High rates of water interruptions, i.e., Maquassi Hills stands at 90,6%, Kgetlengrivier at 88,6%, Moses Kotane at 73,1%, Moretele at 72,1%, Rustenburg at 71,6%, Dr Ruth at 68% and Ngaka Modiri at 67,7%.

High water losses in many municipalities in the North West.

Poor water quality in some municipalities in the North West.

Living with litter

Several municipalities such as Mahikeng, JB Marks, Kgetlengrivier, Maquassi Hills and Kagisano Molopo, were failing to address the scourge of illegal dumping.

Low rates of refuse removal, i.e., Kagisano Molopo at 21.9%, Kgetlengrivier at 21.9%, Moretele at 33,9% and Mahikeng at 34.6%.

Violation of not only residents’ rights in terms of section 24 of the Constitution and municipalities’ obligations in terms of the Waste Act, but it also violates residents’ right to human dignity.

He emphasised that the failure by some municipalities to provide electricity violates residents’ rights of access to adequate housing and basic services. It also undermined residents’ right to human dignity.

Provincial Government mandated but missing

Dr Boshoff further highlighted the systemic failure by the municipalities in the North West Province was also a failure on the part of the provincial government of North West. Sections 139 and 154 of the Constitution mandate the provincial government with the duty to support, strengthen, monitor and intervene where municipalities fail to fulfil their constitutional obligations.

However, the sheer volume of unresolved complaints, confirmed by site inspections and corroborated by oversight reports, shows that the province's support and intervention have been insufficient or ineffective, perpetuating systemic non-compliance at the local level. It was therefore a case of the provincial government of the North West being mandated yet missing in action as the collapse of municipalities continued under its watch.

He proceeded to outline municipality-specific issues, highlighting key problematic areas all beset by persistent water shortages and sewage failures, together with failure to manage illegal dumping and stormwater flooding. He further provided Members with directives the Commission issued, which include but are not limited to, the:

- Deployment of operational teams to resolve sewage spillages within 24 hours of reporting;

- Development and implementation of costed, time-bound action plans within 90 days to permanently address systemic service delivery challenges; and

- Provincial government to review all current interventions at the cited municipalities and implement any additional interventions.

He closed presentations by providing Members with actions taken by the Commission post-release of its report, together with recommendations to the Committee.

She said she was happy that both municipalities had an action plan in place to address the SAHRC concerns and submitted same to the office of the SAHRC and MEC.

Responses

SAHRC

Dr Boshoff said it was well documented that the Commission had some challenges with regard to implementation of its recommendations. There was a simple disregard for the Commission's recommendations, not only by municipalities but also by many stakeholders who simply ignored the recommendations. When the new cohort of commissioners arrived in January 2024, they sought to especially engage with municipal service providers to have certain powers in terms of the South African Human Rights Commission Act, but it was also important for the Commission to escalate its findings and the recommendations issued to Parliament. The reason for that was that the SAHRC was accountable to the National Assembly, and it had high regard for the work of the NCOP, specifically this Committee, when it came to having an oversight over local government in all the provinces.

He said the SAHRC was therefore extremely relieved when it participated in the program of this Select Committee last year in the Free State province. The SAHRC was also extremely grateful for the opportunity to present this report before the Committee.

He agreed with Mr Ceza to fix local government required all hands on deck. The SAHRC required the support and the assistance of both houses of Parliament and all chapter 9 institutions, everyone including the civil sector and the business sector etc.

He highlighted the SAHRC had utilised its powers in terms of what was available to it. Dr Boshoff had presided over subpoena hearings in the North West province since last year. The SAHRC subpoenaed a number of municipalities to appear before them for simply not providing documentation, or even replying to letters it had written to them, ignoring correspondence. The SAHRC further decided to conduct this investigative inquiry to have something tangible, to have a report that it could table in Parliament and upload on its website. He emphasised that many stakeholders, including the public, were extremely perturbed about the state of service delivery in the North West province.

The SAHRC has litigated against many municipalities and will not stop litigating against municipalities' funds permitting. It would continue to subpoena municipalities and provincial government.

He highlighted the role of provincial government as per sections 139 and 154 of the Constitution, the provincial executive must support, strengthen, monitor and intervene when municipalities fail to fulfil their Constitutional obligation. The sheer volume of unresolved complaints confirmed by its site inspections and corroborated by the oversight reports showed that the support provided by provincial government and the intervention made was insufficient and ineffective in dealing with these service delivery challenges.

The SAHRC made specific directives for the attention of the provincial government and called upon them to monitor compliance with the remedial plans. Provincial government must facilitate emergency funding where local budgets were inadequate and address the security of service delivery infrastructure.

The SAHRC further briefed the Committee about the sabotage of municipal infrastructure, this was further confirmed by the two municipalities today. He added that the SAHRC's enquiry was inquisitorial in nature. When conducting these investigations it did not accuse municipalities of wrongdoing. It inquired into what the challenges were, and many of the municipalities admitted to the existence of water mafias and abuse of water tankering to benefit service providers, municipal officials, and even councillors.

Dr Boshoff was grateful for the invitation to advise the Committee on how to deal with the scourge of water tankering, the scourge of sabotage of municipal infrastructure, the vandalism of municipal infrastructure, etc. The SAHRC developed a policy brief, which was influenced by top academics, lawyers, and internal lawyers of the SAHRC. It was a quality policy brief in which it identified the relevant legislation under which these perpetrators should be prosecuted.

He recommended that the municipal managers should lay criminal charges against these perpetrators and further take the scourge of water tankering seriously. This was happening everywhere, in all the provinces and the SAHRC would like to see municipal managers opening criminal cases and utilise the recommendations that it had made in its policy brief. He said the SAHRC was also willing to present the policy brief to the Select Committee. It was of the view that it would address the sabotage of municipal infrastructure that was taking place. The brief was also shared with DWS, and they had a meeting with the Minister to workshop the policy brief. The brief was also sent to the Presidency, National Prosecuting Authority, the National Commissioner of SAPS and many other stakeholders for comments, together with the Office of the Chief Justice for comment.

He emphasised that the SAHRC required the assistance of the Committee to ensure that its recommendations were implemented to adequately address the systemic service delivery challenges at the local government level.

Further discussion

Mr Nzimande asked the Commission why they had not asked municipalities to respond to the failures that were found in the respective municipalities. He agreed that the Committee would have to work closely with the Commission to hold these municipalities accountable.

Addressed to the municipalities, he asked what the financial implications were of always having to bring in service providers, had the municipalities conducted any kind of analysis on this and what consequences management was in place for service providers who failed to deliver.

He said municipalities mentioned hiring consultants, how many consultants were currently hired and what the financial implications of adding more were. He highlighted that the AG expressed serious concerns around the appointment of consultants and the huge financial burden this had on municipalities.

Lastly had Madibeng opened criminal cases against those conducting these syndicates and providing bogus invoices.

Ms M Makesini (EFF, Free State) addressed her questions to Madibeng and the issue of the section 106 report, which had not yet been tabled. She said the Department was given a deadline on when the report should be submitted, so why had the report still not been tabled? She suggested the Committee write to the province for a response on the matter.

She emphasised there was a clear issue across all municipalities in the North West because all of them were under some form of financial recovery plan. This showed clear issues in support from the provincial government in failing to assist these municipalities to come out of recovery plans. She said she was happy with the SAHRC’s presentation and the recommendations made. She called for consequence management on the part of the MEC for failing to exercise its responsibility and assisting municipalities to function properly.

Addressed to Kgetlengrivier Municipality she asked regarding the consultants and whether there were any plans to ensure a transfer of skills from these consultants to municipal officials to ensure that when their contracts come to an end, the municipal officials had been empowered and up-skilled to address issues themselves in future and further improve the status of the municipality.

Regarding councillors of Madibeng who were part of causing the problems at the municipalities she agreed criminal charges should be opened against them. She said they should be further suspended pending investigations against them.

SAHRC Response

Dr Boshoff said he agreed with Members on the municipalities and the involvement of provincial government. He highlighted that the SAHRC was informed last year, when it met with the MEC, that there was a plan being implemented to urgently address the service delivery challenges in the North West province. He emphasised the plan would remain merely a plan if no implementation were performed. He was very sceptical that the plan would bear any fruit. The Commission was still inundated with complaints coming from ordinary residents of the province and if government does not address the recommendations nor directives issued by the Commission in dealing with the challenges in the province he did not see any progress in service delivery.

The Commission required the assistance of the Committee in ensuring that those directives were implemented by the provincial government.

The Chairperson read the chat, one of the questions came from Chief Whip, Cllr Peter Padi who asked why the municipal manager had suspended all the PMU managers. He asked the municipal manager to explain why he suspended all PMU members and how this affected spending of the MIG.

The Chairperson asked why the question was asked on the platform considering the Cllr came from the same municipality. He asked Cllr Padi to speak to this. No response was received - Cllr Padi seemed to be kicked out of the platform. He invited Members to proceed with their questions in the meantime.

Mr Mmoiemang addressed his questions to the SAHRC. The Commission indicated that its report was presented to the Minister and Members of Executive Council (MINMEC) and Cabinet Lekgotla. He assumed a consolidated action plan would need to be followed up by the Committee with the MEC of CoGTA and individual municipalities.

He requested that the municipalities send the presentations that they made today to the Committee. For the Committee to further exercise its oversight powers. The Commission identified certain municipalities and service areas, and he imagined there were hotspots, or rather high-risk areas, which required speedy intervention. He imagined the remedial action with the Commission required some minimum action. He said this was important to guide Members on what needed to be done on the part of the provincial government and the respective municipalities. He said the Committee would need to sit with CoGTA on its efforts to resolve some of the issues highlighted today.

Mr Badenhorst thanked the Commission for its presentation, it was clear to him that the report laid bare a very disturbing reality for residents in parts of the North West. Not only were residents underserved, they were also subjected to conditions which violate their most basic Constitutional rights. These were communities living in raw sewage, unreliable water supply, failing sanitation, and collapsing infrastructure.

He emphasised this was not due to one single failure but a cumulative effect of administrative breakdown, political neglect and weak provincial oversight. He asked whether this was a funding problem or a failure of governance and accountability problem. Taking this into account, he sought to make use of the opportunity to ask the commissioner whether the service delivery failures were primarily the result of weak administration such as poor planning, lack of maintenance and failure to execute or was it a lack of funding. Had the Commission found any evidence of effective consequence management within municipalities, or does the persistence of these failures point to a culture of no accountability among officials?

He asked the two municipal managers how many criminal cases had been laid in terms of infrastructure sabotage in the respective municipalities. And if no criminal cases had been laid in terms of infrastructure sabotage, why had this not been done and when would these criminal cases be opened?

He further asked Dr Boshoff to what extent political leadership, for example, mayors and councils failed to exercise oversight and intervene despite clear ongoing service delivery breakdowns. And would it be fair to conclude that in many instances, political leadership had allowed these conditions to persist, effectively normalising the violation of residents' rights.

To the province, he asked whether, in its view, the current provincial interventions were sufficient or whether they were failing to address the systematic collapse identified in the Commission’s report.

The Chairperson called on the municipalities to respond to the questions raised.

Kgetlengrivier Local Municipality Responses

The Municipal Manager began by confirming several criminal cases had been opened with SAPS and was happy to share proof thereof, i.e., case numbers and follow-up which were made to SAPS.

Regarding mention of pit toilets in the Derby area, he said these were not necessarily pits toilets. This was a rural area and did not have the bulk infrastructure necessary; the toilets provided were regarded as acceptable standards for the area.

Regarding illegal dumping he said the municipality was collecting refuse in all its townships and towns. There were just a few areas where they were unable to access; however, there were dumping sites created for such purpose, and there were consistent efforts to clean such dumping sites. Further, the municipality was now promulgating a bi-law on waste management where it would implement penalties against perpetrators to ensure a reduction in illegal dumping sites.

Regarding the hiring of consultants, he said these were hired for issues of township establishment, where the municipality would not necessarily have the necessary capability, such as EA or geotechnology studies. Thus the appointment of a panel of consultants to then do the work.

Madibeng Municipality Responses

The executive Mayor appreciated the contributions made by Members, he said these further assisted in guiding the municipality on a way forward. He said in the last interruptions the Municipality experienced, it approached the local police station to lay criminal charges against implicated individuals, including councillors; however, their attempt to do so was rejected by the police.

Further, the municipality opened several cases against individuals and service providers who were involved in the disruption of service delivery, including interfering with infrastructure, but very little success has been had thus far in investigations and the arrest of such individuals. The only time the municipality saw prosecution was with respect to duplicate payments. Where officials could process one invoice thrice or twice, and that was picked up, a case was opened, and those implicated, in particular service providers, were criminally charged.

The municipality went to court to obtain an interdict in order to address interruptions and to ensure that the fiddling of municipal offices is done away with. He added that a piece was returned to the municipality due to such an interdict.

Regarding section 106, as mentioned, the municipality was waiting on the MEC’s response on the matter.

The Chairperson said the HoD from the Department responded and said a copy of the report had been issued to the Speaker of the municipality, but they were interdicted by a certain service provider from coming to the council and presenting the report. The HoD further alleged that the Department had posted the report to the Minister of CoGTA, National Treasury, NCOP and provincial legislature. The Chairperson confirmed such report had not been received by the Committee and would follow up on this.

The HoD further indicated that this week the MEC would be consulting with the municipality. The executive Mayor said he welcomed this.

Regarding the Melodie electricity interruptions, the Municipal Manager agreed that the community suffered; however, he was committed to escalating and resolving the issue. As things stand, there weren’t any outstanding cases on electricity interruptions — all issues were resolved.

He further confirmed the municipality would be meeting with the Hawks the coming Friday regarding one or two cases which the municipality intended to open. Especially where revenue was lost, while the municipality generated about R6 million a day. It lost about R12 million last week just because of the strike, and this was the case, the police station refused to open and said it was a sabotage case which would need the Hawks. He emphasised cases would be opened where there were elements of criminality.

Regarding the Siwa plant (sp) which was raised he said the municipality was also not happy when it visited the plant with Members thus the contract with the service providers was terminated. They were working under a grant, specifically the Water Services Infrastructure Grant (WSIG) and had since been replaced. The new service providers were being monitored and the municipality hoped to see improvements.

He agreed the municipality could not celebrate the qualified audits; they hoped for an unqualified audit in December last year. Unfortunately, the municipality had revenue issues , together with issues with the meter readings and with its accounting. However, when looking at the audit paragraphs, they had reduced significantly, and he was confident that more improvements would be made.

He added that many supply chain functions were moved to the CFO's office because of consistent issues; he believed this would further bring great savings to the municipality and better cost management. Further, the municipality requested the SAHRC to formally subpoena the municipality, together with Rand Water and the City of Tshwane, because the people in the Kgabalatsane area and surrounding areas were suffering because the Madibeng Municipality was unable to do all it could because the valves were controlled by the City of Tshwane and Rand Water. The municipality was now waiting for that date. He spoke to the Rustenburg office last week and was given a guarantee that a meeting would take place before them by the end of March. He highlighted that the municipality remained willing to learn and be guided by the Committee to improve its work.

Closing Remarks

The Chairperson closed discussions by appreciating the municipal officials for their efforts and contributions together with the SAHRC for its intervention and presenting its report. Regarding next steps he said the Committee would make recommendations to the NCOP on the steps that would need to be followed. The Committee would further visit provinces and call them before the NCOP for them to give a detailed account on how they were processing recommendations made by the SAHRC and the Committee.

The Committee would further follow up on the issues raised on the section 106 issue with Madibeng and other policy issues when it came to human settlement and ablution facilities more especially in rural housing development.

He encouraged both municipalities to do away with hiring more water tankers and to try and secure and build their own internal capacity to buy those vehicles and have staff that would deliver water to the people instead of water tankers. Overall, he called upon municipalities investing in their infrastructure to avoid reliance on alternative service providers.

The Committee would continue to collaborate with municipalities and the SAHRC to further strengthen its oversight function. He expressed thanks to the Committee support staff for coordinating the meeting and all their support.

The meeting was adjourned.
