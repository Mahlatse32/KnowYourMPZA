Division of Revenue Bill & Special Appropriation Bill: DBE and Department of Transport input; with DBE Ministry

Standing Committee on Appropriations

11 March 2026
Chairperson: Dr M Maimane (BOSA)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Department of Basic Education (DBE) and the Department of Transport (DoT) were invited to present their respective inputs on the 2026 Division of Revenue Bill, Appropriation Bill and Special Appropriation Bills.

The DBE indicated that basic education constituted a significant portion of public expenditure and required scrutiny, particularly in relation to learner transport. It was explained that learner transport and school infrastructure were critical to ensuring equitable access to education, especially in rural areas. The DBE highlighted that while policy and oversight were exercised at national level, implementation occurred at provincial level, resulting in variations in delivery. The learner transport programme, implemented jointly with the DoT under the National Learner Transport Policy (2015) and overseen by the National Inter-Departmental Committee (NIDC), was described as essential but constrained by funding limitations and uneven provincial capacity. It was further noted that infrastructure backlogs remained substantial, with an estimated requirement of over R200 billion to address deficits, and that current funding allocations were insufficient. The DBE emphasised the need for improved planning, maintenance, and alternative financing mechanisms, including public–private partnerships, to accelerate infrastructure delivery.

The DoT presented on financial allocations under the 2025 Special Appropriation and the 2026 Division of Revenue Bill, highlighting significant investments in freight rail corridors, port infrastructure, and the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa (PRASA). Conditional grants such as the Provincial Road Maintenance Grant (PRMG), Rural Road Asset Management Grant (RRAMS), and Public Transport Operations Grant (PTOG) were outlined. Concerns were raised regarding the Public Transport Network Grant (PTNG), which was being phased out due to persistent underspending and implementation challenges, despite ongoing contractual obligations with municipalities and taxi associations. The DoT further outlined the shared responsibility for scholar transport with the DBE and indicated that achieving full coverage would likely require a dedicated conditional grant and clearer institutional arrangements.

PRASA reported progress in rail system recovery, noting that most rail corridors had been restored, and that signalling and telecommunications upgrades were underway. The Rolling Stock Fleet Renewal Programme, including the Isitimela Sabantu project, was highlighted as a key initiative to modernise the passenger rail fleet, although it faced a significant funding shortfall. The government was exploring alternative financing mechanisms, including the establishment of a Rolling Stock Operating Company (ROSCO), to attract private sector investment.

During the discussion, Members raised concerns about inadequate learner transport coverage, particularly in provinces such as KwaZulu-Natal, and the lack of clear accountability between the DBE and DoT. Questions focused on funding models, the potential introduction of a dedicated conditional grant, and persistent underspending of infrastructure grants despite significant backlogs. Concerns were also expressed regarding infrastructure delivery challenges, including poor performance by implementing agents, project delays, and discrepancies between reported and actual conditions at schools. In relation to transport, Members highlighted risks associated with PTNG contracts, governance and oversight issues, PRASA’s financial sustainability, and the need for improved safety, security and service expansion beyond metropolitan areas.

Meeting report

Opening remarks

The Chairperson welcomed the Department of Basic Education and the Department of Transport for presenting before the Committee. He noted apologies from the Minister of Transport, Ms Barbara Creecy, the Deputy Minister of Finance, Mr Ashor Sarupen, and Mr N Kwankwa (UDM). The Chairperson acknowledged the presence of the Minister of Basic Education, Ms Siviwe Gwarube, and Deputy Minister Dr Reginah Mhaule.

The Chairperson clarified that the Committee’s mandate was not to engage with curriculum or content matters, which fell under the Portfolio Committee on Basic Education. Instead, the Standing Committee on Appropriations focused on resource allocation, budgeting, and oversight of appropriations. In line with the Money Bills and Related Matters Act, the Committee was required to interrogate budgets and may propose amendments to the Division of Revenue Bill, Appropriation Bill, and Special Appropriation Bills following their tabling in Parliament.

Remarks by the Minister of Basic Education

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, noted that basic education constituted one of the largest areas of public expenditure, warranting close scrutiny of spending, particularly on learner transport. She highlighted recent tragic incidents involving learners and emphasised the need for improved coordination between government departments to enhance learner safety and strengthen regulatory oversight of service providers.

She highlighted that their presentation would focus on learner transport and school infrastructure delivery, both critical to ensuring equitable access to education, especially in rural and under-resourced areas. The Minister clarified that while the national department was responsible for policy, oversight, and support, implementation and service delivery largely occurred at the provincial level. Conditional grants were transferred to provinces via National Treasury, while most programmes, including learner transport, were funded through the provincial equitable share.

The Minister explained that although the national department set the policy and regulatory framework, implementation of learner transport varied across provinces. In some provinces, the function was managed by departments of education, while in others it was administered by departments of transport. She noted ongoing efforts between the national departments of Basic Education and Transport to establish a more uniform policy framework, while still accommodating provincial differences.

She further stressed that inadequate provision of learner transport had immediate negative consequences, including late arrival, absenteeism, and declining academic performance. She emphasised the importance of holding provinces accountable for ensuring reliable and timely transport for qualifying learners. Strengthening the regulatory framework governing learner transport was a key priority currently being addressed jointly by the two departments.

On school infrastructure, the Minister reiterated that the national department set norms, standards, and regulatory frameworks, while provinces were responsible for implementation and delivery. Infrastructure funding was primarily transferred to provinces, including through conditional grants. She acknowledged persistent challenges in infrastructure delivery and indicated that the sector faced an estimated R140 billion infrastructure backlog, underscoring the scale of investment required to address deficits in school facilities.

Presentation by Department of Basic Education: Scholar Transport, Progress in the Provision of School Infrastructure in the Basic Education Sector

Mr Ramasedi Mafoko, Acting Deputy Director-General, led the presentation to the Committee. 

Status on Provision of Learner Transport

The learner transport programme was implemented through a joint institutional arrangement between the DBE and the DoT under the National Learner Transport Policy (2015). Oversight was coordinated through the National Inter-Departmental Committee (NIDC), which monitored implementation across provinces and reported to the Ministers of Transport and Basic Education. Implementation arrangements varied across provinces: in four provinces (North West, Mpumalanga, Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal) the programme was managed by provincial departments of transport, while in five provinces (Free State, Gauteng, Limpopo, Northern Cape and Western Cape) it was administered by provincial departments of education. This split governance structure required close coordination between departments responsible for identifying qualifying learners, contracting transport services, and ensuring compliance with vehicle safety standards.

The programme provided subsidised transport to a substantial number of learners attending ordinary public schools as well as learners with special education needs, highlighting its importance for ensuring access to schooling, particularly in rural areas where long travel distances remained a major barrier. The DBE focused primarily on identifying qualifying learners and monitoring the programme’s impact on attendance and punctuality, while the DoT oversaw safety standards, vehicle specifications, roadworthiness inspections and driver licensing through agencies such as the Road Traffic Management Corporation (RTMC). The programme therefore functioned as a cross-sector intervention linking education access with transport regulation and service delivery.

There were significant funding constraints. Learner transport was funded mainly through the provincial equitable share, which placed pressure on provincial budgets and limited the expansion of services. The 2025/26 expenditure figures indicated substantial spending commitments across provinces, but demand continued to outstrip available resources. Funding shortfalls affected the ability of provinces to extend transport coverage to all qualifying learners and to maintain service quality. Additional challenges included uneven implementation capacity across provinces, safety compliance requirements, and coordination challenges arising from the shared mandate between education and transport authorities. These factors collectively constrained the programme’s ability to fully meet the transport needs of learners, particularly those in remote rural communities.

Status on Provision of School Infrastructure

Infrastructure backlogs remained substantial despite several major national programmes. Infrastructure delivery occurred through three key programmes: the Provincial Infrastructure Programme, the Accelerated School Infrastructure Delivery Initiative (ASIDI) launched in 2011, and the Sanitation Appropriate for Education (SAFE) initiative introduced in 2018 to eradicate pit toilets in schools. These programmes focused on replacing inappropriate school structures, providing water and electricity, upgrading sanitation facilities, and improving essential infrastructure such as fencing, laboratories, libraries and sports facilities. Funding for these interventions was primarily drawn from the Education Infrastructure Grant (EIG) and the School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant (SIBG).

The data presented across infrastructure categories demonstrated the scale of infrastructure deficits across the school system. Tables covering water supply, electricity, fencing, classrooms and specialised facilities showed that many schools still lacked adequate infrastructure or relied on partial and temporary solutions. In several cases schools depended on multiple water sources or incomplete security infrastructure, indicating uneven access to basic services. The persistence of inappropriate buildings and overcrowded classrooms further highlighted the continuing need for large-scale capital investment to meet minimum infrastructure norms and standards.

Financial estimates presented in the briefing illustrated the magnitude of the funding challenge. According to the DBE’s capital investment planning, R227 billion was required to eliminate the existing infrastructure backlog. This total included R129 billion for capital investment (such as building new schools and classrooms) and R98 billion for maintenance, including both preventative and reactive maintenance of existing facilities. To address this backlog on an annual basis, the department estimated that R57 billion per year would be required, consisting of R32 billion for capital investment and R25 billion for maintenance. By comparison, the Education Infrastructure Grant allocation for the 2026 Medium-Term Expenditure Framework amounted to approximately R49.9 billion, which fell significantly short of the investment required. This gap indicated that current funding streams were insufficient to eliminate infrastructure deficits in the medium term without additional funding sources.

The presentation also provided a practical example through the case of Ndema Senior Secondary School in the Eastern Cape, which had 343 learners and operated with only seven classrooms. Recent interventions included the installation of two 5-kilolitre water tanks to provide potable water. The school was also scheduled to receive fencing under Phase 5 of the fencing programme, with a project estimate of approximately R2.36 million and an allocated budget of R2.30 million, with contractor appointment expected in July 2026. In addition, maintenance needs had been identified with an estimated cost of approximately R2.9 million to be addressed in the 2026/27 financial year. This case illustrated the incremental and project-based nature of infrastructure improvements at individual schools.

Conclusion and Key Recommendations

Addressing infrastructure backlogs would require stronger planning systems, improved maintenance strategies and more flexible infrastructure delivery models. The DBE highlighted the need for regular asset condition assessments, the development of a credible pipeline of infrastructure projects, and the adoption of risk-adjusted maintenance and school replacement strategies to ensure more efficient use of resources.

To address the significant funding gap, the department proposed diversifying financing mechanisms beyond traditional government grants. Suggested approaches included public–private partnerships, blended finance arrangements, alternative project delivery mechanisms such as design–build–operate models, and other innovative financing strategies. These approaches aimed to accelerate infrastructure delivery while reducing reliance on limited public funds.

The final recommendation to the Standing Committee on Appropriations was that it note and deliberate on the report regarding learner transport and progress in school infrastructure provision, particularly in relation to funding requirements and strategies to address persistent infrastructure backlogs in the basic education sector.

Presentation by the Department of Transport: Briefing by the Department of Transport on the 2025/2026 Special Appropriation Bill and 2026 Division of Revenue Bill

Mr Mathabatha Mokonyama, Acting Director-General, led the presentation to the Committee.

Financial Breakdown of Transfers for Conditional Grants

The presentation outlined the financial allocations provided through the 2025 Special Appropriation and the 2026 Division of Revenue Bill to support key transport infrastructure and services. The Minister of Finance had approved R8.3 billion in the 2025/26 financial year to rehabilitate two strategic freight rail corridors: R4.94 billion for the North Corridor Reinstatement Project and R3.40 billion for the Iron Ore Corridor. These allocations were approved as a direct charge against the National Revenue Fund and aimed to restore rail capacity, unlock private investment, and strengthen freight logistics for both export and domestic markets.

In addition, a further R6.8 billion special appropriation was allocated in the same financial year. Of this amount, R1 billion was directed to Transnet through the Development Bank of Southern Africa for upgrades at berths 203–205 at the Durban Container Terminal, while R5.78 billion was transferred to the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa (PRASA) to meet contractual commitments related to the rolling stock fleet renewal programme.

Under the 2026 Division of Revenue Bill, several conditional grants were highlighted. The Provincial Road Maintenance Grant (PRMG) was projected to amount to R54.2 billion over the medium term, including an additional R1.5 billion in 2026/27 to rebuild infrastructure damaged by natural disasters in June 2025. Of this disaster allocation, the Eastern Cape was expected to receive R707.6 million and Limpopo R803.9 million. The Rural Road Asset Management Grant (RRAMS) was allocated R408 million over the medium term to support rural municipalities in establishing road asset management systems and improving road and traffic data collection. Meanwhile, the Public Transport Operations Grant (PTOG) was projected to grow from R8.1 billion in 2025/26 to R9 billion by 2028/29, reflecting an average annual growth rate of 3.7 percent.

Public Transport Network Grant (PTNG)

There were growing concerns regarding the Public Transport Network Grant, which had been introduced more than 15 years earlier following the 2007 Public Transport Strategy to transform South Africa’s fragmented urban public transport system. The grant had experienced persistent underspending, implementation delays and weak performance, leading the National Treasury to announce in the 2025 Medium-Term Budget Policy Statement that the programme would gradually be wound down.

As part of this restructuring, the grant was reduced by R8.3 billion over the medium term, including R3 billion resulting from Treasury’s targeted savings initiative. Nevertheless, the programme continued to allocate approximately R14 billion over the medium term to support cities operating bus rapid transit systems, including Cape Town, Ekurhuleni, George, Johannesburg, Nelson Mandela Bay, Polokwane, Rustenburg and Tshwane. These funds primarily supported indirect operational costs associated with bus services.

A major concern raised in the presentation related to existing contractual obligations between metropolitan municipalities and taxi associations, particularly through Vehicle Operating Company Agreements (VOCAs) funded by the grant. City submissions indicated that the total remaining value of VOCA contracts amounted to approximately R30 billion, while the aggregate annual contract value was about R1.48 billion. As the PTNG allocation was being reduced, these long-term contractual commitments risked creating a structural funding gap unless alternative funding arrangements, contract renegotiations, or service adjustments were implemented. The department indicated that it would continue gathering data from municipalities and ensure that existing agreements were honoured to avoid service disruptions.

Scholar Transport

The presentation addressed the institutional and funding framework governing scholar transport. The function was a shared responsibility between the Department of Transport and the Department of Basic Education. The Department of Transport was responsible for developing transport policy and monitoring compliance, while the Department of Basic Education determined the demand for learner transport and monitored implementation from the education perspective. Provincial governments were responsible for implementation, including needs assessments, contracting service providers, and monitoring service delivery.

The programme’s implementation structure varied across provinces. In four provinces (North West, Mpumalanga, Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal) the function was managed by provincial departments of transport, while in five provinces (Free State, Gauteng, Limpopo, Northern Cape and Western Cape) it was managed by provincial departments of education. Oversight was provided by the National Inter-Departmental Committee, which met quarterly and conducted provincial site visits to monitor implementation.

Achieving full coverage of learner transport services would require a dedicated funding stream, possibly in the form of a conditional grant. Future funding considerations included determining the total number of learners requiring subsidies, clarifying the institutional location of the function within government, and developing grant conditions to regulate scholar transport services.

PRASA - Rolling Stock and Rail Recovery

Mr Hishaam Emeran, PRASA Group Chief Executive Officer (GCEO), presented the final section of the presentation, which focused on rail system recovery and rolling stock modernisation within PRASA. The agency had prioritised infrastructure recovery and had successfully restored 35 out of 40 rail corridors, bringing back important passenger rail services across the country. Recent progress included the recovery of the Central Line in Cape Town (Chris Hani to Cape Town) and ongoing plans to restore additional corridors such as Midway to Lenz, Germiston to Daveyton, and Johannesburg to Nasrec, subject to funding availability.

Significant work was also underway to rebuild the rail signalling and telecommunications system. Progress reports indicated that the KwaZulu-Natal Train Control System project had reached 26 percent completion, slightly ahead of the 25 percent planned progress, while the National Digital Radio Network had reached 32 percent completion. Other initiatives included signalling restoration in Gauteng, procurement processes for signalling upgrades in the Western Cape, and the implementation of the Electronic Authorisation System (EAS) to improve rail traffic management.

The presentation also reviewed the Rolling Stock Fleet Renewal Programme, which aimed to modernise South Africa’s passenger rail fleet through the Isitimela Sabantu project, with trains manufactured locally at the Gibela plant in Nigel. The programme planned to deliver 600 new Electrical Multiple Unit (EMU) train sets, of which 314 trains had already been delivered by December of the reporting year. However, the programme faced significant funding constraints. While R17.9 billion had been allocated over the 2026/27–2028/29 Medium-Term Expenditure Framework, the negotiated contract costs required R34.3 billion, resulting in a projected funding shortfall of R16.5 billion. To address these financing challenges, the government was exploring the establishment of a Rolling Stock Operating Company (ROSCO) to attract private sector investment into rail rolling stock procurement.

Discussion

Mr T Gamede (MKP) asked the DBE, considering that KwaZulu-Natal was currently reaching only 39% of its identified learner transport need, transporting 76,598 out of 196,461 learners, what immediate provincial-level interventions were planned to prevent this gap from further eroding the constitutional right to basic education? Furthermore, given that the migration of the learner transport function between the DoT and DBE had historically blurred accountability, he asked what the specific timeline from National Treasury was to finalise a dedicated conditional grant to address funding and oversight inefficiencies.

Mr K Wakelin (DA) asked the DBE for clarity on the funding modalities. With an infrastructure backlog of approximately R270 billion, he asked how many provincial departments were exploring or developing alternative funding models, such as public–private partnerships, blended finance, and donor funding, and how many had already implemented such models, noting that it was unlikely that government could address this backlog alone. On learner transport, he stated that there was a need to clearly determine accountability, adding that as the Appropriations Committee, it had to establish which department held responsibility and ensure that a dedicated conditional grant was aligned accordingly. Lastly, regarding the infrastructure grant, he noted that there was significant underspending in the 2025/26 financial year. Provinces were requesting additional funding, yet available funds were not fully utilised; for example, KwaZulu-Natal had spent 83% and North West 76%. He asked what steps the national department would take to address this.

For the DoT, he raised concerns regarding the Public Transport Network Grant (PTNG), noting that concerns had been raised with the Deputy Minister of Finance regarding potential irregularities and risks associated with metropolitan contracts. He cited Tshwane as an example, where Phase 2 had been signed and was being implemented, despite earlier indications that only Phase 1 was approved. He stated that this suggested greater financial exposure than presented, raising concerns about contingent liabilities and insufficient oversight of municipalities. He further noted concerns regarding long-term ticketing contracts, such as the 12-year contract awarded in Tshwane, which were not fully reflected in the presentation. Secondly, he requested a written response from the Division Secretary to provide clarity on the TRIMS division within the Department of Transport and Transnet, particularly regarding concession implementation, accountability mechanisms, and reporting timelines, noting that this was necessary to inform the Committee’s oversight role. Lastly, regarding PRASA, he requested clarity on the R7.5 billion train refurbishment programme currently under investigation, as well as the status of the AFRO 400 locomotives. He noted that passenger numbers had declined to approximately 45 million trips per month and asked what the current status was. He further raised concerns that further investment in rolling stock might amount to bailing out previous commitments without sufficient assurance of improved outcomes, and suggested that the possibility of concessions, particularly in regions such as the Western Cape, should also be considered.

Mr A Bateman (DA) asked the DBE how frequently scholar transport vehicles were inspected and how many had been removed from service due to safety violations. He further asked whether the department had data on the proportion of learner absenteeism or dropout directly linked to lack of transport. He raised concerns about the accuracy of information presented, particularly regarding Ndema High School, noting that while the presentation reflected improved conditions, oversight visits indicated that infrastructure conditions remained poor, including sanitation facilities. He stated that this suggested a gap between national reporting and on-the-ground realities. For the Department of Transport, he asked, given the findings of the Zondo Commission and ongoing governance challenges, what safeguards were in place to prevent corruption and ensure value for money. He further stated that the allocation of R8.3 billion for corridor rehabilitation required clear performance milestones and asked how progress would be reported to Parliament and the people of South Africa. Additionally, he asked what the operating model of PRASA was and whether, given the significant capital investment, PRASA would be able to achieve financial sustainability, or at least break even, suggesting that a dedicated session with PRASA might be necessary to unpack its operational plan.

Ms C Mkhonto (EFF) addressed the DBE and stated that there were ongoing severe infrastructure challenges, with schools lacking basic services such as toilets, fencing, and adequate classrooms, citing examples including Sidlamafa Secondary in Mpumalanga and Thembelihle Primary School in Northern KwaZulu-Natal. On learner transport, she noted that the primary challenge in provinces appeared to be the non-payment of service providers, which disrupted daily schooling and negatively affected learner performance, particularly for matriculants. While supporting the proposed R98 billion infrastructure allocation, she raised concerns about potential misuse of funds. She stated that provinces had to be assessed on whether they had the capacity to effectively manage learner transport programmes. For the Department of Transport, she asked when PRASA would expand services to secondary cities such as Mbombela and Polokwane, noting that its focus appeared to be on metropolitan areas.

Ms D Ntuli (ANC), on the DBE, requested the Minister to visit Mavana Primary School in KwaZulu-Natal, stating that there was no fence, no toilets, and no water at the school. She indicated that the school had written multiple letters to the provincial department with no response, and that this had affected enrolment as parents were removing their children to better capacitated schools nearby. For the Department of Transport, she asked, given the continued investment in transport infrastructure, what measurable economic outcomes could be expected over the next three years, particularly in terms of job creation, reduced transport costs, and improved mobility. She also raised concerns about funding shortfalls in PRASA’s rolling stock programme, the risk of trains being delivered but not deployed, and delays in restoring corridors. She asked how many trains were currently operational versus idle and how continued investment was justified under these constraints.

Ms N Gcaleka-Mazibuko (ANC) asked the DBE whether learner transport, which was funded through the equitable share where it competed with other priorities, should not be funded through a dedicated conditional grant, and if so, what policy, legislative, and administrative steps had been taken. She further asked how many qualifying learners were currently not receiving transport and which provinces were most affected. She also raised concerns about the slow progress of the SAFE programme and delays in finalising the Memorandum of Understanding between the DBE and the Department of Transport. For the DoT, she asked what steps were being taken to restore commuter rail services and what the key implementation bottlenecks were.

Mr S Mwali (MKP) addressed the DBE and stated that the lack of policy coordination and uniform implementation across provinces remained a concern, noting that while regulatory reforms were underway, clear timelines were absent. He stated that the failure to provide learner transport raised the question of whether this constituted a violation of constitutional rights. He also raised concerns about the conclusion of programmes such as SAFE and ASIDI, despite ongoing backlogs in sanitation and infrastructure. He noted that maintenance backlogs were increasing and that suppliers remained unpaid, with some withdrawing services and others threatening to strike. For the DoT, he asked what measures were in place to ensure the security of rail infrastructure, particularly signalling systems. He raised concerns about contractor non-payment, rising costs, and reports of criminal interference in infrastructure projects.

Ms O Seate (ANC) stated that the DBE continued to report significant infrastructure backlogs and overcrowded classrooms despite substantial annual allocations and asked what explained the persistent gap between budget allocation and tangible improvements in school infrastructure. Referring to a recent presentation to the Portfolio Committee on Basic Education on 3 March 2026, she noted that there were approximately 9,000 infrastructure projects in the pipeline. Given the scale of this programme, she stated that implementing agents played a critical role in delivery, but there were ongoing concerns regarding project delays, cost overruns, and incomplete infrastructure projects across provinces. She asked which implementing agents were consistently underperforming and what consequence management measures were in place for failure to deliver, stating that the Committee required clarity on what happened to agents who repeatedly failed to meet time, cost, and quality expectations. She further asked how frequently the Department conducted performance reviews of both implementing agents and provincial education departments, why the department continued to rely on agents with a track record of poor performance, what percentage of infrastructure projects had experienced delays or cost escalations due to inefficiencies by implementing agents, and whether the Department had considered building internal infrastructure delivery capacity rather than relying so heavily on external agents. Finally, she asked how the Department planned to manage delivery risks associated with such a large and complex pipeline of infrastructure projects.

For the DoT, she asked what measures were being implemented to ensure that increased government expenditure translated into improved service delivery, rather than continued delays and infrastructure backlogs. She further asked whether the Department had sufficient technical and project management capacity to implement large-scale projects effectively, and what steps were being taken to strengthen planning, coordination, and execution across transport entities. She noted that several metropolitan municipalities had entered into long-term contracts with taxi associations under the Public Transport Network Grant and asked what financial and legal risks had been identified, particularly regarding contract compliance, subsidy obligations, and potential disputes between municipalities and operators. She further asked what oversight mechanisms were in place to ensure municipalities did not enter into agreements that created unfunded obligations for the state. She requested clarity on the scope, implementation plan, and expected impact of the Road Accident Fund's learner transport driver programme, and how it would improve learner safety.

The Chairperson raised the need to consider a shift towards universal provision of learner transport, rather than a targeted approach, noting that some schools already integrated transport into their models. He also questioned the effectiveness of the current funding framework, particularly the reliance on provincial discretion, and whether there was a need to reconsider the federalisation of funding, especially in light of learner migration across provinces and its impact on provincial budgets. On transport, he emphasised the need for a more coherent long-term strategy, including how subsidies could better support integrated and accessible mobility systems.

Responses

Department of Basic Education

Minister Gwarube stated that KwaZulu-Natal had been raised repeatedly and explained that the provincial department was under Section 18 of the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA) and was being managed by the Provincial Treasury due to severe financial constraints. She noted that the province was in deep debt, to the extent that when funds were allocated, they were first used to service debt before other services could be funded.

She indicated that the Deputy Minister, the Director-General, and she were planning a visit to KwaZulu-Natal to assess how support could be provided. However, she emphasised that there was no legal or financial instrument through which national departments could bail out provincial departments. The challenges in KwaZulu-Natal had to be resolved by the Premier and the Member of the Executive Council (MEC). As the national Department, they could only assist within the limits of the law and their mandate.

On the question of making learner transport a conditional grant, she explained that this was one of the options under consideration as part of the joint policy review between the departments of Basic Education and Transport. A conditional grant would allow for tighter controls and accountability mechanisms. She noted that with grants such as the National School Nutrition Programme, funds could be withdrawn if they were not used for their intended purpose, and that at a minimum, provinces were aware that Treasury could enforce compliance.

She cautioned that this reform was complex due to the different institutional arrangements across provinces. For instance, in the Eastern Cape, learner transport was administered by the Department of Transport rather than Basic Education. These arrangements were deeply embedded at the provincial level, and the national department had limited authority to reconfigure them, as this fell within the powers of the Premiers.

On the issue of policy coordination, she stated that the varying provincial arrangements continued to limit the introduction of a uniform national model. While national departments could align policy and set standards, implementation remained subject to provincial autonomy, which constrained standardisation efforts.

With regard to SAFE and ASIDI, she explained that these had been targeted, time-bound interventions designed to accelerate the eradication of inappropriate school infrastructure and were never intended to replace the infrastructure grants allocated to provinces. Treasury had indicated that sufficient time had been provided for these programmes, and that provinces had to utilise their own infrastructure allocations to address remaining backlogs. The role of the DBE would therefore shift more strongly towards oversight and support rather than direct implementation.

On the use of implementing agents, she stated that the sector faced an infrastructure backlog of approximately R140 billion, while simultaneously needing to respond to growing demand for classrooms and maintenance, particularly in provinces experiencing population inflows such as Gauteng and the Western Cape. Provinces were therefore managing both historical backlogs and current pressures. Given these constraints, implementing agents remained necessary, as the department did not have the internal capacity to undertake large-scale construction. She added that challenges arose where agents appointed contractors who failed to complete projects or abandoned them due to cash flow issues, and that there was a strong view that Treasury should blacklist non-performing contractors.

On the infrastructure backlog, Ms Gwarube clarified that the department was not suggesting that pit latrines or mud schools had been fully eradicated. Rather, responsibility for addressing these issues now lies with provinces through their infrastructure allocations, while the national department focuses on oversight.

On the question of universal learner transport, she stated that this would be desirable under a different fiscal context. A model similar to the yellow school bus system in the United States could provide uniformity; however, current fiscal constraints did not allow for this. As part of the ongoing policy review, the department was exploring ways to improve existing systems, including ensuring that learner transport vehicles were clearly identifiable and strengthening reporting mechanisms for unsafe driving. She added that there was also a need to regulate private transport providers used by parents, including enforcing registration requirements.

The Minister further noted ongoing frustrations regarding the ability of the DBE to hold underperforming provinces accountable, given the limits of its authority. She cited KwaZulu-Natal as an example, where only 39% of qualifying learners received transport. To address this, the department was exploring ways to provide technical support to provinces, particularly in budgeting, planning, and implementation, where many of the challenges arose.

Dr Reginah Mhaule, Deputy Minister of Basic Education, encouraged Members who had raised concerns about specific schools in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga to formally write to the Minister detailing these issues, rather than waiting for Committee engagements.

On the matter of universal learner transport, she stated that this was an important issue that required further consideration, adding that, as indicated by the Minister, the process of exploring this option had to begin. She noted that there were precedents, such as in Mpumalanga, where learner transport had been standardised in terms of vehicle colour and branding, which could inform future policy direction.

Department of Transport

Mr Mokonyama stated that the issue of standardisation and uniformity was critical; however, practical considerations had first to be addressed. He explained that if learner transport were to become a conditional grant, clarity would be required on which department transferred the funds and to whom, particularly where functions did not align institutionally. He noted that there were important distinctions between concurrent and shared functions, which complicated funding arrangements, and that contracting responsibilities for learner transport remained at the provincial level, which also had to be taken into account.

In terms of regulatory interventions, he stated that the Department was working to expand Regulation 42(2) to include privately arranged learner transport, as many vehicles currently operating were non-compliant with safety standards. He added that learner transport vehicles, like other public transport, should have distinct markings, and that the department was also considering strengthening driver requirements, including introducing mandatory advanced driving skills training under the PrDP framework over the long term.

On the PTNG, he explained that the issue related to the winding down of the programme while existing contracts remained in place. The department had engaged municipalities to assess current commitments and determine how these would be managed going forward. He confirmed that existing contracts, including ticketing agreements, would be honoured, even as the programme was gradually phased out.

On TRIMS, he stated that this referred to a Transnet rail infrastructure management function established to manage requests for infrastructure access, separating infrastructure management from operations. He explained that while infrastructure remained state-owned, operations could involve multiple operators, and that an independent infrastructure manager was therefore necessary to allocate access and prevent monopoly control.

Regarding devolution, he indicated that the Department was developing a strategy, particularly in the rail sector, which included conducting due diligence, assessing municipal capacity, and determining what functions could be devolved and at what cost. He added that progress on devolution was closely linked to resolving financial challenges within PRASA.

He further noted that transport contributed approximately 8–9% to the economy, and that the department aimed to increase this contribution. The sector was broadly aligned with the National Development Plan, with a target of reaching approximately R400 billion contribution by 2028, from a current level of around R308 billion.

The PRASA Group CEO welcomed the request for a dedicated session on PRASA’s operating model, stating that there was a need to provide a more detailed account of the turnaround strategy, particularly progress made over the past three years.

In terms of governance, he stated that there had been notable improvements, including PRASA now receiving unqualified audit outcomes. Passenger numbers had also increased significantly, from approximately 11 million three years previously to 75 million in the previous year, with projections of exceeding 100 million in the current year. However, he noted that this remained below optimal levels.

On financial sustainability, he indicated that PRASA would continue to require government subsidy, but that a sustainable operational level was estimated at 350–400 million passenger trips per annum, with a target of 600 million by 2031 set by the Minister.

Regarding investigations, he stated that PRASA was following due legal processes, including criminal cases and disciplinary actions, and could provide further documentation where required.

On expansion to cities such as Mbombela and Polokwane, he explained that constraints included infrastructure availability and locomotive capacity, although work was ongoing in regional areas.

He further noted that the AFRO 400 locomotive contract had been declared unlawful and was currently under liquidation proceedings. PRASA, as a major creditor, was seeking to maximise value recovery, and previous auction attempts had yielded limited returns, with only a small number of locomotives sold.

On safety and security, he stated that this remained central to the recovery programme, noting that approximately 85% of rail corridors had been recovered. However, challenges such as infrastructure vandalism and organised criminal activity persisted.

On fare increases, he explained that the adjustment implemented in September of the previous year had been the first in 10 years, and that future increases were expected to align with CPI, while taking into account affordability concerns.

Closing remarks

The Chairperson noted the need to consolidate outstanding questions and formally submit them to the DoT for detailed responses, particularly regarding the liquidation processes. There was concern that, without proper oversight, similar issues could arise in future procurement processes, resulting in the state effectively paying multiple times for the same assets.

The Chairperson thanked both departments for their presentations and emphasised that further engagements, particularly with the Department of Transport, would be scheduled to address outstanding issues in greater depth.

The Committee proceeded to adopt the following:

  • Draft minutes of the Standing Committee on Appropriations meeting held on18 December 2025 (Consideration of the 2025 MTBPS Report).
  • Draft minutes of the meeting held on24 February 2026 (Pre-Budget briefing by the PBO and adoption of the Committee Programme for Term 1, 2026).

The meeting was adjourned.
