Dredging in Mfolozi and Msunduzi flats; Update on FMD outbreak and interventions to control the spread of the disease; with Minister

Agriculture

10 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committees on Agriculture and on Forestry, Fisheries and Environment received briefings on the dredging of the Mfolozi and Msunduzi flats and the opening of the St Lucia estuary mouth. The iSimangaliso Wetland Park presented the history of its environmental authorisation (EA) process, highlighting initial refusals, appeals, stakeholder consultations, and eventual approval in February 2026. Committees emphasised the importance of collaboration, environmental compliance, and protection of livelihoods for communities dependent on tourism, fishing, and farming. Issues raised included lost assessment data due to a defaulting service provider and the need to ensure minimal environmental impact during dredging. A follow-up meeting will monitor timelines, High Level Panel recommendations, and environmental outcomes.

Key points coming to the fore during the Committee discussion were:

  • Initial EA applications were refused in 2023/24; appeal and consultations led to EA approval in February 2026.
  • Success depends on continued coordination with communities, research bodies, government departments, and industry, balancing conservation with livelihoods.
  • Lost assessment data highlighted contract management issues; alternate land for affected farmers and careful monitoring of estuary conditions were emphasised.
  • Committees will hold a joint follow-up to review timelines, High Level Panel recommendations, and ongoing impact monitoring in line with the EA.

The Portfolio Committee on Agriculture received an update on the Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) outbreak from the Department of Agriculture, which highlighted recent outbreaks across multiple provinces and the vaccination of approximately 291 000 animals, with revaccination ongoing and FMD epicentres identified in KwaZulu-Natal. Challenges included insufficient animal health technicians, uncontrolled animal movement, auction-related spread, and the need to support both communal and commercial farmers.

The Committee noted concerns over the reallocation of R16 million intended for small-scale farmers to the FMD programme, and requested written confirmation from the Director-General and Minister that these funds would be protected. Members also sought assurance that the vaccines sourced from Argentina were compliant with the South African FMD strain. The Department was also tasked with providing updates on collaboration with the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), laboratory backlogs, vaccine testing, and overall vaccination progress. The Chairperson emphasised the need for continued monitoring, inter-institutional collaboration, and maintaining South Africa’s FMD-free status.

Department of Agriculture

Ms Lydia Bosoga from the Department of Agriculture introduced the presentation and indicated that colleagues from the Department of Rural Development and Land Reform were also involved, noting that the two entities had previously operated as a single department when the work began.

She explained that the Department first received an inquiry in 2021 concerning challenges faced by the farming community in the area surrounding the iSimangaliso Wetland and the St Lucia estuary. Investigations were conducted to assess the impact from a natural agricultural resources perspective. These investigations found that the area had experienced rising water levels and back-flooding over several years, largely due to flooding in the uMfolozi catchment within the St Lucia estuary system. These conditions were affecting agricultural land and farming activities in the surrounding areas.

Ms Bosoga further explained that the Department’s involvement was guided by its legislative mandate under the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act (CARA). The Act provides for the conservation of natural agricultural resources to maintain the productive potential of land, prevent soil erosion, protect water resources, and regulate the control of weeds and invasive plants. During the Department’s assessment, it was found that certain provisions of the Act were being contravened or adversely affected in the area under discussion.

Specifically, the affected provisions included Regulations 6, 7, and 15, which relate to waterlogging, cultivation practices within watercourses and floodplains, and the management of alien invasive plants. These regulatory breaches were linked to the environmental and farming challenges observed in the area.

During site observations and deliberations, it was noted that some agricultural activities were taking place within the floodplain, while certain riverbanks had been damaged. In addition, the presence of weeds and invasive plants had become a concern. Photographic evidence presented during the briefing illustrated blocked water channels and instances where water runoff had been obstructed or redirected through cultivated land, which further complicated water management in the area.

Some land users proposed that the estuary mouth be opened to prevent back-flooding and waterlogging on cultivated land. They argued that the closure of the estuary mouth had resulted in agricultural land becoming unusable. The Department, however, emphasised the need to manage the area carefully to recover agricultural land while ensuring compliance with environmental and agricultural legislation. Awareness campaigns were therefore undertaken to sensitise farmers about the relevant legislative requirements and sustainable land-use practices.

The Department highlighted that regulations require land users to protect irrigable land from waterlogging, avoid cultivation within floodplain areas, maintain channels that allow water to flow freely, and prevent the obstruction of watercourses. In some instances, interventions such as allowing water to flow freely to the sea could help manage back-flooding.

As part of its recommendations, the Department proposed intensifying awareness and advisory programmes for farmers, particularly because the situation directly affects their livelihoods. Through initiatives such as the LandCare Programme, the Department aimed to provide support, capacity building, and guidance on sustainable farming practices in environmentally sensitive areas. It also emphasised the importance of ongoing engagement to build trust between land users and other stakeholders around the iSimangaliso Wetland Park.

In conclusion, Ms Bosoga noted that the work had been undertaken collaboratively with the Department of Rural Development and Land Reform, which had played a leading role in conducting technical studies, including hydrological assessments. These studies were intended to guide farmers on appropriate conservation measures and sustainable land-use practices in the area. However, she added that more recently some farmers had become reluctant to allow stakeholders access to the area, as they strongly preferred that the estuary mouth be opened.

She then invited Ms Nontuthuzelo Ntshabele from the Department of Rural Development and Land Reform to continue the presentation, particularly on the Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) processes and technical studies that had been coordinated when the departments were still operating as a single entity.

Department of Rural Development and Land Reform

Ms Nontuthuzelo Ntshabele, Chief Director at the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development, thanked the Chairperson and greeted Members of the Committees and Minister Willie Aucamp. She introduced key stakeholders present in the meeting, including representatives from the iSimangaliso Wetland Park Authority, among them the CEO, Mr Sibusiso Bukhosini, Mr Ben from iSimangaliso, and Dr Cedras, a scientist appointed to support the implementation of interventions recommended by a panel of experts.

Ms Ntshabele explained that her presentation focused on the Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) process, particularly the Basic Assessment Report (BAR) update, and how the process had unfolded. The presentation aimed to outline the background to the application, the consultation process with stakeholders, the reasons for the initial refusal of environmental authorisation under Section 30 of the National Environmental Management Act (NEMA), and the specialist studies undertaken to support the application. It also highlighted the role of iSimangaliso Wetland Park in monitoring the estuary and maintaining the ecological integrity of the wetland system.

She noted that the environmental authorisation process began in April 2023 following recommendations made by a panel of experts after a symposium on the matter. The process was conducted collaboratively with iSimangaliso Wetland Park, several government departments, and a range of stakeholders. Initially, the team submitted an application under Section 30A of NEMA for emergency dredging of the uMsunduzi channel to reduce back-flooding. However, the competent authority could not approve the emergency authorisation due to insufficient baseline scientific information to support the decision.

As a result, a Basic Assessment process was initiated in June 2023. This involved fieldwork and a series of specialist studies focusing on hydrology, sedimentation, and ecology. A draft Basic Assessment Report was submitted in February 2024. Following public consultation and the receipt of stakeholder comments, the report was revised and resubmitted in July 2024. The application was initially refused, prompting an appeal led by the iSimangaliso Wetland Park Authority. In February 2025, the Minister set aside the refusal and directed stakeholders to work together to find a balanced solution that addressed both environmental protection and local concerns.

Following the appeal outcome, the project team incorporated additional inputs from stakeholders and competent authorities. Four additional specialist studies were commissioned and completed. These studies were incorporated into an updated Basic Assessment Report submitted on 10 October 2025 for public comment. After further stakeholder inputs, an amended report was submitted on 6 January 2026.

Ms Ntshabele emphasised that the issue required extensive collaboration due to the ecological significance of the area. The iSimangaliso Wetland Park is both a World Heritage Site and a Ramsar-listed wetland, requiring careful environmental management. An intergovernmental task team was established, bringing together the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE), the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development, the Department of Agriculture, the Department of Water and Sanitation, Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife, the uMkhanyakude District Municipality, the Mthubatuba Local Municipality, and the iSimangaliso Wetland Park Authority. Stakeholder consultations also included local communities, small-scale farmers, the Sokhulu farming community, the Umfolozi Sugar Planters, and representatives from the tourism and hospitality sector. Meetings were often conducted in both English and IsiZulu to ensure inclusive participation.

Ms Ntshabele explained that the initial emergency authorisation request had been refused because the necessary baseline scientific information was not sufficient for the competent authority to determine the environmental risks of the proposed dredging activities. Consequently, stakeholders agreed on a three-phase intervention approach as part of the Basic Assessment process:

  1. Phase One: Removal and control of vegetation from the uMsunduzi River channel and potentially parts of the lower uMfolozi River.
  2. Phase Two: Dredging of accumulated sediment from the uMsunduzi channel and parts of the uMfolozi system, including beach channel areas, alongside vegetation management.
  3. Phase Three: Ongoing maintenance, including periodic removal of vegetation and debris in the river channels.

A series of specialist studies informed the Basic Assessment Report. These included hydrological and geomorphological studies, which found that excessive reed growth and sediment accumulation—particularly around the “Honeymoon Bend”—had constricted the estuary channel, reduced tidal exchange, and contributed to significant back-flooding in the lower uMsunduzi and uMfolozi rivers. Remote sensing and drone technology were used to map vegetation growth, sediment accumulation, and topographical features in the area.

Additional studies examined vegetation, marine ecology, wetland and watercourse functionality, agricultural impacts, heritage resources, and palaeontological considerations. The vegetation assessment found no rare or endangered plant species within the affected areas, suggesting that dredging and vegetation removal would have minimal ecological impact if properly managed. Marine and estuarine studies, however, highlighted that prolonged closure of the estuary mouth and sediment build-up had disrupted ecological processes and reduced the presence of estuary-dependent species.

Further studies conducted after the appeal process included terrestrial fauna assessments, hydropedological studies examining the interaction between soil and hydrological processes, wetland offset assessments to address ecological losses, and hydrogeological assessments of groundwater conditions and aquifers. These studies generally concluded that the proposed dredging and vegetation management activities would have low environmental impact if mitigation measures were implemented.

Ms Ntshabele reported that environmental authorisation for the proposed activities was granted on 24 February 2026 following the submission of the amended Basic Assessment Report in January 2026. However, the project cannot proceed until the appeal period has lapsed, as stakeholders still have the opportunity to raise objections.

The environmental authorisation includes several conditions. These include strict adherence to the Environmental Management Programme (EMPr), the appointment of an Environmental Control Officer, limitations on dredging to the approved project footprint, management of sediment disposal and waste, and ongoing monitoring of environmental impacts. Vegetation removal and dredging must also comply with specified thresholds and be implemented only where scientifically justified.

Ms Ntshabele noted that iSimangaliso Wetland Park continues to play a key role in monitoring the estuary system. Monitoring programmes cover Lake St Lucia, Lake Kosi, Lake Sibaya, and surrounding ecosystems. The monitoring programme uses modern equipment such as automated data loggers, turbidity meters, and refractometers to track water quality and ecosystem health. Drone technology and remote sensing are also used to monitor vegetation growth and environmental conditions in real time.

In addition to the environmental assessment process, the Department also undertook broader studies, including sustainable land-use assessments, land ownership analysis, and socioeconomic profiling within the Mtubatuba Local Municipality. These studies identified concerns such as farming activities taking place in inappropriate or environmentally sensitive areas, which may require further policy and land management interventions.

In conclusion, Ms Ntshabele emphasised that the successful implementation of the environmental authorisation would depend on continued collaboration among all stakeholders. Ongoing stakeholder engagement ensures transparency and shared accountability. All activities will be implemented in accordance with EIA regulations, environmental authorisation conditions, and the Environmental Management Programme to ensure sustainable development while protecting the ecological integrity of the iSimangaliso Wetland system.

Isimangaliso Wetland Authority

Mr Sibusiso Bukhosini, CEO of Isimangaliso, indicated that his presentation would be similar to those already delivered but would also provide some historical background. The intention was to help Honourable Members understand where the organisation was coming from and why certain actions had been taken up to this point.

Before proceeding, he thanked the different departments and stakeholders involved, noting that the work done so far demonstrated collaboration in action. These included the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development, the Department of Agriculture, the Department of Water and Sanitation, Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife, and the Isimangaliso Wetland Park Authority. He also acknowledged the role of local government, including the Umkhanyakude District Municipality and the local municipality of Mtubatuba, as well as the traditional authorities, particularly the Sokhulu and Mkhwanazi traditional authorities who were directly affected. He further thanked the St Lucia Ratepayers Association, the scientific community, farmers, youth organisations and others who had contributed. He added that the support received from political leadership had also been important.

Mr Bukhosini then explained that the St Lucia estuary system begins around Maphelane within the Sokhulu Traditional Authority area, which is one of the areas most affected by the issues under discussion. The estuary stretches as far as Charters Creek and beyond. He noted that the St Lucia estuary is the largest estuarine lake system in South Africa, covering about 35 000 hectares. It functions as an estuary only when there is a balanced relationship between saltwater and freshwater. He emphasised that the system forms part of a Ramsar wetland site, which supports diverse plant and animal species and contributes significantly to tourism, fishing and ecological sustainability.

Providing historical context, he explained that the estuary originates from a catchment area that forms a swampy and flat floodplain. Farming takes place in this floodplain area, which partly explains why there is frequent inundation. He explained that floodplains play an important ecological role because they trap sediment, debris and organic material carried by rivers from upstream catchments such as the Mfolozi, uMsunduzi, Pongola and Suthu rivers. In natural systems, the floodplain traps this material while cleaner water flows into the estuary.

He added that in natural estuaries such as Kosi Bay and Mphunzeleni there is little sediment accumulation because the floodplains are functioning properly. However, farming activities on the floodplain at St Lucia have altered this natural process. Human intervention in the system began as early as the 1950s when rivers, particularly the Mfolozi River, were modified. These interventions interfered with the natural functioning of the estuary and contributed to the problems that are experienced today.

He said that because of flooding affecting farms, the former Natal Parks Board, now Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife, undertook dredging operations to try to address the problem. However, the dredging also created unintended consequences. The dredged material was deposited nearby and eventually formed elevated areas that later developed into vegetated and forested areas. This altered the natural flow of freshwater into the estuary, allowing mainly seawater to enter the system.

As a result, salinity levels increased significantly. Mangroves, which require controlled salinity levels, began to die off, and many fish species that depended on freshwater conditions declined. In this way, earlier human interventions disrupted what had once been a fully functional natural system.

He then referred to the Global Environment Facility (GEF5) restoration project undertaken between 2016 and 2018, which attempted to correct some of these earlier mistakes by reconnecting the Mfolozi and St Lucia systems. The plan was to remove more than five million cubic metres of sediment to restore natural flow. However, due to budget constraints only about one million cubic metres were removed.

He explained that the removed sediment was again incorrectly deposited along the coastal dunes, which raised the beach berm to more than three metres. For the estuary mouth to breach naturally, the berm generally needs to be around 1.5 metres. Because the berm had become too high, the ocean could not breach into the estuary even during storms or high water levels.

This created another imbalance. Instead of excessive salinity, the system became dominated by freshwater. As a result, reeds and buffalo grass spread widely and many estuarine fish species disappeared. Only freshwater species such as tilapia and barbel remained in the system.

He further explained that sediment from canalised rivers was no longer trapped in the floodplain but was carried directly into the estuary. This caused sediment build-up and encouraged further reed growth. He also described how the interaction between the Mfolozi and uMsunduzi rivers contributes to flooding. The Mfolozi River flows faster and can overpower the uMsunduzi at their confluence, causing backflow that pushes water back onto the floodplain and floods nearby farms.

He said the overall intervention plan therefore aims to ensure that water can drain from the farms while allowing seawater to enter the estuary to restore salinity levels and flush out sediment.

Mr Bukhosini then explained that when he assumed the role of CEO there was significant conflict between Isimangaliso, farmers and local communities. Farmers had taken Isimangaliso to court, arguing that the park authority was preventing them from continuing farming activities. The case went to the High Court in KwaZulu-Natal, where the farmers lost both the original case and their appeal. The court ruled that the farmers should pay Isimangaliso’s legal costs.

However, recognising that the farmers were already facing financial losses due to flooding and crop damage, he decided not to pursue the legal costs. Instead, he facilitated dialogue between farmers, communities and scientists in order to find a common solution.

In 2020 a symposium was convened bringing together scientists, farmers, community members and government departments. Through this process it became clear that the estuary would not breach naturally unless the system was slightly assisted. A decision was therefore taken to trim the beach berm to a level that would allow the system to breach naturally.

He reported that within days of this intervention the estuary mouth breached and reconnected with the ocean. Since then the mouth has remained open and the system has begun functioning again as an estuary. Although the system has not yet fully recovered, progress is being observed.

He also noted that the then Minister appointed a panel of experts after some scientists raised concerns about the intervention. The panel reviewed the actions taken and emphasised the importance of sound scientific data to guide future management decisions.

He explained that a full environmental impact assessment process was subsequently undertaken. This included scoping, specialist studies such as geomorphology and vegetation studies, water use licensing processes, and extensive consultation with interested and affected parties including farmers and communities. The final submission was made in 2026 and environmental authorisation has now been granted.

He added that the project is currently in a cooling-off period during which appeals could be lodged. To date no appeals have been received, but the authority would respect the legal process before proceeding with implementation.

Mr Bukhosini concluded by explaining that Isimangaliso had also established an internal scientific team responsible for monitoring the estuary and other systems within the park, including Lake Sibaya and the Kosi Bay lake system. Monitoring includes regular field observations, drone surveys and scientific measurements of salinity and ecological conditions.

Early signs of ecological recovery are already visible. Some marine fish species have returned to the system and reeds are beginning to decline in certain areas. Mangrove recovery is expected to take longer as salinity levels gradually stabilise.

He emphasised that the interventions were not intended solely to benefit agriculture or tourism, but primarily to restore the ecological functioning of the estuary. Once ecological balance is restored, both agriculture and tourism would benefit naturally. However, he also noted that farming on a natural floodplain will always carry risks of flooding, and in the long term it may be necessary to consider alternative land for farming.

Discussion

Ms L Arries (EFF) thanked the Chairperson and the presenters for their presentations. She indicated that she had several questions relating to the joint portfolio presentation and the presentations on St Lucia and Isimangaliso.

Firstly, she asked how many hectares of agricultural land had been lost or remained unusable as a result of flooding in the uMfolozi catchment. She also wanted to know what support mechanisms existed for farmers who had not recovered their crops since the flooding events.

She further asked how frequently the relevant departments coordinate their interventions in the area, and what specific decisions or measures had been implemented through this coordination.

Referring to the St Lucia presentation, she noted that it was mentioned that the consulting firm responsible for monitoring the system had disappeared in 2018 without handing over the required data. She asked which company had been contracted, what the value of that contract was, and what actions had been taken to recover the missing data or to hold the contractor accountable.

She also referred to the slides showing farmland flooding in the uMfolozi Flats. She asked how many hectares of agricultural land had been affected, how many farmers had been impacted, and what support had been provided to local communities and farmers affected by flooding linked to the altered hydrology of the estuary.

In addition, she asked how government was balancing environmental conservation with the livelihoods of communities that depend on agriculture and fishing.

Referring to the Isimangaliso presentation, Ms Arries asked what the estimated economic impact had been on farmers and surrounding communities during the period when interventions were delayed and when the emergency dredging application submitted under section 3A of the National Environmental Management Act was refused due to insufficient information.

She further asked why the application had been submitted without the required scientific baseline data, and what lessons the Department had learned to ensure that future emergency environmental applications would be supported by sufficient scientific evidence.

Mr Z Mthethwa (MKP) thanked the Chairperson and greeted fellow Portfolio Committee Members, officials from the various departments, and the Ministers present.

He said his questions related mainly to affected and interested parties. He explained that when dealing with the uMfolozi River, it was important to recognise that the river forms part of the natural heritage of the Mthethwa people. He noted that in the traditional praises of the Mthethwa people, the strength and force of the uMfolozi River are often referenced, which demonstrates the deep historical and cultural connection between the river and the Mthethwa tribe.

He therefore asked when the appeal period for the project would close, noting that the Mthethwa people considered themselves an affected party. He explained that historically, at the confluence of the White uMfolozi and Black uMfolozi rivers, the Mthethwa royal palace had once been located before the community later moved further inland to areas now known as Richards Bay and eMpangeni.

Mr Mthethwa said that when he reviewed the list of stakeholders consulted in the process, he noted that the Sokhulu and Mkhwanazi traditional authorities were included, but the Mthethwa tribe had not been consulted. He emphasised that this was important because the uMfolozi River and surrounding areas, including the uMfolozi Game Reserve, formed part of the heritage assets of the Mthethwa people.

He stated that the Mthethwa community was interested in participating in the process and providing input, particularly on the heritage aspects of the project. He warned that there could be graves of former Mthethwa kings in the surrounding areas and that any drainage, excavation, or disturbance of water bodies might interfere with these heritage sites if they had not been properly considered.

He added that it was not only the Mthethwa people who might be affected. The Tembe tribe could also be affected. He explained that the Tembe community historically had traditional fish ponds, particularly in the Kosi Bay area, but many members of that community had been forcibly removed to areas further inland in northern KwaZulu-Natal, including KwaPhongolo. He argued that they should also have been recognised as affected stakeholders in the consultation process.

Mr Mthethwa further raised the issue of the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act. He pointed out that the sand being excavated from the area during dredging could effectively be regarded as a form of sand mining. He suggested that such sand could potentially be used in local economic development initiatives rather than simply being relocated or dumped.

Referring to the earlier presentation slides, he noted that sand previously moved from one area to another had formed an artificial mound that now obstructed the natural flow of water between the sea and the river. He suggested that proper engagement with the Department of Mineral Resources could allow for better utilisation of this material, while also ensuring that any other minerals encountered during excavation were properly managed.

He added that the project should also be considered in the context of the ocean economy, as there might be opportunities related to minerals or energy resources linked to the work being undertaken.

Mr Mthethwa also raised the issue of local economic development opportunities. He asked what economic prospects or spin-offs could arise from the project. He argued that solving the environmental problem should not exclude opportunities for communities to benefit economically. Procurement processes linked to the project should involve local communities, and where services or contracts are required, communities should be included.

He further emphasised the importance of community preparedness, suggesting that local communities should be informed about the skills required for the work and be given opportunities to participate so that those skills could remain within the community even after the project is completed.

He also asked what the estimated budget for the project was, noting that this would help the Committee understand the scale and value of the work being undertaken.

In conclusion, Mr Mthethwa asked whether there was a risk that the current intervention could create future unintended consequences, similar to previous interventions that had attempted to solve problems but later created new ones. He asked the presenters to explain what possible future risks might arise from the current approach and how these risks were being considered and managed.

Mr N. Paulsen (EFF), referring to the presentation on the Isimangaliso St Lucia Wetland, asked how the Department planned to enforce Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act (CARA) regulations and ensure conservation compliance, particularly in circumstances where officials were currently being prevented from visiting the area due to an impasse with local communities.

He also referred to the report's recommendation that opening the St Lucia estuary mouth would address the problem of back flooding. He noted that this had been an ongoing issue for many years. He recalled that when he first served on the Portfolio Committee approximately ten years ago, when it was part of Forestry and Fisheries, there had already been recommendations to reconcile CARA regulations with the objective of preventing the weakening or destruction of water resources.

He pointed out that Regulation 6 of CARA requires land users to protect irrigated land against waterlogging, yet in this case the flooding appeared to be largely the result of the closed estuarine mouth. He therefore asked who bears the legal and financial responsibility for the loss of agricultural land and crops that farmers have been unable to recover under these circumstances.

Mr Paulsen further asked why the Department of Agriculture was only now calling for spatial information and mapping to provide deeper insight into the issue if the matter had already been under investigation since 2021.

He also raised concerns about community relations, noting that some communities had indicated they were not willing to engage with government unless the estuarine mouth was opened. He asked what specific interventions were being proposed to rebuild trust with communities beyond simply granting their primary demand to open the estuary mouth.

Turning to the issue of dredging and the Environmental Impact Assessment, Mr Paulsen said the Basic Assessment Report appeared to present conflicting findings. On the one hand, the agricultural study indicated that there would be no impact on agricultural production potential because the area was located within a protected area. On the other hand, the hydraulic study indicated that saltation was causing significant backflooding on farmland. He asked how the Department reconciled these two conclusions.

He also referred to the wetland offset assessment, which identified strategic land acquisition as a necessary strategy for long-term ecological sustainability and recovery. He asked why the current focus and budget allocation appeared to prioritise short-term dredging interventions rather than this longer-term strategy.

Finally, Mr Paulsen asked about the role of Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife, noting that there appeared to be some challenges involving this partner institution. He asked what the contingency plan would be if these mandatory partners did not grant approval for the proposed dredging activities.

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) welcomed the presentations from the iSimangaliso Wetland Park Authority and the Department. He indicated that he had a few questions seeking clarity.

Firstly, he referred to information received by the Committee indicating that a consulting firm contracted to monitor the St Lucia Estuary had disappeared in 2018 without handing over critical monitoring data. He asked what accountability measures had been taken against the contractor and what steps the Department or the iSimangaliso Authority had implemented to prevent the loss of critical environmental data in future projects funded through public resources.

Secondly, Mr Mkhize referred to the earlier presentation which indicated that the initial emergency application submitted under Section 3A of the National Environmental Management Act (NEMA) had been refused by the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment because of insufficient baseline scientific information. He noted, however, that the project was now proceeding through a Basic Assessment process after the Minister had set aside the refusal on appeal.

He therefore asked iSimangaliso to clearly explain which specific scientific gaps identified in the original refusal had now been addressed to allow the project to proceed.

Finally, he asked how the current process ensures that the proposed intervention will not compromise the ecological integrity of the iSimangaliso Wetland Park.

Mr A de Blocq van Scheltinga (DA) welcomed the presentations from the various departments and entities. He stated that he was convinced of the need for the project to proceed and expressed satisfaction that the environmental authorisation process had been successful.

Responses

DOA

Ms Bosoga provided the following responses:

  • Land affected: Approximately 497 hectares of land have been impacted.
  • Support for farmers: Agricultural support focuses on building trust and helping farmers understand the necessary actions to ensure the system functions properly within the regulatory framework.
  • Community engagement: Awareness initiatives have been conducted in the communities of Asikhulume, Dukuza, and Monzi, focusing on the value of relevant legislation and how it can support continued farming in the floodplain.
  • Ongoing efforts: The Department intends to continue information-sharing sessions with farmers, emphasising the importance of protecting and sustainably using resources and agreeing to scientifically recommended measures.

In summary, the approach combines education, regulatory guidance, and engagement to support farming while maintaining environmental sustainability.

Ms Ntshabele thanked the Chairperson and Members for their questions and comments, noting that they were valid. She provided the following responses:

Appeal turnaround: The appeal period for interested and affected parties is 20 days from notification. Once this period expires, the Department reviews any submissions; if none are received, the activity can proceed.

Agricultural land impact: The specialist study indicated that clearing the river channel does not affect agricultural productivity, as there is no farming activity within the channel itself. Any impacts are limited to downstream areas, outside active farmland.

Alternative land for farmers: The CEO and team have considered alternative land options. Due to the complex nature of the Isimangaliso area, stakeholder engagement was conducted throughout each development phase. Engagement revealed resistance from farmers and communities, particularly regarding relocation for farming purposes.

Phased approach and cost-benefit analysis: The Department prioritised dredging as the first phase to demonstrate the benefits to communities while conducting a land use and ownership analysis and cost-benefit assessment. This analysis compares the socioeconomic and financial implications of continued flooding versus relocation, helping identify long-term sustainable options.

Community mobilisation: Despite some resistance, e.g., preference for sugarcane plantation land, the phased approach aims to socially mobilise communities, allowing them to see the benefits of eventual relocation and understand its necessity for sustainability.

In summary, Ms Ntshabele emphasised a phased, consultative approach that balances immediate interventions with medium- to long-term solutions, ensuring communities are informed, engaged, and prepared for sustainable outcomes.

iSimangaliso

Mr Bukhosini thanked the Chairperson and Members for their questions, comments, and advice, and provided the following clarifications regarding Isimangaliso's dredging and estuary management project:

Consulting firm that went AWOL: The GF5 project was funded by the World Bank, not the Department. When the original consulting firm became unresponsive, Isimangaliso referred the matter back to the World Bank. To avoid delays and address urgent ecological and community issues, the Department assisted in recruiting an internal scientific team, including Dr Ryan Cedars and a technician, to continue the work. The World Bank may still provide feedback on their original involvement, but the focus has shifted to progressive, in-house solutions.

Stakeholder engagement and consultation: Invitations to all interested and affected parties were widely circulated through district and local municipalities. The Tembe and Mthethwa communities were included in stakeholder processes, with iNkosi Tembe serving on the Isimangaliso board. Engagements also occurred via Amakosi forums, ensuring traditional authorities received updates to communicate with their constituencies.

Environmental constraints and mining: Mining of spoil sand within the core area of the park was not feasible due to ecological sensitivity. Any such action would have required a lengthy Environmental Impact Assessment with uncertain feasibility, which Isimangaliso deemed impractical.

Adaptive management approach: The project follows an adaptive management system, where scientific data collected daily informs decisions. An Estuarine Maintenance and Management Plan ensures ongoing monitoring and timely intervention as needed.

Flooding and estuary opening: The closed St. Lucia estuary mouth contributes to flooding. Community perceptions sometimes misinterpret estuary management actions. To address this, Isimangaliso has implemented a stakeholder engagement and management strategy, simplifying technical and scientific information for communities while continuously updating them via traditional councils.

Budget and funding: Initial funding of R20 million from the then-minister supports the entire process, including dredging and the Basic Assessment Report (BAR) process. Overruns occurred due to additional requirements raised by stakeholders, but all allocations comply with the PFMA. Current estimated costs for completing the project are R10–20 million, pending final Terms of Reference and market-driven budgeting.

Timeline and appeal process: The appeal period is 21 days, as noted by Ms Ntshabele. The expectation is that all processes will conclude by September–October 2026, allowing the project to commence without further delays.

Alternative land for farmers: Consideration of alternative land is a medium-term goal, requiring government-wide support. Community resistance exists, especially for fertile land like sugarcane plantations, but phased engagement aims to demonstrate long term benefits of relocation.

Ecological and socioeconomic impacts: Ecologically, mangroves and fish species have been affected but are showing signs of recovery. Economically, flooding affects subsistence and commercial farmers, impacting livelihoods and local markets. Restoration plans aim to balance environmental, social, and economic outcomes.

Data security and scientific capacity: Isimangaliso is building internal capacity, including scientists and technicians, to ensure long-term management, data integrity, and archiving. Future data collection and storage are secured for sustainability.

Community and stakeholder integration: Decisions are made through a working committee, representing farmers, traditional authorities, ratepayer associations, and departments. All stakeholders participate in drafting documents and making recommendations, ensuring inclusivity.

High-Level Panel (HLP) recommendations: The HLP emphasised integrating ecological, economic, and social considerations. Isimangaliso has incorporated these into its management, ensuring scientifically informed actions, including measures to maintain Outstanding Universal Value (OUV) recognised by UNESCO.

Oversight: The portfolio committee monitors the project closely, providing guidance and ensuring transparency and accountability throughout implementation.

In conclusion, Mr Bukhosini emphasised that Isimangaliso is using a science-driven, adaptive, and inclusive approach to restore the estuary, manage ecological and socioeconomic risks, and engage stakeholders while ensuring regulatory compliance, sustainability, and long-term monitoring.

Update on Foot and Mouth Disease

Dr Emily Mogajane, Chief Director: Biosecurity Coordination, Department of Agriculture (DoA), said the country was currently in phase one of the Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) control strategy, which was expected to take one to two years.

She reported recent outbreaks:

  • Northern Cape: Hartswater (Magareng Local Municipality) with 7000 animals affected; Kuruman (Gasegonyana Local Municipality); no further spread.
  • Western Cape: New cases in Drakenstein, George, Mossel Bay, eMfuleni, Kalkfontein, and Malmesbury; most animals vaccinated within a 10 km radius. These cases were extensions of previous outbreaks.

She highlighted the seven pillars of the FMD strategy, including vaccination, surveillance, and diagnostic capacity. Laboratory confirmation was pending for some suspect cases, but coordination with relevant authorities was ongoing to expedite results.

Provincial updates:

  • Limpopo: 42 833 communal animals, 13 934 commercial animals, and 2 411 emerging sector animals vaccinated; state veterinarians working closely with farmers.
  • Northern Cape: 824 animals vaccinated; vaccination using Biogenesis Bago and Botswana Vaccine Institute (BVI) vaccines ongoing. Support includes 4.7 million doses funded by the Department.
  • Eastern Cape: Roadblocks established, funding of R20.55 million received, and vaccination ongoing; the province had previously controlled the outbreak successfully.
  • Western Cape: 41 000 animals vaccinated; interprovincial and intraprovincial movement controls in place; awareness campaigns and contracted veterinarians assisting.
  • Free State: Outbreak increased from 277 to 307 cases; infection noted in pigs; 119 000 vaccines administered; roadblocks and joint operational cooperation enforced.
  • Northwest: 61 225 animals vaccinated, including 15 000 with Biogenesis Bago; ten movement permits issued; monitoring ongoing.
  • KwaZulu-Natal: 11 540 animals vaccinated, focusing on Ugu and Harry Gwala districts; Red Cross permits used for animals moving to abattoirs.
  • Gauteng: 281 000 animals vaccinated; revaccination planned; 721 movement permits and 3 098 direct slaughter permits issued.
  • Mpumalanga: 109 animals vaccinated in February, plus 24 000 with Biogenesis Bago; 19 Red Cross permits issued for direct slaughter.

Dr Mogajane emphasised the importance of revaccination schedules, depending on vaccine type --BVI: three months; Agricultural Research Council (ARC): longer; Biogenesis: six months.

The total number of vaccinated animals since new vaccines arrived was approximately 291 000.

She explained that KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) was an epicentre of infection, contributing to the spread in the Eastern Cape, Northwest, Free State, and Mpumalanga. Vaccination efforts aimed to eliminate the virus in this province. Limpopo and Mpumalanga included buffer zones, particularly near Kruger National Park, where buffaloes may carry the disease.

A national command centre was coordinating with provincial joint operational centres, collecting epidemiological data to monitor FMD progress. A public website would provide access to outbreak data.

Dr Mogajane concluded by requesting that the Portfolio Committee take note of the current FMD update, ongoing activities across provinces, and responses to previous questions.

Discussion

The Chairperson said the brief indicated that since last week, the movement of animals was still a problem. Instead of decreasing, the situation had worsened in some provinces -- in Mpumalanga, cases had increased from 79 to 88, and in the Free State, from 277 to 307.

Mr Z Mthethwa (MKP) asked if they had received and shared the test results of the vaccines from Argentina to ensure they were compliant with the South African FMD strain? There was a need for confirmation that they were vaccinating with a solution appropriate for the local strain. Was the strain shared with Argentina to produce this vaccine, or was it a coincidence that they had an exact match? Were unemployed graduates -- some 5 000 students -- being involved in the process to track progress? Had it been confirmed that auctions had been the causative factor in the national spread, or was this spread due to collaboration between private and public sectors in addressing FMD? What measures were in place, given that KZN was an epicentre, to ensure that food from affected animals was safe for human consumption? What steps were being taken to protect future animals from infection in this epicentre?

Ms L Arries (EFF) asked what criteria the DoA uses to determine whether an outbreak was new or linked to an existing outbreak. Had it conducted an internal review to determine whether delays or poor coordination had contributed to the outbreak's escalation? Regarding last week’s presentation, there was a backlog, so could the Department provide details on the exact backlog for laboratory confirmations, why testing systems were unable to keep up, how many suspected cases were waiting for lab confirmation, and how long farmers had been waiting?

Inkosi R Cebekhulu (IFP) raised concerns about KZN's lack of enforcement, yet it was a main source of the FMD spread. The uMfolozi Park and private government reserves were keeping buffalo, which contributed to the spread. The focus seemed to be on commercial farmers, but there was a need to educate communal livestock farmers about the risk of moving animals. Illegal movement of livestock after hours, especially at night, continued from north of uThukela to south of uThukela. National and provincial departments must act to prevent this. Livestock theft contributed to unregulated night movement, bypassing monitoring systems.

The Chairperson said the challenge was that the Director-General (DG) and Deputy Director-General (DDG) of the DoA were not present, which was unfortunate because there were outstanding questions, including on collaboration with the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR). Previously, she had asked how the Department was collaborating with the CSIR, which had accredited laboratories that could fast-track testing and confirm positive cases. The CSIR also had the capacity to produce limited vaccine doses to supplement ARC production. It was unacceptable that this collaboration was not being leveraged. The Department should submit a detailed report on collaborations with CSIR for FMD control and management. Relying on imports instead of strengthening our own institutions was a problem. There was no clear evidence that extraordinary measures were being taken to ensure the ARC was fully funded for the FMD facility.

In 2024, this Committee’s oversight visit to ARC and Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP) had revealed that the ARC had partial funding -- around R400 million -- and had requested the balance to finalise the FMD facility. Recommendations had been made to the Ministers of Agriculture and Finance to fund ARC fully, allowing local vaccine production. Outright reliance on imports was not sustainable.

The Department of Agriculture was legally responsible for FMD, a state-controlled disease. Other departments and entities must be involved because FMD affects farmers, the national economy, food security, and export markets. Vaccinations had started, but infections continued to increase.

The issue of animal movement must be addressed. In the Free State, the cause of increased infections was unclear; in KZN, animals were moving over long distances to the Western Cape. Those responsible for perpetuating illegal animal movement must be held accountable. Departments must show tangible results from vaccinations, ensuring that infections decrease over time. The movement problem must be investigated and resolved to prevent further spread.

Responses

Dr Magajane, together with Dr Botlhe Modisane, Chief Director: Animal Production and Health, DoA, responded to several issues raised by Committee Members.

She began with the matter of unemployed graduates who had not yet been absorbed into the sector. She said the ARC had been requested to appoint animal health technicians, and only 20 had been appointed to date, which remained insufficient. Collaboration with the red meat industry had resulted in funding to support employment of additional animal technicians. Within two weeks, these technicians would commence training at the University of Pretoria to assist with vaccination processes.

Regarding vaccination procedures, Dr Magajane explained that if technicians or veterinarians visited infected farms, they had to undergo quarantine, necessitating the deployment of additional trained personnel. The newly employed technicians would receive training in FMD procedures to ensure proper implementation.

On the issue of auctions, she said that auctions operated under the Agricultural Produce Agents Act, and were primarily managed by the private sector, making closures challenging. The Department had recommended the closure of auctions in epicentres, which some provinces had implemented, while others had requested national assistance. Some FMD infections were detected in auctions, while others occurred elsewhere. Last year, the Minister published a gazette preventing the gathering of animals, and online auctions were suggested as an alternative.

Regarding meat safety, Dr Magajane emphasised that FMD was highly contagious among animals, but did not affect humans. Properly processed infected meat, with removal of glands and the fifth quarter, was safe for consumption. Ongoing research was examining the virus in bone marrow and the fifth quarter, which may inform potential future policy amendments.

She said animals bred in certain areas, such as Africana cattle, may exhibit different behaviours and may not show clinical signs like dairy cows, which could develop mastitis, decreased milk production, or hoof infections. Vaccinations were critical, particularly in sensitive areas. She also highlighted that some community farmers, including certain Muslim farmers, were not exporting and had not implemented containment measures. Effective management of infections in communal areas was essential, as some animals may show subclinical signs, posing risks for continued disease spread.

Dr Modisane addressed the remaining questions raised by Members. He apologised on behalf of the DDG, who had been summoned to the Minister’s office before the meeting, and added that the DG had also sent apologies.

Regarding collaboration with the CSIR, he said the DG had organised a meeting to discuss joint work with the ARC on vaccine production. The CSIR's focus was primarily on Ribonucleic acid (RNA), deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and plant-based remedies. A full report on this collaboration would be provided to the Committee.

On vaccine testing, before importing Argentinian Dollvet and Biogenesis Bago vaccines, samples had been sent to the Pirbright Institute in the UK to ensure strain compatibility. The strains present in South Africa were also present in these vaccines. One test (SAT2), which had caused challenges in KZN, was still awaited. Experts had affirmed that even with slight mismatches, increasing protective doses in vaccines would provide sufficient protection. The Argentinian vaccine uses a high protective dose (e.g., 32) to ensure efficacy, and doses could be adjusted if vaccine failures occurred. The recent rains had affected progress, with 291 000 animals vaccinated to date, especially in the Free State and North West.

Regarding laboratory backlogs, additional laboratories in the country were being engaged to assist with blood testing and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for virus detection.

On law enforcement, movement of animals may still occur. Permits, such as Red Cross permits, required officials to verify disease-free status and enforce a 28-day quarantine, but some movement was inevitable.

Regarding support for farmers, he said complaints had arisen that communal farmers were receiving less attention than commercial farmers. The Department was aiming to balance support and vaccination efforts across emerging, commercial, and communal farmers. Reports would indicate numbers vaccinated in each category.

Dr Modisane concluded by adding to Dr Magajane's responses, to ensure all areas of concern were addressed.

Further discussion

Mr A Mngxitama (MKP) expressed deep disappointment regarding the reallocation of R16 million from small-scale farmers to the FMD programme. He requested that this matter be included on the agenda for the next meeting, with the Minister and DG present, and a written commitment that funds would not be diverted. He cited complaints from small-scale farmers in Gauteng who had been adversely affected.

The Chairperson clarified that provinces had autonomy over allocated funds, and that the Minister and DG had not provided written confirmation regarding transfers. The Committee would follow up, including engagement with the Chairperson of the National Council of Provinces (NCOP), to ensure that small-scale farmers’ funds were protected.

Mr Mngxitama insisted that the money belonged to small-scale farmers, and must be returned.

The Chairperson emphasised that her intention was to remind Members of the previous meeting, and that she was not patronising Mr Mngxitama. She assured him that the Committee was on the side of small-scale farmers, and would intervene where necessary.

Dr Modisane noted that the Department had not yet consulted with the DG and Minister on the matter, and requested an opportunity to provide feedback after consultation regarding whether the decision was final, or only a proposal.

The Chairperson requested a follow-up to ensure that allocations for small-scale farmers were not diverted, and that consultations were reported back to the Committee.

She also emphasised that while the complete movement of animals may not be stopped, collaboration with other institutions, including the police, was essential to reduce movement and infection spread. Vaccination progress should be monitored weekly, or by the end of the month.

She thanked all Members and departmental officials for their contributions, and emphasised the need for continued collaboration to combat FMD and maintain South Africa’s FMD-free status.

The meeting was adjourned.
