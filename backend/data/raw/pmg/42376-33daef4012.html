2025 National Senior Certificate Exam Outcomes & Second Chance Matric Programme; with Deputy Minister

Basic Education

17 February 2026
Chairperson: Ms J Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Committee was briefed on the 2025 National Senior Certificate (NSC) examination outcomes, systemic performance trends, infrastructure challenges, learner support, governance concerns, and quality assurance matters.

The Department reported a 79% pass rate in the 2025 NSC examinations. While Members acknowledged the improvement, concerns were raised about the interpretation of the pass rate, learner throughput, Mathematics participation, and the definition of “quality passes.”

Umalusi confirmed that the standardisation process does not compensate for poor teaching or curriculum weaknesses. The Chairperson of Umalusi emphasised that the NSC is internationally comparable and remains the strongest predictor of university success. Umalusi also reported the highest proportion of raw results accepted without adjustment (approximately 82%), indicating increasing system stability.

Concerns were raised about declining Mathematics and Accounting results. The Director-General noted that Mathematics performance had improved in previous years and only declined in the latest cycle. Debate emerged around the narrative that privileges university-bound learners over those pursuing Technical Mathematics and vocational pathways.

Umalusi highlighted persistent group copying in certain examination centres as a serious concern. High-risk centres had been identified and subjected to enhanced monitoring. The CEO of Umalusi stressed that educators involved in examination misconduct must face disciplinary action and possible referral to SACE.

The Department confirmed that the national investigation task team had completed its work regarding the alleged examination leakage, and the implicated official remained suspended pending finalisation of forensic processes.

Members expressed deep concern that approximately 200 000 matriculants cannot be accounted for in higher education, training institutions, or employment. A joint sitting involving Basic Education, Higher Education, Labour, and other relevant departments would be prioritised to address learner transition into post-school opportunities.

The Umalusi Chairperson stressed that the schooling system should not be designed solely around university entry, but rather to produce an educated citizenry capable of economic participation and democratic engagement.

Members reported schools operating in dilapidated conditions, overcrowded classrooms, combined primary and secondary arrangements, and long-standing uncompleted projects.

The Committee resolved to convene a focused infrastructure engagement on 3 March and proposed inviting the Department of Public Works and Infrastructure, Provincial Education Departments, and National Treasury to account directly. Members expressed frustration with ongoing delays attributed to implementing agents.

The Deputy Minister addressed concerns regarding learners in quintile 1–3 schools who are SASSA beneficiaries. Discussions were underway with the Department of Social Development to address the challenge of learners losing grant support upon turning 18 while still in Grade 12.

Members raised strong concerns about School Governing Bodies requiring parents to fund essential learning materials such as textbooks. The Committee indicated that access to education should not depend on a parent’s ability to contribute financially.

The Department clarified that not all undocumented learners are foreign nationals; some are South African citizens lacking documentation due to family circumstances. Joint interventions with the Department of Home Affairs were ongoing, though immigration-related matters remain complex.

The Department outlined intervention measures for under-performing schools, including raising the performance threshold, deploying support teams, and strengthening district accountability. However, Members highlighted concerns about sustainability and inconsistent consequence management.

Circuit manager accountability and alleged politicised appointments were also raised. The Department indicated that investigations and oversight mechanisms were in place.

Members insisted that serious allegations, particularly those involving senior officials, should not be addressed solely through written responses and may require in-person accountability. Specific schools were flagged for urgent intervention.

Meeting report

Opening

The Chairperson opened the joint sitting by extending a warm welcome to the members, the Chairperson of the Select Committee on Education from the National Council of Provinces (NCOP), members of the Select Committee, and all delegates present. She also welcomed the Deputy Minister, the Director-General, the Department of Basic Education (DBE) delegation, Umalusi representatives, stakeholders in attendance, and the respective support teams.

The Chairperson explained that the purpose of the meeting was to engage the Department of Basic Education and Umalusi on the 2025 National Senior Certificate (NSC) examination outcomes. She indicated that, as the NCOP Select Committee on Education would also be focusing on the same matter during the week, it had been agreed that a joint sitting would be convened to ensure efficiency and coordinated oversight.

She then invited the Chairperson of the NCOP Select Committee to introduce his delegation. The Chairperson further indicated that the session would be structured in two parts, with her presiding over the first round of engagement and the NCOP Chairperson leading the second round of questions. She thereafter handed over proceedings to the NCOP Chairperson, Mr Makhi Feni.

Mr Makhi Feni (ANC), Chairperson of the Select Committee on Education in the NCOP, greeted all colleagues and expressed appreciation for the continued presence and cooperation of the Deputy Minister, noting that the Deputy Minister had consistently engaged with and supported the Select Committee without disappointment. He also acknowledged the Director-General, accompanying officials, and the support and administrative staff from the NCOP, commending them for their consistent preparation and support in ensuring that committee meetings function effectively.

The Chairperson indicated that he was accompanied by members of the Select Committee and reflected that this joint sitting was a historic first arrangement between the two committees. He considered whether to invite members to introduce themselves individually, but noted the constraints of time, as the committee had another sitting scheduled immediately after the meeting.

The following Members of the Select Committee on Education, Innovation and Sciences introduced themselves during the meeting: Ms N Du Plessis (DA, Gauteng), Ms J Adriaanse (DA, North West), Ms M Kennedy (EFF, Limpopo), Mr P Sibande (ANC, Mpumalanga), Ms A Matshobeni (EFF, Eastern Cape), and Ms D Fienies (ANC, Northern Cape). Each Member confirmed their participation in the joint sitting and acknowledged the Chairperson.

Chairperson Maimela indicated that members of the Portfolio Committee would introduce themselves during their engagements, as the members of the Select Committee had already done so. Before proceeding to discussions, she commended the Class of 2025 for sustaining an upward trajectory in performance, achieving an overall National Senior Certificate pass rate of 88%.

She noted that over the past five years, performance had steadily improved, rising from 80% in 2022 and recovering from the COVID-19 period, during which results had declined to approximately 76%. She described the sustained improvement as commendable and extended appreciation to learners, teachers, district officials, schools, the Department of Basic Education, provincial departments, and all stakeholders who contributed to the achievement.

Chairperson Maimela further emphasised that, while the performance deserved recognition, Parliament also had a responsibility to engage critically with public discourse surrounding the results. She observed that despite the sustained improvement, there remained significant criticism regarding the quality and credibility of outcomes in the basic education sector. She indicated that the Department would be expected to respond to such concerns during the engagement.

In managing proceedings, she reminded Members and presenters that the meeting was constrained by time, particularly as Members were required to attend the State of the Nation Address debate later that afternoon. Given that both the Department of Basic Education and Umalusi had circulated comprehensive presentations in advance, she requested that each entity limit its oral presentation to 15 minutes, focusing on key highlights and critical issues to allow sufficient time for Members' questions.

Before handing over to the Deputy Minister and the Department, the Chairperson requested the Committee Secretary to table apologies. Apologies were received from the Minister of Basic Education, Ms Siviwe Gwarube, who was attending a Cabinet meeting. Further apologies were submitted from Members of the Portfolio and Select Committees.

Dr Reginah Mhaule, Deputy Minister of Basic Education

 greeted both Chairpersons and members of the Portfolio and Select Committees, explaining that her remarks would precede the Department's formal presentation and would not form part of the allocated 15-minute presentation time. She informed the committees that both she and the Minister had been attending another meeting but had agreed that she would leave early to attend the joint sitting, with the Minister expected to join later.

She indicated that this was the Department of Basic Education’s first appearance before Parliament in the new academic year, noting that the 2025 school year had commenced with certain challenges, including learner transport accidents and pressures relating to school admissions. She highlighted that rapid population growth, coupled with financial constraints, had slowed the pace of school infrastructure development, thereby intensifying admission pressures.

Reflecting on the announcement of the 2025 National Senior Certificate results, the Deputy Minister acknowledged that the sector continued to face criticism regarding the quality of its outcomes. However, she maintained that the Department was moving in the right direction and described the improvement in results as part of a long-term journey rather than an overnight achievement. She cited positive feedback regarding South African learners’ performance both in local universities and internationally, noting that many learners not only obtained academic certificates but also earned distinctions and awards, which, in her view, demonstrated that the education system was stronger than often perceived.

Addressing recurring criticism of the 30% pass requirement, she reiterated that learners must pass a minimum number of subjects to qualify and that a 30% mark in a single subject does not automatically translate into university admission. She questioned whether it would be reasonable to fail a learner who had performed well in most subjects because of one lower mark and argued that the current pass standard was fair, even if not ideal.

The Deputy Minister reported that participation and retention rates had improved significantly from Grade 1 through to Grade 10, although dropout rates remained a concern between Grades 10 and 12. She noted that, contrary to common assumptions, dropout rates were higher among boys than girls and attributed this trend to broader social challenges affecting young men. She suggested that greater attention should be directed towards supporting the boy child, as policy interventions in recent years had focused heavily on issues such as teenage pregnancy affecting girls.

She emphasised the need to strengthen performance in gateway subjects, particularly mathematics, where both participation and pass rates remained below desired levels. While other gateway subjects such as physical sciences and economics, had shown better performance, mathematics required urgent attention. She encouraged school principals not to discourage learners from taking mathematics and science combinations, as subject choices significantly influence access to university programmes such as medicine. At the same time, she raised concerns about the limited capacity of higher education institutions, pointing out that while more than 600 000 learners complete matric annually, universities admit only approximately 200 000 students, leaving a substantial number without placement.

The Deputy Minister stressed that the Department must prevent any regression in pass rates, acknowledging that while improvement is difficult, decline can occur easily if vigilance is not maintained. She reported that the Director-General had been visiting districts nationwide and that district officials had expressed appreciation for the renewed focus and support.

In conclusion, she underscored the importance of strengthening foundational learning in the early grades, stating that a strong foundation is critical to long-term educational success. She reiterated that the Seventh Administration’s priorities include foundational learning reform, consistent with commitments made during the recent Lekgotla and international engagements such as the G20. She called for closer collaboration between the Basic Education and Higher Education portfolios to avoid blame-shifting and to align outputs with tertiary capacity and labour market needs. She further emphasised the need to expand skills development pathways, arguing that the goal of the education system should not be limited to university access but should also ensure that young people are equipped with practical skills to contribute meaningfully to the economy.

The Director-General thanked the Chairpersons and Members of both the Portfolio and Select Committees, as well as the Deputy Minister, and outlined the structure of the Department's presentation. He indicated that the presentation was divided into four parts.

The first part focused on the National Senior Certificate results for the Class of 2025, with an emphasis on analysing the data to extract lessons and inform ongoing improvement efforts. He stressed that the analysis of results is not a once-off exercise but forms part of a continuous process of reflection and system strengthening.

The second part of the presentation addressed administrative and behavioural irregularities identified during the examination cycle. The third part responded to directives issued by Umalusi following its quality assurance review of the November–December 2025 examinations, noting that both the Department’s response and Umalusi’s report would reflect on these directives. The fourth and final part dealt with the Second Chance Matric Programme, including an explanation of the notable improvement recorded among part-time candidates.

The Director-General further informed the committees that the meeting might be the last of its kind attended by Professor Yunus Ballim, Chairperson of the Council of Umalusi, who was concluding his term after serving two terms on the Council. He described Prof Ballim as one of the country's distinguished public servants who had consistently contributed to strengthening quality assurance in the education system.

He concluded by indicating that Ms Priscilla Ogunbanjo would present the first and final sections of the Department’s presentation, allocating approximately ten minutes to her input and five minutes to the remaining segments, after which the Chairpersons would determine the sequencing of the engagement with Umalusi.

DBE Briefing

Ms Priscilla Ogunbanjo, Director: National Assessment and Public Examination, DBE, greeted the Chairpersons, the Deputy Minister, the Director-General, and the Committee Members. She indicated that she would focus primarily on reflections arising from learner performance in the 2025 National Senior Certificate (NSC), rather than repeating statistical results already circulated. She explained that the presentation would emphasise lessons learned, the Department's response to Umalusi directives, and the Second Chance Matric Programme. She further noted that the NSC serves multiple purposes, including providing diagnostic data, and that the Department uses performance data and analytical reports to inform targeted support programmes.

In tracking the Class of 2025, she reiterated that learner retention from Grade 1 to Grade 10 remains relatively stable across the system, with more significant dropout patterns emerging between Grades 10 and 12. She highlighted specific pressure points in Grades 4, 8, and 10, where repetition contributes to enrolment "bulges." Despite these challenges, she reported that the system's throughput rate had improved from 45 per cent in 2008 to 64 per cent in 2025 within the public basic education sector, noting that when independent examination bodies such as SACAI and the IEB are included, the overall rate is even higher.

She informed the committees that the Class of 2025 was the largest cohort to sit for the NSC examinations. At the same time, part-time candidate numbers had decreased, which she described as a positive development, reflecting improved pass rates among full-time candidates and the availability of the May/June examination cycle as an additional opportunity for those needing to rewrite.

Ms Ogunbanjo reported that enrolment patterns by gender remained relatively consistent, with approximately 55% female and 44% male candidates. Overall enrolments increased across subjects, including technology-related subjects, although participation in some of these areas remained below desired levels. She noted a reduction in the number of progressed learners, indicating that more learners were advancing to higher grades through direct promotion rather than progression concessions.

She further highlighted an increase in the number of learners with special education needs receiving accommodations, attributing this not to a rise in disability prevalence but to improved identification systems and the effective provision of necessary concessions and support measures.

Finally, she drew attention to the socio-economic profile of candidates, stating that approximately 79% of learners were beneficiaries of social grants. However, she cautioned that many learners turned 18 during 2025 and therefore no longer qualified for certain grant benefits, which may have had implications for their support during the examination year. She concluded by indicating that the remainder of her presentation would focus on the key lessons derived from the data and the Department’s strategic response.

Ms Ogunbanjo outlined the Department's priorities going forward, indicating that particular attention would be given to key transition points in the schooling system where repetition and dropout rates remain high. She identified Grades 3 to 4, 7 to 8, 9 to 10, and 10 to 11 as areas of concern, noting that repetition is most prominent at these stages. She further highlighted significant dropout patterns between Grades 10 and 11 and between Grades 11 and 12 and confirmed that these phases would receive focused intervention.

She reiterated that the increase in identified learners with special education needs reflected improvements in diagnostic systems and the awarding of appropriate accommodations. However, she confirmed that participation in mathematics remained a concern, with approximately 35 per cent of learners taking mathematics compared to 65 per cent opting for mathematical literacy. The Department intends to intensify efforts to encourage greater participation in mathematics.

On examination administration, Ms Ogunbanjo stated that the 2025 examinations were generally well managed. While acknowledging the reported question paper leakage, she emphasised that irregularities remained minimal in proportion to the scale of the examination system. With nearly 7 000 examination centres and over 900 000 candidates, administrative irregularities affected approximately 0.15 per cent of candidates and behavioural irregularities approximately 0.14 per cent, which she described as statistically minimal. She informed the committees that the Minister had appointed a National Investigation Task Team to probe the leakage, that preliminary findings had been submitted, and that final recommendations were expected by March. The task team had already made recommendations regarding examination administration, security processes, Umalusi’s oversight role, and the strengthening of regulatory frameworks.

In terms of improvement measures for 2026, she indicated that the Department would prioritise increased psychological support for learners, given the personal and social challenges many candidates face. She further acknowledged that while disciplinary processes for learners implicated in irregularities are handled expeditiously, similar urgency is often lacking in cases involving educators or officials. The Department is therefore engaging labour relations directorates to fast-track disciplinary processes where officials are implicated.

Additional measures include strengthening question paper security at the national level, improving quality assurance processes to eliminate errors, and enhancing moderation standards. Although only four errors were identified across 162 question papers, Ms Ogunbanjo emphasised that even a single error is unacceptable, as it may disadvantage candidates. In instances where errors materially affect performance, the affected questions are excluded from marking.

The Department also intends to improve the standard and quality of School-Based Assessment (SBA), strengthen provincial monitoring capacity, intensify the drive to ensure that all learners with special needs receive appropriate accommodations, enhance training for invigilators, and address persistent shortages of markers in specific subjects by improving recruitment and planning processes.

Ms Ogunbanjo proceeded to provide a brief overview of performance outcomes, reiterating that the overall pass rate for the Class of 2025 was 88 per cent. She reported that seven of the nine provinces recorded improvements, with the Northern Cape achieving the highest increase at 3.6 per cent. The Eastern Cape recorded a slight decline of 0.8%, while the Free State reflected the largest decrease at 1.66%. She indicated that the Department would analyse the underlying causes of the declines in these two provinces and provide targeted support accordingly.

She noted that no-fee paying schools performed well, with improvements evident across quintiles, and that fee-paying schools continued to perform comparatively well, including when benchmarked against independent schools. Although female candidates constituted many enrolments, male learners performed slightly better overall. She emphasised that the narrowing of the performance gap between the highest- and lowest-performing provinces was particularly significant. The differential, which had previously stood at 13.2 percentage points, had reduced to 6.4 percentage points, indicating that systemic performance disparities were diminishing and that improvements were occurring across the system rather than in isolated pockets.

Regarding the quality of passes, she reported that 46.4% of candidates achieved admission to bachelor's degree studies, representing the highest number recorded to date. She further indicated that only 343 candidates out of approximately 746 000 obtained a National Senior Certificate without qualifying for admission to higher education programmes. The overwhelming majority of candidates passed at levels that enable access to higher education pathways, whether at bachelor's, diploma, or higher certificate levels. She noted that while the 343 candidates without higher education eligibility could still enter the labour market, the overall results reflected broad access to post-school opportunities.

Ms Ogunbanjo also addressed equity trends across school quintiles. She explained that although approximately 80% of candidates originate from Quintiles 1 to 3 schools, historically, the majority of bachelor-level passes had come disproportionately from Quintiles 4 and 5. However, this pattern had shifted significantly. She reported that 66.7% of bachelor’s passes now originate from Quintiles 1 to 3 schools, reflecting improved performance among learners from lower-income communities. She described this as a critical equity milestone and indicated that the Department aims to continue progressing toward proportional alignment with enrolment patterns.

She concluded by reiterating concerns raised by the Deputy Minister regarding capacity constraints in the higher education sector. While the number of candidates qualifying for bachelor’s and diploma studies continues to increase, the absorption capacity of universities remains limited. She stressed that diploma passes constitute strong qualifications but are often undervalued in public discourse, and she suggested that greater emphasis should be placed on strengthening and promoting diverse post-school pathways beyond traditional university admission.

Briefing by Umalusi

Prof Yunus Ballim, Chairperson of Umalusi, greeted the Chairpersons of both committees, honourable members and officials present, and extended his best wishes for the work of the committees in 2026. He introduced the Umalusi delegation accompanying him, including Dr Mafu Rakometsi, Chief Executive Officer of Umalusi; Dr Eva Sujee; Senior Manager for Quality Assurance and Assessment for School Qualifications; and Acting Senior Manager for Public Relations and Communications.

Prof Ballim explained that Umalusi carries out its legislative mandate through a standard-setting and quality assurance framework designed to safeguard the integrity of qualifications. He noted that the National Senior Certificate (NSC) is an exit-level qualification at NQF Level 4 offered by both public and independent schools, and that the Department of Basic Education is one of three assessment bodies responsible for administering the qualification.

He reminded members that Umalusi had commissioned an international benchmarking study conducted by UK ENIC (formerly UK NARIC), now operating as Ecctis, to compare the NSC against comparable international qualifications. The NSC was benchmarked against the International Baccalaureate Diploma Programme, the Kenyan Certificate of Secondary Education, the New South Wales Higher School Certificate (Australia), the Zimbabwean Forms Five to Six Advanced Level, and Cambridge Assessment International Education AS and A Levels. The study concluded that the NSC provides its holders with an internationally comparable level of education. Prof Ballim informed members that the full report is publicly available on the Umalusi website.

He emphasised that, based on these findings and its statutory mandate, Umalusi remains committed to jealously safeguarding the credibility, authenticity, and integrity of its certificates. He stated that credible certification is the most important assurance that can be provided to young people in South Africa.

Turning to the matter of examination credibility, Prof Ballim recalled that the Minister of Basic Education had informed the nation on 11 December 2025 of a breach relating to the NSC examinations, specifically affecting Mathematics Papers 1 and 2, Physical Sciences Papers 1 and 2, and English Home Language Papers 1 to 3. He indicated that Umalusi had engaged with the matter within its quality assurance and oversight mandate.

The Chairperson of Umalusi informed the committees that a National Investigation Task Team (NITT) had been established to investigate the source and spread of the reported examination breach. He stated that the Executive Committee of Umalusi had received an interim report from the NITT on 6 January, presented by its Chairperson, Professor Chika Sehoole, and Deputy Chairperson, Mr Brian Schreuder.

According to the interim findings, the breach was limited to three subjects, affecting seven question papers, and was localised to approximately 40 candidates across seven examination centres in the Pretoria area. In the context of a total enrolment of approximately 600,000 candidates who wrote the affected examinations, Umalusi assured Members that the breach was contained and did not compromise the overall credibility of the 2025 NSC results.

He further indicated that while the number of implicated learners currently stood at approximately 40, the investigation remained ongoing. Should additional individuals be identified, appropriate consequences would follow. He noted that Umalusi participates in the NITT process as an observer and emphasised that, in terms of Section 21(2)–(4) of the amended General and Further Education and Training Quality Assurance Act, Umalusi is empowered to withhold or cancel certificates where necessary.

The Chairperson commended the Department of Basic Education for detecting the breach early through its quality control systems and for appointing a credible investigative team to address the matter.

Turning to the main business of the meeting, he indicated that Umalusi’s presentation would be delivered by Dr Mafu Rakometsi and Dr Eva Sujee. The presentation would highlight areas of improvement identified in the Department’s examination processes, including compliance with directives, improvements in question paper moderation, school-based assessment, state of readiness, marking processes and guidelines, standardisation procedures, verification of marking, and recurring areas of non-compliance that remain of concern to Umalusi. He concluded by handing over to Dr Rakometsi to proceed with the presentation.

Dr Mafu Rakometsi thanked the Chairperson and Members and indicated that he would not read the full presentation outline but would highlight key aspects of Umalusi's mandate. He noted that Umalusi quality assures a range of qualifications and informed the committees that the National Certificate (Vocational) Levels 2 and 3 are being discontinued, as these levels are being internalised by the Department of Higher Education and Training. He further indicated that certain legacy or phased-out qualifications are also being discontinued.

Dr Rakometsi drew the attention of members to the legislative framework that underpins Umalusi’s work, specifically the National Qualifications Framework Act, No. 7 of 2008, as amended, and the General and Further Education and Training Quality Assurance Act, which establishes Umalusi and defines its powers and responsibilities. He referred members to the relevant clauses cited in the presentation that govern Umalusi’s quality assurance, standard-setting, and certification functions. He then handed over to Dr Eva Sujee to proceed with the substantive presentation.

Dr Eva Sujee greeted Members and indicated that her presentation would focus on Umalusi's framework for the quality assurance of assessment. She explained that Umalusi follows a comprehensive quality assurance process to ensure that all certificates it issues are credible. While the presentation focused primarily on the Department of Basic Education (DBE), she noted that quality assurance samples also included the other assessment bodies, namely SACAI and the IEB. She further drew attention to the fact that, due to budget constraints, some sampling processes had been conducted on a smaller scale than ideal.

She reported that in 2024, Umalusi conducted its usual quality assurance processes, including monitoring the DBE’s state of readiness for the November–December examinations. A state of readiness presentation was made to the Portfolio Committee on Basic Education on 24 October, and Umalusi expressed satisfaction with the examination systems and irregularity management structures in place at that stage. Umalusi issued its Quality Assurance of Assessment Report to the DBE on 29 December. It also participated in marking guideline standardisation meetings across 68 subjects, verified the marking of 37 subjects, and considered 68 subjects presented by the DBE for standardisation.

Dr Sujee explained that areas of improvement reflected both compliance with previous directives and innovations introduced by the DBE. She highlighted internal moderation and the predictability of question papers as strong areas of compliance. However, she noted recurring areas of non-compliance, particularly low performance in criteria related to text selection, the types and quality of questions, and the accuracy and reliability of marking guidelines.

Concerning school-based assessment (SBA), she reported improvements in the quality of English First Additional Language tasks and in mathematics, particularly in the organisation of learner and teacher files and in marking quality compared to previous cycles. Nonetheless, recurring weaknesses were identified in the alignment of assessment tasks with curriculum and assessment policy statements (CAPS), cognitive levels and degrees of difficulty, as well as in the quality of internal moderation and the provision of constructive feedback to learners.

On the monitoring of state of readiness, she highlighted positive developments, including the introduction of commitment agreements signed by parents to reinforce adherence to examination rules and regulations. However, she raised concerns about unfilled vacancies within provincial and district examination and assessment directorates, as well as delays in vetting staff appointed to handle live question papers. In terms of marker recruitment, she commended the Eastern Cape for introducing an online application system for recruiting marking personnel.

Regarding the writing and marking of examinations, she concurred with the earlier statement that Umalusi was generally satisfied with the conduct of examinations. However, she pointed to recurring non-compliance issues, including inconsistencies in invigilators’ execution of duties, cases of candidates found in possession of unauthorised materials, and instances of group copying detected during the marking phase.

In relation to marking guidelines and standardisation, she reported overall improvement and expressed satisfaction with the marking processes. However, she noted late submission of internal moderators’ reports and instances where senior marking personnel failed to meet pre-marking requirements. She also raised concerns about non-compliance in the execution of roles and responsibilities by contracted security personnel at marking centres.

On verification of marking, Dr Sujee reported significant improvement in the consistent application of approved marking guidelines and moderation processes. Nevertheless, she noted non-adherence in some cases to the prescribed one-to-five ratio for the appointment of Deputy Chief Markers to Senior Markers, as well as poor quality marking and internal moderation in Life Orientation in certain provinces. She concluded by handing over to the Chief Executive Officer to address the standardisation process.

Dr Rakometsi addressed the committees on the standardisation process. He explained that standardisation is a statistical process used to control for factors other than a learner's knowledge, ability, and aptitude that may influence examination performance. Umalusi is mandated, in terms of its founding legislation, to adjust raw marks during the standardisation process where necessary. He noted that this practice predates Umalusi and was also applied during the era of the former South African Certification Council.

He emphasised that Umalusi standardises the results of all assessment bodies under its jurisdiction, not only those of the Department of Basic Education. Standardisation is applied to ensure that learners are neither advantaged nor disadvantaged by extraneous factors and to maintain equivalence of standards across examination cycles. This ensures that the qualifications delivered to universities, colleges, employers, parents, and learners remain consistent over time.

With respect to the National Senior Certificate, he explained that 25 per cent of the final mark comprises School-Based Assessment (SBA), while 75 per cent is derived from the written examination component. It is the written component that is subject to standardisation. The Assessment Standards Committee of Council considers multiple sources of evidence before making standardisation decisions, including detailed reports submitted by both the Department and Umalusi. He clarified that members of the Assessment Standards Committee are independent experts drawn from universities and research institutions and are not employees of Umalusi.

He reported that 68 subjects were submitted by the Department for standardisation in 2025. Of these, 56 were accepted at the raw mark level, seven were adjusted mainly upwards, and five were adjusted mainly downwards. Similar processes were applied to the results of the IEB and SACAI. He indicated that the presentation also outlined examination challenges encountered during the cycle but did not elaborate on them during his remarks.

In conclusion, Dr Rakometsi confirmed that Umalusi had issued a formal approval statement to the Department regarding the release of the 2025 NSC results. He expressed appreciation to the Portfolio Committee, the Chairperson, members, the Director-General, and the Minister for their support throughout the year.

Discussion

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) raised concerns regarding recurring cases of group copying and alleged invigilator complicity during examinations, and requested clarity on what structural reforms, beyond administrative directives, had been implemented to address these challenges. She further asked how many schools had been blacklisted on the SunShield system by the Department of Basic Education over the past three years.

Drawing on observations from oversight visits in Limpopo, she expressed concern that several learners did not possess documentation confirming their South African citizenship, including some learners currently in Grade 12. She sought clarity on the Department’s plan to ensure that affected learners are assisted to obtain the necessary documentation before writing their final examinations.

Ms Xaba-Ntshaba also raised concerns about teacher absenteeism, particularly in Life Orientation, citing a complaint from a parent in Ivory Park alleging that certain teachers were not attending classes. She requested intervention from the Department to address the matter.

Mr S Louw (ANC) directed several questions to both Umalusi and the Department of Basic Education.

Addressing Umalusi, he asked, given that only 3% of examination centres were monitored, how many centres Umalusi could confidently confirm were free of systematic irregularities. He further queried whether Umalusi believed that the standardisation process was effectively compensating for weaknesses in curriculum delivery and requested trend data comparing raw marks with adjusted marks over time.

Turning to the Director-General, Mr Louw revisited a previous question regarding alleged irregularities in the filling of a vacant post. He clarified that the post in question was that of Chief Director: Media Liaison and National and Provincial Communication. He alleged that after the closing date for applications, an instruction from the Minister's office resulted in the inclusion of a curriculum vitae that had not been submitted within the stipulated timeframe, and that HR was directed to include the candidate despite the application period having closed. He requested that the matter be investigated as a matter of urgency, given the seriousness of the allegation and the importance of adherence to proper recruitment procedures.

He also referred to the output rate for progressed learners, noting a decline from 5.5% to 4.3% in 2025, and asked whether the Department still considered the current progression policy fit for purpose, and what refinements were being considered.

In addition, he requested information on differentiated academic support models for schools with high concentrations of progressed learners, and how the effectiveness of these interventions was being measured.

Finally, Mr Louw raised concerns about persistent inequalities between quintile one to three schools and quintile four to five schools, as well as disparities between districts and provinces. He asked what targeted interventions were in place for districts performing below the national average, how their impact was monitored, and how the Department justified the continued performance gap between lower and higher quintile schools despite the policy of free education.

Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) directed his question to the Department of Basic Education.

He asked how many learners who wrote the matric examinations were unable to progress further in their studies and whether the Department is tracking where those learners are currently. He further sought clarity on whether the Department knows how many such learners there are and what their status is.

Mr Shikwambana raised serious concerns regarding the examination paper leak, stating that the root of the problem lay within the Department itself. He remarked that had it not been for an invigilator who identified and reported the matter, the credibility of the matric results currently being celebrated could have been severely compromised.

He argued that this pointed to a significant weakness in the Department’s security controls and its ability to monitor and manage its own staff, particularly given allegations that internal officials were involved in leaking examination papers to learners.

Deputy Minister’s Responses

Deputy Minister Mhaule responded to Members' questions as follows:

She began by addressing the issue of the 79% of learners who fall within quintile 1 to 3 schools and who are beneficiaries of SASSA grants. She indicated that the Department differentiates between active and inactive grant recipients. Most SASSA beneficiaries are enrolled in no-fee schools, meaning that tuition and core schooling costs are covered. She explained that, beyond this, the Department works with the Department of Social Development and external partners to assist vulnerable learners with personal necessities, such as toiletries, where capacity allows.

She acknowledged the challenge of learners who become inactive grant recipients upon turning 18 while still in Grade 12, particularly those whose birthdays fall early in the academic year. She informed the Committee that engagements have taken place between the Department of Basic Education and the Department of Social Development at both Ministerial and Director-General levels to explore possible policy adjustments. A joint team led by both Directors-General has been established to consider options, including proposals that would allow learners who are still in school to continue receiving support beyond the age threshold, subject to National Treasury approval.

On undocumented learners, she clarified that not all undocumented learners are foreign nationals. Some are South African citizens who lack documentation due to family circumstances. The Department is working with the Department of Home Affairs at the Deputy Minister level, conducting joint outreach programmes and roadshows in districts with high numbers of undocumented learners. District officials collaborate with local Home Affairs offices to assist South African learners in obtaining documentation. She noted, however, that challenges remain regarding undocumented foreign nationals, as Home Affairs operates within the constraints of immigration legislation, and higher education institutions have their own admission requirements. She indicated that this matter extends beyond the mandate of Basic Education alone.

The Deputy Minister emphasised the importance of Life Orientation as a subject that develops life skills and supports career guidance. She stated that career guidance in Grades 7 to 9 is particularly important, as subject choices made in Grade 9 determine pathways in the Further Education and Training (FET) phase. Early guidance is therefore critical to ensuring learners make informed subject selections.

Regarding school infrastructure, she stated that while some schools are well-resourced, others, particularly in rural provinces, remain in poor condition. She referred to past initiatives aimed at eradicating mud schools and acknowledged that some schools built by communities require complete rebuilding rather than renovation. She cited financial constraints and reliance on implementing agents as ongoing challenges, but indicated that provinces are being encouraged to prioritise projects efficiently to ensure more equitable conditions across schools.

On resource allocation, she stated that the national norms and standards for school funding apply equally to rural and urban schools on a per-learner basis. However, urban schools often benefit from additional private sector support due to their proximity to businesses and industries. The Department therefore directs corporate social investment partners to prioritise rural schools where possible.

Addressing the shortage of Mathematics and Science teachers, she noted that some schools have all their posts filled yet still lack teachers in critical subjects. She emphasised that principals and School Governing Bodies (SGBs) must prioritise the filling of posts according to the school’s subject needs. She indicated that leadership decisions at the school level play a critical role in ensuring appropriate staffing.

In conclusion, she acknowledged the scale of the system, with more than 25 000 schools nationwide, and stated that national leadership relies on provincial and district structures to raise issues requiring intervention. She encouraged Members of Parliament to continue raising concerns from their constituencies, as such information assists the Department in targeting oversight visits and interventions more effectively.

Umalusi’s Responses

The Chairperson of Umalusi responded to Members as follows:

He stated that the Department of Basic Education should not be expected to design the schooling and examination system primarily around university admission requirements. In his view, the purpose of the National Senior Certificate (NSC) is broader than university access. The schooling system must produce an educated citizenry, individuals capable of contributing to the economy, but also of participating meaningfully in democratic processes and civic life. Education, he emphasised, should not be reduced to a narrow pipeline into universities.

He further stressed the importance of structured engagement between the Department of Basic Education and the Department of Higher Education and Training. He noted that the Post-School Education and Training (PSET) sector includes not only universities but also TVET and FET colleges, whose performance and capacity are equally important to the country’s development.

On the issue of Mathematics, he indicated that he has undertaken a study at a university examining first-year performance in Mathematics-related subjects, including Mathematics, Economics, and Physics. Preliminary findings suggest that by the end of the first academic year, performance gaps between learners from quintile 1–3 schools and those from quintile 4–5 schools narrow significantly. Similarly, differences between learners from private and public schools diminish once students are placed in a supportive and structured university learning environment. While acknowledging that resources remain important, he stated that they are not the sole determinant of learner capability or potential.

Responding directly to the question of whether standardisation compensates for curriculum weaknesses, he answered unequivocally that it does not. Standardisation, he said, cannot remedy poor teaching or weak curriculum delivery. Umalusi is deliberate in ensuring that the standardisation process does not mask systemic weaknesses in instruction or learner development.

Regarding raw results, he noted that in the most recent examination cycle, the highest proportion of raw results in the DBE system was accepted without adjustment. For Umalusi, acceptance of raw results indicates that examination outcomes are stable, reproducible and consistent over time, meaning that an “A” in one year is comparable in standard to an “A” in another. He stated that the acceptance rate, which reached approximately 82%, reflects increasing system stability and confidence in the integrity of the assessment process.

In closing, he indicated that this was likely to be his final meeting with the Committee before the end of his term in June. He expressed appreciation to the Portfolio Committee for its rigorous oversight and indicated that although the questioning is robust, it contributes meaningfully to accountability and system strengthening. He then handed over to the Chief Executive Officer.

The Chief Executive Officer of Umalusi responded as follows:

He began by reinforcing the point that the National Senior Certificate (NSC) remains the strongest predictor of success at university. He referred to past debates comparing the NSC with the National Benchmark Tests (NBTs). He indicated that evidence continues to show that the NSC is a reliable and internationally competitive qualification.

Turning to the issue of group copying, he described it as a persistent and concerning phenomenon within the examination system. He noted that invigilators who are typically educators, are entrusted with authority during examinations, and where group copying occurs, it often indicates a failure of supervision at that level. He stated that the Department of Basic Education, as the examination authority, must ensure that this practice is decisively eradicated.

She indicated that high-risk examination centres have been identified and subjected to closer monitoring. Where educators are found to be complicit, disciplinary action must be taken and communicated clearly as a deterrent to others. In serious cases, educators should face dismissal and be reported to the South African Council for Educators (SACE) for professional misconduct. Similarly, learners found guilty of cheating must face appropriate consequences in accordance with examination regulations.

On monitoring, she stated that Umalusi calibrates its monitoring targets based on identified risks and available resources. While monitoring approximately 50% of centres may appear limited, it must be understood within budgetary and logistical constraints. He acknowledged that Provincial Education Departments also conduct monitoring; however, as both implementers and assessors of examinations, an independent quality assurance function remains essential. Umalusi therefore, maintains an external oversight role to ensure credibility.

Regarding standardisation, he reiterated that it cannot be used to compensate for deficiencies in teaching or curriculum delivery. Standardisation operates on aggregated national data and cannot correct weaknesses at the individual school level. Its purpose is to ensure consistency and fairness in examination outcomes over time, not to remedy systemic instructional shortcomings.

Finally, he noted that Umalusi archives and analyses standardisation data over time as part of its research function. This enables longitudinal studies to assess trends and the stabilisation of standards across subjects and examination cycles.

She expressed appreciation to the Chairperson and members of the Select Committee, members of the Portfolio Committee, and the respective support teams for their continued cooperation and support. She also thanked the stakeholders who participated in the meeting, as well as the Department, and confirmed that the Committee would reconvene on 3 March.

She noted that the meeting had been relatively positive and relaxed, particularly considering the good examination results discussed. However, she anticipated that the upcoming meeting on 3 March, which would focus on more challenging matters, may not be as smooth.

The meeting was adjourned.
