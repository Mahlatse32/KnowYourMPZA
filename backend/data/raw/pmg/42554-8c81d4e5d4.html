PSC Bill: Negotiating Mandates; SA National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited A/B: deliberations; Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill: briefing; with Deputy Minister

NCOP Cooperative Governance & Public Administration

10 March 2026
Chairperson: Mr TM Kaunda (ANC, KwaZulu-Natal)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

Negotiating Mandates (awaited documents)

The Select Committee on Cooperative Governance and Public Administration (Traditional Affairs, Human Settlements, Water & Sanitation) in the National Council of Provinces convened virtually to consider the tabling of negotiating mandates on the Public Service Commission Bill by permanent delegates; to receive a clause-by-clause tabling of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency (SANWRIA) SOC Limited Amendment Bill; and to receive a briefing on the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026.

Eight of the nine provinces tabled negotiating mandates in favour of the Public Service Commission Bill (B30B of 2023). The Western Cape was the only province not in support of the Bill in its current form, proposing amendments as set out in its report. Several provinces in favour attached inputs and conditions to their mandates, to which the Department would be expected to respond.

The Department of Water and Sanitation presented the clause-by-clause tabling of the SANWRIA SOC Limited Amendment Bill. The Department confirmed that one written submission had been received from the FW de Klerk Foundation, which supported the technical amendment. The Bill comprised three clauses and sought to correct a legal anomaly in the principal Act (Act 34 of 2024) by automatically listing the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency as a Schedule 2 major public entity under the Public Finance Management Act, and providing for the delisting of the Trans-Caledon Tunnel Authority. Members engaged briefly and the Chairperson noted that a special meeting had been approved for Friday to adopt the Committee's report on the Bill.

The Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026, arose from the Constitutional Court judgment of 30 May 2023, which declared the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Act of 2019 unconstitutional due to procedural defects in the public participation process. Parliament had until 29 May 2027 to finalise the new legislative framework following a court-granted extension. The Bill had undergone extensive public consultation, receiving 134 written submissions, and had been certified as constitutionally compliant and approved by Cabinet.

Members engaged extensively on the Bill, raising questions relating to the 50% women representation requirement, dispute resolution mechanisms, the status of Khoisan recognition across provinces, communal land rights, the payment of traditional leaders, tools of trade, voting rights in local councils, and access for persons with disabilities in the public participation process.

Clause-by-Clause Tabling of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited Amendment Bill

The Chairperson invited the team from the Department of Water and Sanitation to present the clause-by-clause tabling of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency (SANWRIA) SOC Limited Amendment Bill.

Adv Bronwyn Naidoo, Director: Legislative Development and Legal Support, Department of Water and Sanitation, introduced herself and indicated she would lead the discussions and present the Department's responses to comments received from the Select Committee.

Adv Naidoo began by noting that, in addressing comments received, the Department had taken into consideration relevant constitutional court cases. She referenced the Constitutional Court judgment in Matatiele Municipality and Others v The President of the Republic of South Africa, which confirmed that the Constitution contemplated that people would have a voice in the legislative organs of the state not only through elected representatives but also through participation in the law-making process. She also referenced the judgment in Merafong Demarcation Forum v The President of the Republic of South Africa, which found that being involved in the law-making process did not mean that one's views must necessarily prevail, and that there was no authority for the proposition that the views expressed by the public were binding on the legislature if they were in direct conflict with the policies of government.

She confirmed that the DWS had received one written comment from the Select Committee, that being from the FW de Klerk Foundation. The Department expressed thanks to the Foundation for its support of the technical amendment and for understanding the importance of listing the agency as a Schedule 2 major public entity under the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), a position already adopted in the principal Act (Act 34 of 2024). The Department confirmed that the Bill was a purely technical amendment correcting the wording of the principal Act to lawfully and automatically list the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency through an Act of Parliament.

The Department further confirmed that the constitutional imperatives and objectives articulated by the Foundation could be identified throughout the text of the principal Act, as well as being expressly stated in Section 5 of the Act. The principal Act also embodied a comprehensive governance chapter (Chapter 3) premised on governance codes and supporting legislative prescripts, as confirmed in Section 9, which addressed the Foundation's concerns regarding governance and transparency. Further sections providing for various governance and accountability interventions were contained in Sections 13 to 20, 27, 35, 45, and 46 of the principal Act. The DWS also confirmed that the principal Act adequately attended to financial matters, reporting, and accountability under Chapter 5, with the Minister obliged to table corporate plans and annual financial reports in Parliament under Sections 33 and 34.

Adv Naidoo then proceeded to the clause-by-clause reading of the Bill. She noted that the Bill contained a long title which sought to amend the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited Act of 2024, so as to provide for the listing of the agency as a major public entity in Schedule 2 to the PFMA; to provide for the delisting of the Trans-Caledon Tunnel Authority from Schedule 2 to the PFMA; and to provide for matters connected therewith.

Clause 1 related to the eradication of the legal anomaly embodied in Section 3(2) of the principal Act. It read as follows: Section 3 of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited Act, 2024, hereinafter referred to as the principal Act, is hereby amended by the deletion of subsection (2).

Clause 2 provided for the further resolution of the anomalous situation through the insertion of Section 52A after Section 52. It read as follows: The following section is hereby inserted in the principal Act after section 52, Amendment of Laws, Section 52A, with effect from (a) the incorporation date, the PFMA was hereby amended by the insertion, in Schedule 2, after the item with the words "SA Forestry Company Limited", of the following item: "South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited"; and (b) the date of dissolution and delisting of the Trans-Caledon Tunnel Authority in terms of Section 37(2) of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited Act, the PFMA was hereby amended by the deletion in Schedule 2 of the following item: "Trans-Caledon Tunnel Authority".

Clause 3 contained only the short title: the Act was called the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency SOC Limited Act, 2025.

Adv Naidoo thanked the Chairperson and the Committee for receiving the clause-by-clause reading of Bill 13 of 2025.

The Chairperson thanked Adv Naidoo and invited Members to raise questions and seek clarifications. He reminded the Committee that a special meeting had been approved by the Chair of Committees for Friday, where the Committee would adopt its report on the Bill, and that Members would have a further opportunity to engage.

Discussion

Mr K Ceza sought clarity from Adv Naidoo. He asked how the incorporation of the South African National Water Resources Infrastructure Agency into Schedule 2 of the PFMA would alter its financial and governance status compared to its predecessor, the Trans-Caledon Tunnel Authority, and what implications this shift would have for accountability in managing strategic water infrastructure in the country.

Mr K Mmoiemang noted that the matter was technical in nature, principally aimed at addressing the anomaly that had been missed during the enactment of the principal Act. Remarks by the Deputy Minister

The Chairperson welcomed Deputy Minister Burns-Ncamashe back to the meeting and invited him to make his introductory remarks and allow his team to present.

Deputy Minister Burns-Ncamashe greeted the Chairperson and Members and conveyed the apology of Minister Velenkosini Hlabisa, who had delegated the matter to the Deputy Minister's office in line with the delegation assigned to that office for matters concerning traditional and Khoisan leadership. He indicated that the Department appreciated the opportunity to brief the Committee on the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026, which had completed all Cabinet processes and was now serving before Parliament in accordance with Joint Rule 201.

The Deputy Minister explained that the purpose of the briefing was to provide Members with an overview of the background, objectives, and key principles of the Bill prior to its formal introduction in Parliament. He noted that the Bill arose from the Constitutional Court judgment of 30 May 2023, which declared the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Act of 2019 unconstitutional due to procedural defects relating to public participation, specifically in relation to Sections 59(1)(a) and 72(1)(a) of the Constitution. He noted a pattern of consistency in legislation relating to traditional leadership being found wanting in respect of these provisions, pointing to the Communal Land Rights Act of 2010 and the amendment of the Restitution Act as prior examples. He emphasised that the Court did not make findings on the substantive content of the Act, but rather on the process, and that the Court had suspended the declaration of invalidity, allowing the Act to remain operational while Parliament remedied the procedural defects.

The Deputy Minister stated that Parliament had subsequently been granted an extension until 29 May 2027 to finalise the new legislative framework. He noted the importance of expediting the process, as the legislation also had implications for the participation of traditional and Khoisan leaders in municipal councils in terms of Section 81 of the Municipal Structures Act, which would become relevant ahead of the upcoming local government elections. He confirmed that following the judgment, the Department developed the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026, which was published for public comment in terms of Section 154(2) of the Constitution, and that 134 written submissions had been received from stakeholders including traditional leaders, Khoisan communities, civil society organisations, and members of the public.

The Deputy Minister stated that the Bill sought to establish a comprehensive legislative framework for the recognition, governance, and functioning of traditional and Khoisan leadership institutions in South Africa, while largely retaining the core principles of the 2019 Act. He highlighted several technical adjustments and additional principles introduced, including the recognition of Khoisan kingships and queenships, the establishment of Khoisan kingship and queenship councils, a 50% representation requirement for women in traditional and Khoisan leadership councils and houses, strengthened community participation and accountability mechanisms, proportional representation within the houses of traditional and Khoisan leaders, and the requirement for royal families to submit customary laws of succession and genealogies to the Minister or relevant Premiers. He then handed over to the Director-General (DG), Mr Mashwahle Diphofa, to take the Committee through the presentation in greater detail.

Briefing on the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026

The DG thanked the Chairperson and Members and, in the interest of time, invited Dr Sadhana Singh, Chief Director: Policy and Legislation, COGTA, to take Members through the presentation.

Dr Singh then presented to the Committee in terms of Joint Rule 201.

The purpose of the presentation, Dr Singh explained, was to brief the Select Committee on the key provisions, objectives, and policy intent of the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026. The briefing was provided in accordance with Rule 201 of the Joint Rules of Parliament, which allowed Parliament to receive an overview of the objects and intent of a Bill prior to its formal introduction. The presentation was also intended to assist the Committee in preparing for the parliamentary legislative process that would follow once the Bill was formally introduced.

On the background, Dr Singh confirmed that on 30 May 2023, the Constitutional Court declared the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Act of 2019 unconstitutional and invalid, finding procedural defects in the legislative process relating to public participation. The Court suspended the declaration of invalidity for 24 months, during which time the Act remained operational. The original deadline was 29 May 2025. Recognising that the required process could not be completed within that timeframe, the Minister approached the Constitutional Court to request an extension. On 29 May 2025, the Court granted a further two-year extension, meaning Parliament now had until 29 May 2027 to address the procedural defects.

On the development of the Bill, Dr Singh noted that the Department undertook a public consultation process in accordance with Section 154(2) of the Constitution. The draft Bill was published for public comment, and 134 written submissions were received. Submissions were received from traditional leaders, Khoisan organisations, civil society, academic institutions, Chapter 9 institutions, applicants in the constitutional matter, and members of the public. All submissions were analysed and informed revisions to the Bill.

Dr Singh outlined the government approval processes the Bill had undergone. A Social Economic Impact Assessment and Quality Assurance System (SEIAS) sign-off was obtained from the Presidency. The Bill was pre-certified by the Office of the Chief State Law Advisor (OCSLA) as constitutionally compliant for submission to Cabinet. It was subsequently considered and supported by the Governance, State Capacity and Institutional Development (GSCID) FOSAD cluster. The Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill was presented at the GSCID Cabinet Committee meeting on 4 November 2025. Cabinet on 12 November 2025 noted the progress and approved the submission of the Bill to Parliament. In compliance with Rule 201 of the Joint Rules and Rule 276(1) of the Rules of the National Assembly, the Minister approved the submission of the draft Bill, together with the Memorandum on the Objects of the Bill, to the Speaker of the National Assembly and the Chairperson of the National Council of Provinces. OCSLA certified the Bill as constitutionally compliant on 23 February 2026. A Notice of Intention to Introduce the Bill was published in the Government Gazette on 26 February 2026 under Notice 3805 of 2026.

On the purpose of the Bill, Dr Singh explained that it sought to provide for the recognition of traditional and Khoisan communities and leadership positions, including procedures for the withdrawal of such recognition; the recognition, establishment, functions, and administration of kingship or queenship councils, Khoisan kingship councils, Khoisan queenship councils, principal traditional councils, traditional councils, Khoisan councils, and traditional sub-councils; the establishment, composition, and functioning of the National House of Traditional and Khoisan Leaders; the establishment of provincial houses of traditional and Khoisan leaders; and the establishment and composition of local houses of traditional and Khoisan leaders.

On the objectives of the Bill, Dr Singh indicated that the Bill sought to achieve several key legislative objectives. Firstly, it provided for the recognition of Khoisan communities, leaders, and governance structures, giving effect to constitutional recognition. Secondly, the Bill repealed the Traditional Leadership and Governance Framework Act of 2003 (Act 41 of 2003) and the National House of Traditional Leaders Act of 2009 (Act 22 of 2009) to create a single integrated legislative framework governing traditional and Khoisan leadership institutions, address legal gaps and uncertainties in existing legislation, and enhance uniformity in how matters relating to traditional and Khoisan affairs were dealt with across the country. Thirdly, the Bill introduced amendments to the Independent Commission for the Remuneration of Public Office-Bearers Act of 1997, the Remuneration of Public Office Bearers Act of 1998, and the Local Government: Municipal Structures Act of 1998.

On the main principles, Dr Singh noted that, since the Constitutional Court had made no findings on the substantive content of the 2019 Act, the main principles of that Act had largely been retained. Differences between the 2019 Act and the 2026 Bill included: the recalculation of five-year terms of office of traditional and Khoisan leadership structures from 2027 going forward; the inclusion of transitional provisions to regulate matters already implemented under the 2019 Act; and the incorporation of technical amendments that had been contained in the Traditional Affairs General Amendment Bill of 2022, which had subsequently been withdrawn from Parliament following the Constitutional Court judgment.

Additional principles introduced in the 2026 Bill, informed by public consultation, included: recognition of Khoisan kingships and queenships, and Khoisan kings and queens; establishment of Khoisan kingship and queenship councils; 50% representation of women in traditional and Khoisan leadership councils and in the houses of traditional and Khoisan leaders; strengthened community participation in traditional and Khoisan leadership councils; strengthened public accountability mechanisms for statutory structures of traditional and Khoisan leadership; proportional representation within the houses of traditional and Khoisan leaders; and the requirement that royal families submit customary laws of succession and genealogies to the Minister or relevant Premiers to assist in addressing succession disputes.

Dr Singh recommended that the Select Committee note the briefing on the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill, 2026, consider the key provisions and objectives of the Bill as part of its parliamentary legislative responsibilities, and support the continued parliamentary processing of the Bill.

Discussion

Mr Nzimande raised what he described as a matter of principle. He asked what informed the decision to prescribe a 50% gender representation requirement in the context of a bill recognising traditional and Khoisan leaders as indigenous peoples. He questioned whether this requirement was a Eurocentric idea, and whether, in genuinely recognising indigenous peoples' beliefs and ways of doing things, the imposition of a gender split was not inconsistent with indigenous practices that may have been eroded by the influence of Europe.

Dr Scheurkogel noted that since the Bill had not yet been formally tabled before the Portfolio Committee in the National Assembly, his only question and concern at this stage related to how the Department would ensure that, when the public participation process was conducted, all communities affected would genuinely be reached. He emphasised the sensitivity of the Bill and compared it to the Demarcation Bill in terms of requiring comprehensive, inclusive, and meaningful public participation. He asked for the Department's commitment on this point.

Mr Ceza indicated that the Bill would require much more indulgence during the Committee's processing. He raised the issue of 50% representation and whether that would also extend to 50% land allocation in terms of traditional and communal lands. He raised practical issues including the need to deal with violence against women in traditional communities, disabilities within traditional communities, and the backlog of unpaid traditional leaders, requesting a breakdown by province. He recalled a previous interaction with the Committee on the issue of traditional leaders not receiving remuneration from provinces. He raised the question of traditional leadership participation in social and labour plans of mining operations within their traditional communities, as a prerequisite to operational approval. He raised concerns about the tensions created at the post-operation stage where social and labour plans signed as prerequisites for development had not translated into community benefit, creating conflict between provincial political leadership and traditional communities. He also raised the need to address the inclusion of persons with disabilities, including the visually impaired, in the public participation process, citing Chief Kgoshi Mathabeng George Maila of the Maila Segolo Traditional Council, who was the Secretary of the House of Traditional Leaders in the Sekhukhune District of Limpopo, and who had raised the issue of accessibility in the law-making process for the visually impaired.

Mr Peter asked, under correction, whether Khoisan and traditional leadership communities had been informed and meaningfully consulted before the Bill was published for public comment, or whether the public hearings had proceeded without their involvement or knowledge. He noted that there was a cry from both Khoisan and traditional leadership that they had been ignored when bills affecting them were being proposed.

Responses

Mr Diphofa addressed the question on 50% women representation. He clarified that the provision arose not from Western influences but from the Constitution itself. Chapter 12 of the Constitution, which dealt with the institution of traditional leadership, made clear that the institution was recognised but subject to the Constitution. The issues of equality contained in the Bill of Rights therefore applied to traditional and Khoisan leadership institutions. The provision had not been initially included but was incorporated in response to strong submissions during public consultation, so that it could be subject to further public participation when Parliament processed the Bill. He noted that the issue of representivity within houses of traditional leaders had in fact been present before the current Bill, in that provinces had been expected to take gender representivity into account when assigning traditional leaders to serve in the national House, and this approach had contributed to better representivity.

The DG further addressed the range of issues raised by Mr Ceza. He noted that once the Bill was passed into law, it would create the foundation for addressing related questions, including the interface of recognised traditional structures with development projects such as social and labour plans in the mining environment, gender-based violence and femicide, and issues of communal land tenure. He confirmed that the Department was working with the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development on the communal land tenure question through intergovernmental coordination. He noted that the question of payment backlogs for traditional leaders did not necessarily have to await the finalisation of the Bill, and that those should be raised directly with the relevant provincial legislatures, as payments were administered by provinces. He confirmed that an instance involving a backlog of payments for headmen and headwomen in KwaZulu-Natal had previously been escalated to the national level and resolved through the intervention of the Minister working with the Minister of Finance.

Mr Diphofa further confirmed that regarding the moratorium on the recognition of headmen and headwomen, the then Minister of COGTA, Dr Dlamini-Zuma, had written to Premiers on 26 June 2019 to communicate that no moratorium was in place, and that the temporary suspension declared in a letter by the then Minister Pravin Gordhan on 3 September 2015 was intended only as a short window for provinces to conduct an audit. Where provinces continued to apply a moratorium, that was not based on any national declaration.

Mr Diphofa addressed Mr Peter's question regarding consultation with Khoisan and traditional leadership communities. He confirmed that communities had been engaged through a number of processes, including an all-inclusive Khoisan convention convened by the Minister, at which a large group of Khoisan leaders had participated. Information had also been shared through organised formations and various councils, and submissions had been actively solicited at the executive processing stage of the Bill.

On the status of recognition in the Western Cape, the DG confirmed that at the current stage there were no formally recognised traditional or Khoisan leaders in the Western Cape. However, a large volume of applications for recognition had been received by the Commission on Khoisan Matters and were being processed.

On the Communal Land Rights Act, he confirmed that the Act had been declared unconstitutional, and that issues relating to communal land tenure fell within the mandate of the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development. The Bill before the Committee dealt only with the governance and institutional framework of traditional and Khoisan leadership, not tenure, but intergovernmental coordination with the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development was ongoing.

Further Discussion

Mr Mmoiemang expressed appreciation for the presentation by the Deputy Minister and the team. He noted the significance of the Bill for the Northern Cape, where there had already been a special arrangement for Khoisan leaders to participate in the provincial House of Traditional Leaders in the absence of legislation. He welcomed the Bill being placed back on the Committee's agenda as an exciting development. He asked whether the same mechanism for dispute resolution applicable to traditional leadership would apply to Khoisan leadership, specifically whether a Premier of the relevant province would be required to appoint a commission in the event of a dispute over who was the traditional or Khoisan leader. He further asked whether a profile had been completed of Khoisan communities and leaders in the Northern Cape and other provinces. He also raised the issue of the Communal Land Rights Act (CLARA), noting that the key basis for the Constitutional Court setting it aside was not only procedural public participation defects but also the issue of dualism in terms of how a different property dispensation for communal land was going to be governed compared to the rest of the country. He asked which department, Agriculture or COGTA, was responsible for bringing a revised Act back, and whether there had been engagement between the two departments, given the implications for the security of tenure of people under traditional leadership whose tenure remained unprotected in contrast to citizens in urban areas who held certificates of ownership.

Mr Badenhorst acknowledged the important role that Khoisan leadership played in many communities. He framed his questions around Section 9(1) of the Constitution, which guaranteed that everyone was equal before the law and had the right to equal protection and benefit of the law. He asked, firstly, what uniform criteria and standards the Department applied to ensure that all traditional and Khoisan structures were recognised on an equal basis, without any group receiving preferential treatment through political or administrative discretion, and whether the recognition criteria would be publicly available and consistently applied across provinces. Secondly, he asked how the Department would ensure that the recognition and verification of traditional leadership was applied consistently across provinces and was not subject to different interpretations by different Premiers, and whether there was a national verification framework to ensure equality of treatment regardless of location.

Thirdly, he asked how the Department would ensure that governance, accountability, and transparency standards applied equally to all traditional and Khoisan councils, and what the consequences would be if a council failed to meet governance standards, and whether those consequences would apply equally to all traditional institutions. Finally, he asked what framework would be used to ensure that financial and administrative support to traditional leadership institutions was distributed fairly and equitably, and whether Parliament would receive transparent reporting on the allocation of resources to ensure equal treatment across all traditional leadership structures.

Mr Mabebo raised the issue of communal property associations (CPAs) within traditional and tribal land. He raised the matter of the Department having excluded traditional leaders when touching the Bill. He also raised the issue of departmental accounts not being audited. He noted that traditional leaders in some provinces were not being recognised as public office-bearers and did not receive support from the Department, including tools of trade and capacity building. He raised the moratorium in the Northern Cape which had resulted in headmen and headwomen not being recognised by the Department, noting that eighty percent of headmen and headwomen in the North West in particular were not yet recognised, despite performing their work.

Ms Molokomme associated herself with the point raised by Mr Mabebo regarding backlogs in payments to traditional leaders and the need for tools of trade and capacity building. She emphasised that traditional leaders needed both financial support and capacity support to be able to perform their functions effectively. She raised the issue of traditional and Khoisan leaders' participation in local councils, noting that they were represented but without voting rights, and that they had been raising concerns about being unable to make meaningful inputs and take decisions on matters affecting their communities and the lives of the people they led. She suggested that the Bill should address this by providing traditional leaders with voting rights in local councils on matters affecting their traditional environments. She asked when the Department expected to table the Bill before the National Assembly and whether it would be tabled before the 2026 local government elections, emphasising the need to speed up the process. She also asked what the state of recognition of traditional and Khoisan leaders in the Western Cape was. She requested that the Department provide the Select Committee with a consolidated report on dispute claims within traditional and Khoisan leadership.

Adv Naidoo responded to several questions. She confirmed that the Department had engaged with Khoisan communities during the consultation process, including engagements in the Northern Cape where Khoisan leaders had participated. These consultations formed part of the broader stakeholder engagement process, and the Bill had also been published for public comment through which submissions were received.

On dispute resolution, Adv Naidoo explained that the Bill provided a structured mechanism to deal with disputes relating to traditional leadership. Where disputes arose in relation to the recognition of leadership positions, the President or the relevant Premier could appoint an investigative committee to investigate the matter and provide a report before a final decision was taken. The Bill also provided for the Commission on Khoisan Matters, which considered applications and matters relating to the recognition of Khoisan communities and leaders. These mechanisms ensured that disputes were investigated objectively and resolved through a formal legal and administrative process.

On the Communal Land Rights Act and the communal property regime, Adv Naidoo confirmed that the CLARA had been declared unconstitutional, and that issues relating to communal land tenure and property regimes fell within the mandate of the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development. The Bill before the Committee dealt only with the governance and institutional framework of traditional and Khoisan leadership, but intergovernmental coordination between the Department of Traditional Affairs and the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development was maintained to ensure complementarity on communal land matters.

On equality before the law and Section 9 of the Constitution, Adv Naidoo confirmed that the Bill ensured consistency and fairness by providing uniform national criteria and procedures for the recognition of traditional and Khoisan communities and leadership positions. Recognition decisions were taken through investigations, consultations with the relevant houses of traditional leaders, and publications in the Government Gazette, promoting transparency and consistency across provinces. The Bill strengthened governance and accountability within traditional and Khoisan leadership by introducing a code of conduct for members of houses and councils, providing monitoring and reporting mechanisms, and making provision for disciplinary measures and the withdrawal of recognition where a leader violated the law, the code of conduct, or customary law.

On resources and support to traditional leadership structures, Adv Naidoo noted that the Bill recognised the need for traditional and Khoisan leadership institutions to have adequate resources to perform their statutory functions, and provided that organs of state should provide financial, administrative, infrastructure, and human resources support to traditional leaders, councils, and houses. These resources were required to be used strictly for statutory functions, ensuring accountability in the use of public resources. On tools of trade, she noted that the practical provision was largely an implementation matter administered by provincial governments, which would need to determine the appropriate support required.

Adv Naidoo confirmed that the Bill had not yet been formally introduced to Parliament, that a letter had been signed by the Minister of COGTA with the intention to introduce the Bill, and that this had been gazetted accordingly.

Ms Trisha Ramsumair, Secretary of the Commission on Khoisan Matters, added further information on the question of tools of trade and uniformity across provinces. She confirmed that the Department was currently busy with a project to develop a handbook for the provision of tools of trade for traditional and Khoisan leaders, the aim of which was to ensure uniform standards across provinces. The project had not yet been finalised but once completed it would be converted into a ministerial determination to ensure uniform provision across all provinces. She also confirmed that the Commission on Khoisan Matters was already established and was already conducting investigations into applications from members of the Khoisan community. She provided an estimate for the Northern Cape, noting that the bulk of applications received by the Commission arose from that province, and that those structures could be used to contact relevant community members and prospective applicants and leaders.

The DG added further points. On dispute resolution, he noted that the Bill's provision for the documentation of customary laws of succession and genealogies as a requirement, rather than a voluntary exercise, would create a much more reliable basis for dealing with succession disputes. The Department had been working with royal families to develop such customary laws of succession and genealogies, but in the absence of a legislative requirement this had depended on the willingness of individual royal families. Once the Bill passed into law, it would become mandatory.

He further addressed Mr Badenhorst's concerns, noting that when the Committee reached the detail of the Bill, it would find measures addressing uniformity across provinces and accountability, and that the Committee would then be able to assess their adequacy. He committed to providing provinces with a mapping of the Khoisan communities known to the Department and the Commission on Khoisan Matters. He noted that the Department historically worked on the basis of five groupings: the Nama, the Korana, the Griqua, the Cape Khoi, and the San, and that while these groupings were broadly accepted, internal distinctions were beginning to emerge, particularly within the San grouping. The Commission had received over 200 applications for recognition and was processing them, providing a useful sense of the role players, communities, and proposed leaders.

Deputy Minister Burns-Ncamashe added remarks on several points. He confirmed that the Communal Land Rights Act was a competence of the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development, but that COGTA remained a strategic role player given the implications for the functioning of the institution of traditional and Khoisan leadership. He noted that the executive had completed its consultation with traditional and Khoisan leaders, but that Parliament, when starting its own processes, needed to be intentional and deliberate in ensuring the Bill was referred to the relevant houses in line with the provisions of the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Act, so that inputs could cascade from the national house through provincial and local houses to the level of traditional councils, ensuring that affected communities had a genuine opportunity to participate. He emphasised that public hearings must be held in rural areas, not in towns, so that communities that were most affected by the legislation could meaningfully participate, and he cautioned against NGOs purporting to represent the views of rural communities without having visited those communities. He noted the significance of proportional representation as a newly introduced principle, which would ensure that representation in houses of traditional leaders was commensurate with the constituencies the institution served. On the issue of traditional leaders not being recognised as public office-bearers, he expressed doubt that such situations existed since the Constitution prescribed in Section 49 that a recognised traditional leader of whatever level was a public office-bearer, but invited members to provide practical examples so that the matter could be followed up with the relevant province.

The Deputy Minister further addressed the tools of trade, noting that a handbook had been adopted at the departmental level and that certain aspects required ministerial approval to ensure consistency, including from the Independent Commission on Remuneration. He emphasised the importance of ensuring parity among leaders of the same category, including headmen and headwomen and Khoisan leaders, so that no leader of a specific category was treated differently from other leaders of the same category. He added, on a lighter note, that if the principle of 50% gender representation could be adopted by traditional leadership, it should equally be demonstrated by democratically elected institutions in Parliament and in municipalities.

The Chairperson thanked the Department for the comprehensive responses and expressed the Committee's anticipation of receiving the information on the status of disputes. He confirmed the Committee was looking forward to the legislative process unfolding and called for public hearings to be intensive, reaching all relevant stakeholders including those in remote and rural nodes, and not only those close to larger cities and towns.

Mr Ceza raised a follow-up matter regarding the accessibility of the public participation process for persons with disabilities. He cited Chief Kgoshi Mathabeng George Maila of the Maila Segolo Traditional Council, who was the Secretary of the House of Traditional Leaders in the Sekhukhune District of Limpopo, and who had raised in July 2023 the issue of the law-making process being potentially exclusionary for the visually impaired. Mr Ceza asked whether the Traditional and Khoisan Leadership Bill made provision for the intersectional representation of visually impaired members of traditional and Khoisan communities and their access to the public participation process.

The Chairperson invited the Department to respond.

DG Diphofa responded from two perspectives. He noted that in the Department's handbook on tools of trade, there was a provision specifically addressing reasonable accommodation requirements for traditional leaders with disabilities. He cited Chief Kgoshi Maila as an example of a visually impaired traditional leader who had noted that load-shedding did not affect his ability to work given his disability and his training. However, he acknowledged Mr Ceza's point that the issue extended beyond the handbook to the practical accessibility of the public participation process itself and committed to ensuring that the Bill would be made available in an accessible format for persons with disabilities during public participation. He undertook to monitor the implementation of these provisions to close any gaps in providing tools of trade and accessible public participation materials for persons who were visually impaired.

The Chairperson added that the Bill should also be translated into relevant languages ahead of public hearings, so that the language dominant in a particular area was the language used in public hearings, rather than English being imposed in areas where it was not the dominant language. He emphasised the importance of being responsive to the language needs of all participants, including sign language, in all law-making processes.

The Chairperson thanked the Deputy Minister, Director-General, and team, and released them from the meeting.

Consideration and Adoption of Minutes

The Chairperson placed the minutes of the previous meeting before Members for consideration and adoption.

Mr Nzimande moved the adoption of the minutes. Mr Ceza seconded. The minutes were adopted.

Mr Badenhorst noted that his hand had been raised throughout the consideration of the minutes but that he had not been given an opportunity to speak before the vote was taken. He drew the attention of the Chairperson to an error on page one of the minutes, in the attendance section, where the Chairperson, Mr TM Kaunda (ANC, KwaZulu-Natal), had been listed as absent, when he had in fact been present and had chaired the meeting.

The Chairperson acknowledged the correction and noted that the amendment needed to be effected. He asked Members whether they agreed that the minutes, with that correction, now constituted a true reflection of the deliberations.

Members agreed and the minutes were adopted with the amendment.

Closing Remarks

Mr Badenhorst notified the Committee that the Joint Standing Committee on the Financial Management of Parliament had scheduled a meeting on Friday from 08:00 to 13:00, which was a double booking with the special COGTA meeting also scheduled for Friday. He indicated that he would be unable to attend the COGTA special meeting on Friday as a result.

The Chairperson noted this and indicated the Committee would consider it.

Mr Mmoiemang noted that, for the purpose of accommodating Members, it would be important to be mindful of the convention that Fridays were generally scheduled for joint committee meetings and suggested that the time of the special meeting on Friday be considered to avoid conflicts of this nature in future.

The Chairperson agreed to revert to Members with the team and the Committee Whip on the scheduling matter. He thanked all Members and participants for their attendance and engagement, noting that the meeting had been very fruitful, particularly in respect of the engagement on traditional and Khoisan leadership.

The meeting was adjourned.
