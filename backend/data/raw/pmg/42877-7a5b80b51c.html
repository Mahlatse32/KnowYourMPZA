National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC); Perishable Products Export Control Board (PPECB) 2026/27 Annual Performance Plans (APPs); with Minister

Agriculture

05 May 2026
Chairperson: Ms D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Annual Performance Plan (APP) of Government Departments & Entities 2026/27

The National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC) goal is to strengthen South Africa’s agricultural marketing system through policy advisory services, market research, regulatory oversight, and farmer support initiatives. Its APP is aligned with national development priorities, including inclusive economic growth, poverty reduction, food security, and improved governance. It highlighted significant challenges such as funding constraints, staff retention issues, and limited stakeholder awareness of the organisation’s mandate. NAMC proposed several interventions focused on improving resource mobilisation, strengthening partnerships, enhancing communication, and building institutional capacity to ensure the effective delivery of its mandate and long-term sustainability.

The Perishable Products Export Control Board (PPECB) aim is to improve South Africa’s perishable export sector through improved food safety, digital transformation, operational efficiency, and industry development. Challenges include slow economic growth, unemployment, inflation, port and logistics constraints, weather risks, and increasing global competition. It noted continuous improvement in customer service, enhancing digital systems, reducing paper-based processes, supporting black smallholder farmers, and strengthening food safety compliance through audits and technical competency programmes. It reported that 1200 farmers had been trained in 2025/26 and that digital temperature monitoring systems were being introduced to improve export logistics. PPECB also planned to invest heavily in statutory operations and information technology while using data and AI tools to improve performance and decision-making.

Committee members asked if the entities were doing enough to advance meaningful transformation, particularly for black farmers, women, youth, and persons with disabilities. They requested the need for clearer demographic reporting. Concerns were raised about the lack of measurable indicators in the APPs for black producer ownership, participation, income, and market share. Several members argued that existing targets were too low given the scale of need, highlighting that so many small-scale farmers existed while relatively small numbers were being reached through training and development programmes. Market access emerged as a central issue as emerging farmers often lacked the resources, infrastructure, and support necessary to compete in export and domestic markets.

NAMC and PPECB acknowledged many of the concerns and emphasised budgetary and capacity constraints. They outlined ongoing efforts to support smallholder farmers through training, export readiness programmes, and transformation initiatives, while also noting plans to improve reporting and alignment with broader government strategies such as greenhouse gas emission plans.

The Chairperson expressed frustration with the Department’s communication failure about board appointments, and the limited progress in employing 10 000 extension officers despite this commitment made by the President. Minister John Steenhuisen acknowledged the budget limitations affecting Department staffing and committed to providing updates on board appointments.

Meeting report

During introductions, Mr Anton Kruger, PPECB Board Chairperson, introduced himself as the recently appointed new chair and noted that the PPECB had appointed 10 new board members.

The Chairperson stated that she was blindsided by the appointment of the new chair. She complained that the Minister and his office did not introduce the new chair to the Committee.

National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC) 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan

Dr Simphiwe Ngqangweni, NAMC CEO, and Ms Lebogang Dire, NAMC CFO, presented the APP and budget. The key points included:

The presentation outlined the mandate, strategic priorities, outcomes, budget allocation, human capital, and challenges facing NAMC.

NAMC derives its mandate from the Marketing of Agricultural Products (MAP) Act No. 47 of 1996.

Its responsibilities include:

  • Investigating and recommending statutory and regulatory measures to the Minister.
  • Monitoring and reviewing implementation of these measures.
  • Advising the Minister on agricultural marketing policy, coordination with national development priorities, and international agricultural trends.

The four main objectives of the Act:

  • Increasing market access for all participants.
  • Promoting efficient agricultural marketing systems.
  • Optimising export earnings from agricultural products.
  • Enhancing the viability of the agricultural sector.

Core Functions

NAMC operates through three main divisions:

- Agricultural Regulatory Measures

  • Oversees statutory measures such as levies, registrations, records and returns.
  • Oversees 11 agricultural industry trusts.

- Markets and Economic Research Centre (MERC)

  • Conducts research and market analysis.
  • Advises government on agricultural marketing policy and trends.

- Agribusiness Development

  • Identifies market opportunities for smallholder farmers.
  • Facilitates market access projects and farmer integration into markets.

Contribution to Government Priorities

Priority 1: Inclusive Growth and Job Creation

NAMC contributes by:

  • Supporting economic growth and employment.
  • Promoting integrated economic and trade strategies.
  • Supporting implementation of the African Continental Free Trade Area to expand exports.
  • Strengthening trade relations and economic diplomacy.

Priority 2: Reduce Poverty and Cost of Living

  • Supporting skills development through vocational and technical training.
  • Strengthening partnerships with the private sector to create workplace learning opportunities for graduates and artisans.

Priority 3: Build a Capable and Ethical State

  • Supporting reforms aimed at improving governance and performance of public entities.
  • Supporting professionalisation of the public sector.
  • Improving planning, policy coordination, and oversight systems.

Outcomes and Performance Indicators

Programme 1: Institutional Effectiveness

Outcome: NAMC delivers on its mandate and core functions.

Focus areas:

  • Corporate governance,
  • Effective business processes,
  • Resource support services.

Target: Maintaining an unqualified audit outcome across the MTEF period.

Programme 2: Viable and Efficient Agricultural Sector

Purpose: Provide research and policy advisory services.

Key targets included:

Investigating and recommending 80% of statutory measure applications to the Minister.

Producing:

  • Status reports on statutory measures,
  • Agricultural industry trust reports,
  • Transformation reports,
  • Policy advisory reports,
  • Market intelligence reports,
  • Trade probe reports,
  • Supply and demand estimates,
  • Food-input cost policy reports,
  • Value chain analysis reports,
  • Fresh Produce Market Intelligence Reports.

Programme 3: Enhanced Market Access

Purpose: Increase market access for farmers and agribusinesses.

Targets included:

  • Linking 120 smallholder farmers annually to market opportunities.
  • Facilitating two production scheme designs annually.

Organisational Structure

The organisation has:

  • 62 approved posts,
  • 43 filled positions,
  • 19 vacancies projected for 2026.

The Markets and Economic Research Centre has the largest staffing component.

The structure includes governance, finance, research, regulatory measures, agribusiness development, communication, legal services, internal audit, and compliance functions.

Budget Allocation

  • Total projected revenue and expenditure for 2026/27: 9 million.
  • Main income source: Government transfers.
  • Largest expenditure category: Compensation of employees.

Programme Allocations for 2026/27

  • Programme 1: Administration: R30.0 million (56%)
  • Programme 2: Market and Economic Research Centre: R13.2 million (24%)
  • Agricultural Regulatory Measures: R6.8 million (13%)
  • Programme 3: Agribusiness Development: R3.9 million (7%)

Challenges Identified

The presentation identified several operational and institutional challenges:

  • Financial resource constraints.
  • Uncompetitive salary scales.
  • High staff turnover.
  • Poor understanding of NAMC mandate by stakeholders.
  • Inadequate communication and visibility.
  • Loss of stakeholder rapport.
  • Difficult economic conditions limiting funding opportunities.

Proposed Solutions

Suggested interventions included:

  • Diversifying funding and strengthening partnerships.
  • Reviewing remuneration and introducing non-financial incentives.
  • Improving career development and succession planning.
  • Increasing stakeholder engagement and outreach.
  • Strengthening communication and visibility strategies.
  • Exploring innovative funding models.

Perishable Products Export Control Board 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan

Mr Lucien Jansen, PPECB CEO and Ms Aneesa Moosa-Camroodien, PPECB CFO, presented.

The main focus areas include:

  • Improving export efficiency
  • Digital transformation
  • Food safety
  • Economic sustainability
  • Supporting smallholder farmers
  • Strengthening South Africa’s export competitiveness

Key Economic & Industry Context

South African operating environment

  • Economic growth expected between 2% – 1.6%
  • Inflation projected at 5%
  • Unemployment remains high at 4%
  • External pressures include: weather conditions, port and logistics challenges and global market instability

Important considerations affecting PPECB

  • Ministerial priorities
  • Port logistics constraints
  • Economic growth and job creation
  • Digital skills development
  • Border Management Authority requirements

Global Trends Identified

PPECB highlighted several global trends affecting exports:

  • Increasing export volumes
  • Highly competitive markets
  • Rise of misinformation and information overload
  • Tariffs and protectionism
  • Need for integrated digital systems
  • Changing consumer demands

South African Fruit Export Markets (2025)

Main export destinations

  • European Union:37%
  • Middle East:16%
  • Asia:15%
  • United Kingdom:11%
  • Russia:9%
  • North America:7%
  • Africa:4%

Key takeaway

  • Europe remains the dominant export market.
  • PPECB must maintain strong compliance and quality standards to protect market access

Strategic Focus Areas

PPECB aims to operate both as a regulator and an enabler of trade and industry growth

Main focus priorities

  • Improve customer experience
  • Provide timely and relevant information
  • Increase transformation, innovation and digitalisation
  • Improve operational efficiency and reduce costs
  • Support sustainability of black smallholder farmers and suppliers

Major Performance Targets for 2026/2027

Corporate Services:

  • 85% procurement from B-BBEE suppliers
  • Strategic supplier performance target: 80%
  • Customer satisfaction target: 80%
  • R1.6 million allocated to Enterprise Supplier Development

Operational Services:

  • Reduce printed export certificates by 50%
  • 30% of container loading processed via TITAN 2.0®
  • 30% of inspected perishable products processed digitally via TITAN 2.0®
  • 17 customers onboarded onto PPECB Intellex platform

Food Safety:

  • Expand MRL analyte scope to 80%
  • Conduct 1,220 food safety audits
  • Ensure 92% of inspectors and assessors are technically competent

Transformation & Development:

  • Place 6 graduates with disabilities
  • Train 25 smallholder farmers in business skills
  • Graduate 50 students annually through the Agri Export Technologist Learnership Programme (AETP)

Board Strategic Priorities

The Board identified five strategic priorities:

  • Growing government confidence
  • Organisational evolution
  • Strengthening PPECB’s value proposition
  • Digitisation
  • Using data and AI tools to measure progress

Strategic Projects

The PPECB Laboratory project is a key strategic initiative to strengthen food safety and export compliance capabilities.

Budget Allocation (2026/27) R859 million

Allocation breakdown:

  • Statutory Operations: 61%
  • Information Technology: 21%
  • Corporate Services: 10%
  • Food Safety: 7%
  • Development: 2%

Key takeaways

  • Most funding is directed toward core inspection and cold-chain export services.
  • Strong investment in IT reflects PPECB’s digital transformation strategy.

Key Initiatives for 2026/2027

  • Succession planning
  • Business intelligence improvement
  • Cost reduction initiatives
  • Systems integration
  • Strengthening organisational culture

PPECB is positioning itself as modernised, digitally enabled and globally competitive export regulator

Core priorities:

  • Improve operational efficiency
  • Strengthen food safety systems
  • Enhance digital capabilities
  • Support transformation and inclusion
  • Maintain South Africa’s reputation in global perishable exports

Discussion

Mr Z Mthethwa (MKP) noted that both entities played an auxiliary role in the sector at the tail end of the production of agricultural products. Both were a tertiary function in terms of their role. There were two streams of agriculture which were established farms and emerging farms. He asked that the two entities be more explicit about the difference between the two when presenting in the future.

He said that it was important that the demographics were stated clearly as both played an important role in transformation in the sector. The Committee could be misled without these demographics. He asked if the outcomes and indicators took into consideration the transformation agenda.

He argued that emerging farmers were battling to receive market access. He questioned how much effort was going into assisting with market access. He noted that emerging farmers did not have the disposable income for marketing and market access.

He noted that geopolitics affected the sector. The agricultural sector was aimed at international markets and he questioned how the sector adjusted when routes like the Strait of Hormuz were closed. He argued that the sector needed to prioritise the South African market.

He stated that the agricultural sector was allowed to be private sector-driven; however, this was at the expense of transformation. Government had a duty to balance private sector and state funding in the sector to assist emerging farmers and transformation.

Ms S Davids (ANC) noted that the NAMC APP spoke about market access and transformation; however, it did not sufficiently measure black farmer participation, market share, or food affordability. She asked what outcome indicators NAMC would introduce to measure real transformation.

To PPECB, she noted that the APP spoke to market access, security of exports, and support for black producers. She argued that transformation was not being measured through ownership, income, market share, or participation. She asked what indicators PPECB would introduce to measure if black producers were gaining greater access to this.

Ms N Ndalane (ANC) was concerned that the PPECB indicators were only reported annually and asked if there could be more regular reporting.

She questioned why NAMC had not increased its targets and how it measured the success of its targets and indicators.

Dr N Nkabane (ANC) asked NAMC to expand on its resource mobilisation strategy.

She noted that the NAMC APP emphasised inclusion but that statistics on women, youth, and persons with disabilities were not applied. She asked how they would close this gap.

She questioned how PPECB would align with the greenhouse gas emission plan that the department was working on.

She asked how PPECB measured outcomes and requested that they report on black exporter participation and market access for emerging farmers.

Mr A Mngxitama (MKP) had an issue with how targets were determined. There were approximately 2.5 million small-scale farmers and he questioned if the target took this number into account.

He drew attention to NAMC’s commitment to engage schools, but there was no target on how many learners would be reached through the programme.

Mr C Smit (DA) brought up the Committee’s site visit to the crate export terminal. He asked if PPECB had implemented the temperature monitoring system that they said they were going to introduce. He asked for the progress on setting up a loading space that would not be affected by weather interference. He asked if the Committee could assist with that process.

He requested NAMC speak to how they planned to open up markets to small-scale farmers.

Mr K Madlala (MKP) referred to slide 21 and asked for a demographic breakdown of small-scale farmers.

NAMC response

Dr Ngqangweni acknowledged the reality of the two agricultures, namely established and emerging farms. When it came to reporting on the two, he recognised that NAMC could do a better job. He noted that NAMC planned to target black, smaller-holding farmers. He conceded that the 120 farmers that NAMC assisted each year were a small number. This was due to budget constraints. He highlighted that the number had increased from 80 in previous years. NAMC would like to increase that number as they built more resources.

He referred to the questions on measurements of smallholder farmers and said that they were working as part of the master plan, which brought together the government, the agricultural sector, and the private sector. NAMC was part of round tables where the topic of transformation was discussed. The master plan aimed to increase black farmer ownership to 20% by 2030.

He noted that NAMC had 14 SETA graduates in their graduate programme for the year and would assist in finding them opportunities in the sector.

Mr Mngxitama expressed disappointment that NAMC was only assisting 14 graduates and that this was not sufficient to make an impact on youth unemployment rates.

Dr Ngqangweni explained that NAMC was a small entity and could not assist as many graduates as they would like. The number of graduates was dependent on the funding provided by AgriSETA.

PPEBC response

On global exports, Mr Jansen explained that when exports could not make it to the intended market, they were rechannelled to other markets if they were already on the water. If they were still in the country, the farmer had to reapply to have the goods exported to a new market, and lastly, if they could not be exported, they would go into the local market.

He noted that PPECB provided training to smallholder farmers so that they understood how to be export competent and assisted in finding the best markets for them. The previous year they had trained 1200 farmers.

Mr Jansen explained that PPECB programmes were annual but that they reported and presented the progress of the programmes to Parliament every quarter.

He said that PPECB’s strategy was still a draft strategy and he assured the Committee that PPECB would align it with the Department’s greenhouse strategy.

Mr Jansen acknowledged that the greatest barrier for smallholder farms was infrastructure, which affected their ability to get accredited to export produce. He explained that the targets for the smallholder farmers development programme were mainly determined by the names and numbers provided to PPECB by the nine provinces on how many farmers needed training.

On temperature monitoring, PPECB had previously manually monitored the temperatures. They were aiming to digitally monitor container temperatures by the end of the first quarter. They were speaking to Transnet about having prior warning for when they would open and close hatches to reduce the waiting time when loading.

Further discussion

Ms L Arries (EFF) said that PPECB received an 80% satisfaction rate from customers and asked how they addressed dissatisfaction and planned to improve that satisfaction rate.

She argued that assisting only 25 smallholder farmers was low. She asked how that number aligned with the national transformation strategy and if R1.6 million was sufficient to drive meaningful change.

Ms Arries asked if their B-BBEE procurement reflected real economic empowerment or if it was only to ensure compliance.

She questioned who was benefiting the most from the NAMC intervention.

Ms Arries asked how NAMC addressed structural inequality in the agricultural market. Could it provide examples of success stories from NAMC’s intervention?

PPEBC response

Mr Jansen noted that 80% was reasonable for a customer service entity according to their research. They followed up with clients when there were complaints so that they could address them. He explained that the biggest complaint was inspectors arriving late.

He emphasised that they used 85% B-BBEE suppliers, and that this was the target set by PPECB.

Mr Jansen explained that smallholder farmer development was based on the numbers they received from the provinces, but that they would be updating that number as more farmers were identified.

Ms Aneesa Moosa-Camroodien addressed smallholder expenditure and noted that, in total, R13 million was budgeted, which was 2% of the total transformation budget. She broke that down, explaining that R2.3 million was for smallholder development, R5.3 million was for supplier development transformation, and R1.6 million was an additional amount.

Dr Ngqangweni noted that it was difficult to track who benefited the most from NAMC. He said that the organisation could look at conducting a study to get a better picture of this.

He noted that R1.2 billion was collected through levies and was spent on research, quality control, market development, and other services. R200 million of that was earmarked for transformation. He said that he could send the Committee documents with more information.

Department of Agriculture response

Mr Dipepeneneng Serage, Deputy Director-General: Agricultural Production, Biosecurity, and Natural Resources Management, suggested that the department present a value chain to the Committee to show the role of each entity and how they worked together holistically.

Mr Serage explained that PPECB had two mandates, the first being logistics and cold chain under the Department of Transport, which was mandated in 1926. The second mandate, which led to NAMC being moved to the Department of Agriculture, was quality control of products for export.

Ms Bongiswa Matoti, Acting DDG: Agricultural Research and Regulatory Services, responded about the number of trainees being reached. She noted that 10% of funding went towards training. In the previous year, 14 000 farmers were trained, of whom 3 000 were youth. The Department had conducted a skills audit. To address unemployment, the Department's target was to place 1 000 unemployed youth on farms.

Chairperson's comments

The Chairperson expressed her disappointment about the Department's addressing of unemployed youth. The President had announced the appointment of 10 000 extension officers; however, only 3 010 were appointed. She asked for an update on the budget so the Committee could respond to questions by their constituencies about further appointments.

She noted that the Committee was not formally told about the appointment of the NAMC Board. She expressed frustration that the PPECB Board Chairperson was not formally introduced to the Committee before he addressed the Committee. It was not acceptable that the Committee was not informed.

She asked for an update on the Agricultural Research Council (ARC) Board, whose term would end on 30 June 2026. She hoped that their appointment would not be delayed.

She highlighted the court case between the Department of Agriculture and the South African Veterinary Council (SAVC) on the appointment of its board. She questioned who would be liable for the costs of that case, as the Department was found liable.

She requested an update on the suspension of Ms Kwena Komape, Deputy Director-General: Economic Development, Trade and Marketing and what the investigation outcome had been. She stressed the need for transparency in these processes and the need for consequence management.

She was frustrated with the Department’s lack of timely communication about events. The Committee seemed to be an afterthought. She explained that the Committee could not attend events on a Tuesday as it was the Committee’s meeting day. The Department was aware of its schedule.

Minister’s response

Mr John Steenhuisen, Minister of Agriculture, explained that the Deputy Director-General’s office was dealing with Ms Komape’s case and they could provide further information to the Committee.

The Minister said that the Department had several court cases and that legal proceedings were complicated. He noted that they had been able to bring on a few good advocates to assist the Department.

On extension officers, he said that the appointment of extension officers was dependent on the budget. If they hired 10 000 extension officers, it would take money away from other programmes. He noted that some of the resources were going towards hiring 328 animal health technicians to assist with foot-and-mouth disease.

He agreed to try not to schedule events on Tuesdays. He asked for clarity if the Committee expected his attendance at all Committee meetings. The Department considered the Committee to be important and he would try to provide information timeously. He committed to sending the Committee an updated timeline on the various board appointments.

The Chairperson noted that she would always go through the Minister for information, as the Committee was an oversight mechanism for the Minister.

She agreed that the extension officers could not be hired in one go as the budget did not allow for that. She stressed the need for communication on the timeline and quantity of extension officer appointments to the public.

She requested that the Deputy Director-General present the value chain to the Committee and the Committee secretary would send possible dates.

She commended the Minister for his attendance at Committee meetings.

Committee programme

The Chairperson went through the Committee’s programme for the second term. She noted that the international study tour had been postponed until January 2027.

Mr Smit asked for further details on the Western Cape oversight visit.

The Chairperson clarified that the Western Cape visit would focus on foot-and-mouth disease.

Ms Arries argued that the Committee should go to the Northern Cape as their numbers were low, and she wanted to know if the reported numbers were a true reflection of the situation in the province.

The Chairperson explained that the Western Cape was chosen because they were able to contain the disease and that the visit fell during a three-day week, so there was no time to travel.

The Committee programme was adopted.

The meeting was adjourned.
