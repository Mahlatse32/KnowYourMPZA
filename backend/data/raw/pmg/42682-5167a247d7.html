Allegations of irregularities in DBE appointment processes; with Minister

Basic Education

24 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Portfolio Committee met to investigate the allegations of political interference in appointments at the Department of Basic Education (DBE). The Committee was aghast that the requested documents were not provided to it. The Minister and Department apologised and promised to provide those that were not confidential. Some Members were of the opinion that it was a deliberate tactic to hamper the Committee’s investigation.

The Committee was concerned about ministerial interference in the recruitment process for the DDG: Curriculum post. In May 2025 the panel responsible for this could not come to agreement on the two finalists. The Minister opted to break this deadlock by recommending the second highest scorer since the panel could not agree. This raised the ire of the Department of Public Service and Administration (DPSA), whose authority is needed to sign off on new DDG positions. The DBE and DPSA have since been deadlocked for the better part of a year over this.

The Chief Director: Communications post was also not free from controversy. It had come to light that the previous Chief of Staff to the Minister and the current Advisor to the Minister, had rerouted two additional CVs outside the normal HR channels directly into the inbox of the HR Director. The reason this caused alarm is the Minister had already had time to view the shortlist of those who qualified versus those who did not as this was sent to her desk. This undermined the statement of the Minister that her office followed all HR protocols. However, it remains unclear if the Advisor or previous Chief of Staff were acting in their own interests or if they had received instruction from the Minister, who denied such instruction taking place. The Committee resolved to secure the attendance of the previous Chief of Staff to clarify this.

The extent of executive authority conveyed onto the Minister by the Public Service Act and its Regulations was also discussed in detail.

After an exhaustive deliberation across four and a half hours, the Chair recommended that that a forensic investigation would be appropriate. Legal advice would be sought by the Committee on how best to proceed. The documents in the Committee’s letter of request had to be provided by the Department. Should any item not be provided, there needed to be an accompanying letter explaining why that document could not be handed over.

Meeting report

The Committee adopted its report on DBE Quarter 3 2025/26 Performance and 10 March 2026 minutes. In moving for adoption of the Second Term Committee Programme, the Chair said that members had until 27 March to submit proposed changes.

After it was noted that the Minister would be stepping out briefly for 15 minutes at 10:00, Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) asked why she was leaving and where she would be going.

The Minister responded that she had to quickly open proceedings in the NCOP before returning. She clarified that this is because the NCOP does not allow Deputy Ministers to open proceedings on behalf of Ministers so she will have to be there in person before returning to this Committee.

DDG: Curriculum recruitment process

Missing Documents; DPSA Stalemate; Internal DBE Compliance

The Chair noted with concern that the various documents requested by the Portfolio Committee had not been provided by DBE. This disempowers the Committee and she asked the Department to enlighten the Committee why no reasons have been given for the absence of these documents. The Department cannot absolve itself of responsibility by relying on pending processes currently before Cabinet.

The Chair stated that these documents were requested as part of the investigation into whether there has been undue political influence by the Minister as it relates to the Department’s recruitment procedure and the applications received. The Minister’s denials of this will be interrogated by this Committee. She asked that the Minister confirm if all parties involved are at the meeting and why the Committee has not been provided documents.

The Minister responded by asking for clarity as to which specific documents have not been provided, especially in light of the current recruitment processes being ongoing – meaning certain documents are, by their nature, restricted. The only missing person is the previous Chief Director for Communications, who has resumed private employ, hence he must be summoned via subpoena as per the rules.

The Chair asked the Minister to clarify what she meant by her answer on the documents. Were they not provided because the recruitment process is currently live and ongoing or because the request did not properly specify them?

The Minister replied that she is asking only for clarification on which specific documents are needed so that she may provide them. These documents are of a sensitive nature as they contain personal information (such as in CVs) and so they cannot be handed out without proper consideration of that sensitive information. The credibility of the recruitment process depends on this confidentiality.

The Chair lamented that the Committee’s request for documents was already narrow in scope and did not relate to items of a personal nature – yet despite being specific in asking for documents, the Minister and the Department have provided zero documents, only a barebones presentation. She admonished the Department for not communicating with the Committee about documents not to be included, noting that the approach instead seems to have been to ignore the request entirely, despite two written requests by the Committee.

The Minister noted the letters dated 12 March, whose subject was: Appointment of the DDG: Curriculum and the role-players involved; organisational structure, recruitment framework, applications shortlists, and records of submissions made to the Minister all formed part of the request. She apologised for the lack of documents, noting that the request was not properly canvassed against the backdrop of the confidential nature of the recruitment process itself.

The Chair stated that the documents requested would not have raised a confidentiality issue because they related only to the recruitment process itself, and if this framework properly guarded against political interference. The core question remains if the framework itself is open to tampering by political parties. She said that the Committee has been disadvantaged by this lack of documents as they have not been able to fully appraise themselves of the situation.

DBE Director-General Mr Hubert Mweli responded to the Chair that he unfortunately does not have all the information at his immediate disposal, despite his reading of the 12 March letter. He offered that in the meeting for preparation to appear before this Committee the agreement had been that all they needed to provide was a presentation.

The Chair was not satisfied with this response and asked if there had been a specific agreement during this preparatory meeting that the Department would attempt to obfuscate the Committee’s investigation by not providing documents. She asked this because she was concerned about the general pattern of non-compliance with discovery requests made to the Department, and that it pointed to a disregard of the authority of the Committee.

Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) raised concerns about the Department’s lack of compliance with the requests made by the Committee. He asked for clarity about the preparatory meeting held by the Department and the Minister before appearing before the Committee. He wanted to know who was present in that meeting, and what agreements were made in light of the failure to submit the requested documents. Due to the lack of documents the only option was to personally ask the involved parties one-by-one about the recruitment process, hampering the Committee’s efficiency and offering no assistance. The Department had, by its own hand, disempowered the fact-finding mission of the Committee, and that the role-players present must now be examined person-by-person due to the complete lack of documents.

The DG Mr Mweli responded that it is common practice for Department staff to hold preparation meetings and that there was nothing malicious about it. The process involves doing research, compiling objectives and challenges, and then presenting these to the Minister and Deputy Minister to prepare them with relevant information. This is also done to ensure that the presentation is in line with the brief requested by the Committee. As to who was involved in this process, Mr Khunou was not, whereas Ms Khoza and Ms Le Roux were a part of it.

[Minister departed for the NCOP at around 30 minutes into the meeting]

Mr D Louw (ANC) was concerned about the missing documents and the flouting by the Department of the Committee's requests. The Committee’s power to request documents is a privilege granted to Cabinet under the rule of law. This oversight function depends on the disclosure of these specific documents as it helps guide the Committee in their line of questioning.

Mr Louw believed that the Department’s error was not deliberate and asked when the outstanding documents will be provided to the Committee. The Department’s presentation, yet to be presented, did not carry evidentiary weight because it is hamstrung by the lack of supportive evidence in the form of documents.

Mr Louw said the key question remains why the DBE preparation meeting concluded that documents should not be provided to the Committee. He could not accept the apologies offered by the Minister and the Department, and that he must insist that the outstanding documents be provided as soon as possible.

DG Mweli clarified that there had been two separate meetings to properly prepare. Both were led by the Minister; the first resulted in the presentation sent to the Committee, and the second version involved incorporating ministerial feedback on that presentation. According to him, the Minister has final approval of the presentation, as well as the attachment of any documents. The only instruction had been to prepare a presentation, not to prepare any associated documents. Outstanding documents could be provided upon specific request.

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) was not satisfied with the Department’s explanations about compliance with protocols and procedures, which he characterised as blame-shifting. He pointed to sections 92, 100, 42 and 55 of the Public Service Act 103 of 1994 which provides ministers with their mandate as well as enforcement mechanisms for oversight bodies such as the Committee to ensure that there is compliance.

DG Mweli said that the DBE had followed the public recruitment protocol laid down by DPSA. This involves prospective candidates for high level government positions being interviewed by a panel, which in turn is chosen by the ministerial teams of both departments. This resulted in a shortlist of candidates, who then had each of their presentations considered by that panel. Ms Khoza and Ms Le Roux were a part this process whereas the CFO, Mr Khunou, was not.

The panel gave each applicant an independent score of between one and five. This resulted in a list of applicants ranked by this score, also numbered rank one, rank two, rank three, etc. However, despite this system, the panel could not reach agreement on who to recommend to Cabinet for the appointment. Instead, two of the applicants were recommended – both rank one and rank two.

DG Mweli said that after the panel reported this stalemate and recommended the two names, the Minister wrote to the staff concerned about her potential choice. However, the DPSA Minister refused to sign off on the choice, stating that a single choice must be recommended by the panel because Cabinet members are not responsible for choosing between applicants. The DG agreed with DPSA's contention, but despite this there has not been a resolution by the panel. However, continued communication between the DBE and the DPSA has not amounted to anything. After seeking legal advice from the State Attorney’s Office, it is trite and correct law that the DBE is within its rights to proceed with its recommendation, and that the DPSA's objections held no legal grounds. The positions these recommendations relate to are that of DDG: Curriculum and the post of DDG: Teaching. On for DDG: Teaching position the panel is unanimous, but on the position of DDG: Curriculum the panel is split between two candidates.

DBE CFO Mr Patrick Khunou added that the reason the DPSA has been unwilling to engage in the recruitment proceedings for the DDG: Curriculum post is because, according to the DPSA, the DBE lacks the proper ‘organisational framework’. However, upon inspecting its records, DBE found that the DPSA had, in fact, approved that same organisational structure in 2010. The DPSA objections lose further merit in light of the reality that the post in question has been vacant only since 2023, and yet operated for many years under an organisational structure approved by the DPSA. Therefore, the DPSA argument holds no water. If the DPSA continues to be an obstacle, the Minister – under Public Service Regulation 2A – may intervene accordingly by approving the organisation’s recruitment structure, which happened prior to 2010 and remains in effect.

Mr L Komane (EFF) asked the Minister why disclosure of personal details of the recruitment applicants to the Committee is seen as a breach of confidentiality when the Committee itself may hold in camera proceedings to protect such details. Members are often trusted with confidential information and the Minister’s explanation about the missing documents is unsatisfactory.

Mr Komane admonished the Minister for failing to oblige the Committee’s requests for documents. This conduct is not befitting the office of the Minister. There is clearly a breakdown in communication within her office. The behaviour borders on childish. Her Department will be facing additional scrutiny on his behalf, and there will be follow-up requests if any questions posed today remain unanswered.

Since the Minister was absent at the time of this question it was not responded to.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) called for an order asking Mr Komane to refrain from calling the Minister’s conduct ‘childish’, and to refrain from using unnecessary metaphors in his questioning. The casting of ad hominem aspersions achieves no purpose and is unparliamentary in nature. Intimidation of parties is no laughing matter.

Mr Shikwambana stated that Ms Jordaan had misinterpreted Mr Komane’s remarks, and that her disagreement is not suitable grounds for, and nor does it justify, an order. Members may express their thoughts on the issue at hand and may not be policed in their approach to questioning.

After asking members to settle down, the Chair asked Ms Jordaan to continue.

Ms Jordaan continued that the Minister has already covered the issue of missing documents and that her apology and promise to attach in future, prior to upcoming presentations, a letter stating which documents cannot be produced. This would enable the Committee to investigate and conclude if such documents are, in fact, privileged or not. She proposed that the Committee close on the issue of the missing documents and proceed to the interrogation of the actual recruitment procedure.

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) questioned Ms Jordaan’s proposal to move forward despite the lack of documents, contending that it goes against the spirit of oversight inherent to these meetings. Any alleged party lines should not blur the cross-examination of ministers. The Committee should continue the discussion into where, why, and how the specific documents were not produced. She expressed particular concern for the complete lack of documents provided by the Department for this meeting.

The Chair stated that absence of documents does pose an obstacle for the meeting’s agenda. To address this, she proposed that the personnel instead be examined viva voce, by answering questions put to them verbally.

Deputy Minister Dr Reginah Mhaule offered assistance to the Committee in the direction of their line of questions because the Committee is starting their inquiry at a high level of the organisational workflow. She suggested that questions on advertisements and recruitment are best suited to the DG Mr Hubert Mweli and the DDG/CFO Mr Patrick Khunou. More granular HR concerns should be directed to the Chief Director of Human Resources Ms Sibusiso Khoza, assisted by her colleague Deputy Director of HR Ms Belinda le Roux.

The Chair reminded the Deputy Minister that the reason the Committee has to use a top-down approach is because of the absence of those documents which would have allowed a narrower approach. She lamented the difficulty of the meeting ahead, purely because of the murky nature of the recruitment processes without said documents to shed light on the inner workings thereof.

DDG: Curriculum Split Panel; Cadre Employment; Executive Authority

The Chair narrowed her questioning to the panel split on the two candidates for DDG: Curriculum. She asked if the Minister recommended the person with the highest score (applicant occupying rank one) or if she chose the other applicant (the person occupying rank two).

DG Mweli responded that the Minister chose the applicant who had scored the second highest. On this scoring, Mr Mweli clarified that it is a collated score – meaning each panel member gives the applicant a rating, then those ratings are added up, and that total is their ‘score’. Despite this list the panel may still discuss, as long as any deviation from this selection ranking is thoroughly justifiable.

Chief Director of HR Ms Caroline Khosa opened by stating that the Department has worked with many organs of state over many years yet this is the first time she has appeared before a Portfolio Committee. She joked about how nervous this has made her but emphasised that she and her team have worked tirelessly to support a proper recruitment process.

The Chair, after reassuring Ms Khosa, opened the floor to questions.

Ms Jordaan asked the DG if he could confirm if the executive authority (in this case it would be the Minister), under the Public Service Act (PSA) is ensuring compliant recruitment processes, including if the applicants had the proper employment history to be considered in line with the internal guidelines.

Mr Mweli reiterated that the Minister may exercise her executive authority in a limited capacity by performing oversight duties and functions as listed in section 3(1) of the PSA. It must also be noted that section 3(7)(a) specifically grants executive authorities an oversight mandate into human resources planning within their specific organ of state.

In response to Ms Jordaan’s question about following the rules internally, the DG remained steadfast in his resolution that the Department had faithfully complied with all necessary internal guidelines as provided for by Chapter Eight of the SMS Handbook, Chapter Two of the PSA Act, and the DPSA protocols on recruitment.

Ms Jordaan asked if any instruction was given by the Minister to favour any candidate. She mentioned the communications to the Minister that Mr Mweli acknowledged earlier and asked how the influence of multiple suggestions may have influenced the final decision-making process. She emphasised her concern for incorrect applicant records or incorrect advertisements and asked if there was any evidence of these errors taking place.

DG Mweli responded that the Minister had never tried to personally intervene beyond what her office allows for. A ministerial recommendation is not equivalent to an instruction – he is not being told to select that particular candidate, only that there may be compelling reasons (excellent work record, highly qualified, unique skillset etc) for that candidate to also be considered.

Minister Gwarube, after thanking the DG for outlining some of the applicable law, noted that the Public Service Act is the starting point for all discussions of executive authority, as well as the Public Service Regulations promulgated under this Act. These codifications require all appointments to be both lawful and procedurally fair, as well as being objective in judgement. They must also be merit-based, as well as ensuring all candidates are given their fair chance to apply and make their case. Additionally, the executive authority must be satisfied that the process is compliant, and all the information therein is accurate. There is a clear difference between oversight and interference and she had acted well within her oversight functions.

The Minister stated that, in fulfilling her oversight role, she must ensure that no candidate has received any additional or prejudicial support. Interference would only arise if she had attempted to influence, prejudice, or otherwise support one candidate over the others. Since she had not done this, then no interference had occurred.

Mr Shikwambana remarked how concerned he is about the state of the Department’s affairs. Despite following the correct and lawful procedures, it appeared that the Department had superseded their own authority and acted beyond their mandate in some instances. DG Mweli is on the record as agreeing that things were handled poorly. He made reference to the chain of custody of the documents as a “comedy of errors”. However, beyond this, he maintains that the Department's actions were within its mandate.

Mr Shikwambana stated that the applicant’s scores should be based only on the independent scoring system mentioned earlier. He raised concern about the Department’s seeming deviation from this, implementing their own arbitrary judgement by taking recommendations from sources other than the panel. He asked what the Minister’s reasoning for her choice of applicant was, and if this reasoning was arbitrary. He was particularly concerned about any potential influence the Minister may have had over the choice of candidate at any moment.

[The allegations of influence were denied by the Minster when she returned to the meeting.]

Mr Louw asked Ms Khoza how many people made up the panel that considered the applicants. He was concerned with the guidelines for how such a panel may be constituted. He pointed to the deviation of procedure mentioned earlier – namely how it seems the Minister may sometimes be seen as a ‘panel’ in her own right, which is at odds with the authority granted to the actual panel.

Mr Louw asked if this existing panel had adhered to the HR guidelines and policies when selecting, discussing, and recommending the candidates. What advice had HR given to the Minister on these guidelines and had they been adhered to?

Ms N Sharif (DA) asked the DG if the Minister has the executive authority for departmental appointments. If so, from where would this draw its legislative authority. Secondly, is the Minister entitled to make a recommendation of a candidate? Lastly, she asked if the Minister was presented with a clear recommendation for the position of DDG: Curriculum.

DG Mweli responded that the Minister may appoint Chief Directors and Directors, but DDGs must be appointed by Cabinet with approval by the DPSA. The Minister’s authority to appoint within her Department can be found in section 3(7)(b) of the PSA, which clearly states that an executive authority has all the necessary powers and functions to make lawful appointments within their own departments.

Ms T Magagula (ANC) asked HR if any deviation from the rankings provided for by the independent scoring criteria is, in fact, legally allowed. The clear approach is simply to choose the top-scoring candidate. She inquired what the procedure (if a lawful process even exist) was for choosing someone other than the top-scoring applicant. In essence, she was concerned about why the Minister would be allowed to overturn the panel’s decision.

It was clarified by Ms Khoza that, technically speaking, the top scorer need not always get the job. If there are compelling and lawful reasons for why another candidate should be considered then that same candidate deserves a fair attempt as well, even if at first glance the top scorer may seem to fit the bill.

Turning to the DG’s mention of legal action, Ms Magagula asked who would bear the costs, and specifically what actions is the Department involved in. Additionally, who does the Department propose would be accountable if the action(s) are unsuccessful.

DG Mweli stated that the DPSA's reason for denying the recommendation will unravel as soon as it faces judicial scrutiny. The reason in this instance was the allegation by the DPSA that the DBE’s organisational structure was non-compliant with DPSA Regulations. The DG explained that the DPSA had confirmed the DBE organisational structure to be in line with those same Regulations over the previous years. Since then, the structure has not changed. Therefore, the DPSA's own approval of the DBE structure is fatal to their allegation ab initio and it will not stand in open court.

Ms Magagula raised concern if the Department had properly complied with its own internal protocol on recruitment and asked HR if these had been properly adhered to. HR was also asked if she had advised the Minister on the adherence to these internal rules and regulations, especially in light of how to properly recruit applicants to the positions. The Committee must not stand idly by and must be sure to act on any improprieties.

Mr Mweli responded that advice was given to the Minister at every stage of the recruitment process. While her question had been to Ms Khoza, he too had suitable qualifications and experience to answer HR-related questions to some extent. Alongside himself, both Ms Khosa and Ms Le Roux were both present at these advisory sessions.

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) asked if the recruitment framework of the Department is in line with the internal guidelines. If it is so, then how many members of the panel were elected by the Department to oversee the recruitment process. The guidelines must be at the centre of all departmental actions insofar as such conduct falls within its oversight. If the process was not followed, which appears to the be the case in the deviation from the guidelines [the Minister being given the opportunity to select between two candidates], then there must be enforcement or correction thereof.

The DG replied that all guidelines had been followed, with specific reference to Chapter Eight of the SMS Handbook and the associated DPSA Regulations. He stated that there is no set number of panel members in reference to Public Service Regulation 2F.

Ms Xaba-Ntshaba said that the Minister does not have the power to choose between candidates, and that this is a serious departure from the accepted protocols. There has been no statutory permission for ministers to choose between candidates – only that they must say yes or no to a single candidate recommended by the panel.

DG Mweli pointed out that there is a difference between choosing and simply recommending. The Minister is well within her rights to make recommendations as long as she has a compelling and lawful reason to do so. The keyword here is lawful, because these recommendations only remain valid so long as there is a bona fide reason, alongside clear rationale, for making the recommendation to begin with.

Mr Komane asked about the experience of each panel member and if they were fit and proper to sit, adjudicate, and then recommend applicants. If they were indeed fit and proper, then there is no need for the Minister to step in and undermine that process by stepping into role reserved for the panel specifically.

DG Mweli emphasised that all of the panel-members were suitably qualified to stand as panel-members. Acknowledgement was made of the hard work these members have done. He firmly believed that they have done their work to the best of their ability. The Minister making a recommendation is not a reflection of the quality of the panel itself; it reflects only that the Minister wanted to recommend a candidate.

On the delegation of authority to panel members, DG Mweli responded that the selection criteria are set out in Regulation 67 of the Public Service Regulations 2016. This, read with Chapter Eight of the SMS Handbook (Employment of Heads of Departments) and paragraph 7 of the DPSA Executive Protocol for Department Heads and DDGs, allows a panel to be selected who then make recommendations on the suitability of candidates. Also, this recommendation is dependent on the panel reaching consensus, and the final decision must be based on a convincingly wide array of evidence. DDGs may also only be recruited upon agreement with the DPSA and approval by Cabinet.

The DG confirmed that he had been a part of the panel. He had already been a part of many panels previously, inter alia for the DG for the Department of Social Development, the DG for the Free State, and for the DG of the Department of Science and Technology. In all previous instances he had been involved in, the recommendation came from the panel. However, the Minister, using her executive authority, may make her own recommendations. He did confirm that this is the first instance this has happened to his knowledge.

Mr Komane asked if there was a political affiliation between the Minister and the candidate she chose, and if so then this had been unlawful cadre employment.

The Minister firmly denied that she was involved in any form of cadre employment. Her Department has taken a strong stance against such unlawful practice because it erodes the foundational tenets of good governance. It robs the properly qualified people from attaining the positions they have earned. |Her only intention was to execute her oversight duties in a way that may help end the deadlock and see the DDG: Curriculum position filled by someone of the proper calibre.

Investigating the Years of Delay in DDG Positions

Ms P Mngandi (MKP) asked about the DDG: Curriculum position vacant since December 2020 and the DDG: Teaching position vacant since September 2023. The DDG: Curriculum was then advertised in November 2024, with interviews following three months later in 2025. She asked why this process had taken over four years to get to this stage, and why the second position, DDG: Teaching, is entering its third year of procedural malaise.

DG Mweli responded that one reason it had taken so long is it often takes time to hear back from potential applicants if they are interested or not. For example, DBE was in communication for several months with an advocate from Gauteng, only for him to turn down the DDG: Curriculum position in the end. There are considerable variables at play which in turn affect the length of this process.

On the recruitment delays, the Minister asked if the Committee is interested in both the DDG positions, or specifically the one relating to curriculum.

The Chair replied that the DGG: Curriculum is of interest as that post had a split panel, whereas the panel for the DDG: Teaching position had reached a single verdict.

The Minister replied that the delay has been due to a fair number of factors. One issue is that the Curriculum position requires very specialised knowledge which is hard to find in a candidate. So by its very nature, recruitment for this position will require casting a wider net. The reason for the emphasis on this skillset is that the position is at the core of what the Department does – it is responsible for the literal education curriculum itself. This meant DBE had to be circumspect in its search because the wrong choice could have a serious impact.

Ms Mngandi stated that since the DBE had submitted Cabinet appointment lists in August to September of 2025, and an application for organisation structure approvement in November 2025, as well as obtaining legal compliance advice, then why is there still an administrative deadlock with the DPSA. What concrete steps have been taken to resolve or mediate this dispute in order for these critical leadership roles to be properly filled?

Mr Khunou replied that the DPSA argument is invalid due to the organisational structure being pre-approved by the DPSA in 2010. On potential avenues to avoid litigation he noted Public Service Regulation 2A which allows the Minister to intercede when there is an inter-departmental deadlock.

The Minister said that in an effort to move forward, she has considered her powers under the Public Service Act. It gives the EA power to appoint within their own department and it does not necessarily require adhering to the first-ranked candidate chosen by a panel. Reg. 67(1) of the Public Service Regulations states that a panel may be appointed by the EA to make a recommendation on the post, which the Minister followed. Afterwards, in light of the stalemate, Reg. 67(8A) had to be considered, which gives the EA the power to refuse the recommendation. Upon this refusal, reasons must be given. The Minister’s reasoning was that, when measured holistically and beyond the scores themselves, the panel’s first-ranked recommendation would not be fit for the post, hence she recommended the second-ranked candidate.

Ms Mngandi spoke in her vernacular.

Ms Jordaan raised a point of order and requested translation.

Ms Mngandi’s remarks were translated as follows: her concerns arose from Ms Jordaan’s invoking of section 56 orders where she must instead issue a request. She alleged that Ms Jordaan was positioning herself in defence of the Minister and the Department and asked her to desist from that.

Ms Jordaan, on learning that the comment was directed at her, asked for greater focus on the matter at hand and for members to desist from personal attacks throughout the meeting.

The Chair noted that Ms Jordaan does not have the right to call for an order in an attempt to obtain translation – she must instead make a request for translation vis-à-vis her right to reply. She cannot call for an order if she does not understand what was said. Once translation is given, if Ms Jordaan chooses to do so then she may raise a point of order.

The Chair requested that members should refrain from addressing each other directly and called for decorum to be maintained. She reminded members that they have a duty to act in proportion to their status as Members of Parliament. That is, members must not fall into the habit of raising points against one another when they are meant to be aligned in the interrogation of the issues at hand.

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) said that the Committee is looking to increase oversight into the administrative functioning of the Department. Some grade 11 and grade 12 learners did not only have to write the exams without proper support but were then expected to mark their own paper due to an absence of markers. He noted that the ill-prepared manner in which the Department has come before the Committee has only strengthened his resolve to see the implementation of a more cohesive, responsive, and accountable oversight framework. The crux of his question was if damage had been caused to the education system by all of these issues.

DG Mweli responded acknowledged that there has indeed been damage to South African’s education milestones. The lack of coordination offered by these two DDG positions has meant that certain responsibilities have gone unfulfilled, resulting in gaps in the curriculum and losses in the teaching sector. He acknowledged the Acting DDG Dr Mthembu’s help in holding the ship together as they attempt to find a replacement.

Mr Mkhize asked if the Minister making her own recommendation forms part of the proper procedure in the recruitment for Department positions. Following such a recommendation, the amount of weight accorded to that recommendation must also be discussed – is it equal to the panel’s powers or does it supersede the panel.

On the Minister’s power to recommend, Mr Mweli responded that the Minister does indeed have the executive authority to recommend a candidate. This power stems from the fact that the Minister has the final say on approval and, as long as the process leading up to such consideration is lawful, she may exercise her discretion on that. To be lawful, the process must be procedurally compliant and must also be substantively compliant.

Mr Mkhize asked for further clarity on the rankings of the applicants and if these rankings were influenced by the Minister or if they were ranked according to their scores only.

DG Mweli maintained that the rankings tell the whole story: the candidate who was ranked number one of the scoring list, is the candidate who is seen as having placed in first place. This was done through various competency tests to generate these scores. The crux of the issue is that the panel themselves could not agree if the candidate in the first position should actually be recommended.

Mr Mkhize asked why only two names were forwarded to the Minister for her thoughts when there were five applicants in total. Why did the Department not send the full roster of five applicants to the Minister? He echoed Ms Ngandi’s concerns about the length of this process – noting that many years have passed since the positions became vacant. HR Recruitment Process at DBE

Ms Y Govana (ANC) admonished the delegation for the lack of documents, noting that it has made the task of investigating the recruitment process that much harder. She asked HR to outline the steps of their recruitment process. There will also need to more engagement with all levels of government to improve access to education and ensure proper positions are filled to that effect.

Ms Khoza replied that once a position has reserved the necessary funding then HR will begin the process of job evaluation. This evaluation allows the Department to determine the level of the post, as in what role it is, is it level 13 or level 14 in the organisational structure, what are the benefits, etc.

Ms Khoza clarified that the first stage in the recruitment process is this evaluation of the post itself – what are the responsibilities, what skills are required, what qualifications are needed. This in turn allows HR to properly prepare an advert in accordance with the position. Every candidate at a level 13 position must complete a pre-entry certificate, which is non-negotiable as it is mandated by the Framework on the Professionalisation of the Public Service (FPPS).

The second stage is the competency profile. This is what is disclosed in the advertisement for the position, such as experience needed, minimum qualifications, and relevant skillset. This same competency profile must, by necessity, clearly communicate what is expected of the candidates on their application for that position.

Thirdly, is the creation, categorising, and sorting of applicants who meet the requirements versus those who do not in a single database. This is then tabulated to form a shortlist of candidates to be presented to either the DG or to the Minister, alongside asking for a panel to be constituted to decide on that shortlist.

Penultimately, the fourth stage is the actual interviews. These are to be conducted by the panel after receiving the required permissions from either the DG or the Minister. Potential candidates are informed that they will have to prepare a presentation for the panel, with approval being sent for competency tests down the line.

In conclusion, after passing the competency test, the fifth stage is the actual appointment itself. This is the stage that requires the procedural and substantive fairness of said appointment, coupled with it being prima facie lawful. At the end of this chain of events, provided each has been complied with, the appointment will be made and position filled.

It seems to indicate that the Minister’s favoured applicants were not on the shortlist, so she added them ex post facto.

The Chair inquired into the presumption of authority that the Chief of Staff carries. Specifically, when the Chief of Staff gives instructions – either horizontally to contemporaries or vertically downwards to his subordinates – do those receiving the instruction take it to be with the implicit agreement of the Minister, or do they perceive the Chief of Staff acting in his own capacity? She asked the DG what his impression was of the interactions between the Chief of Staff and himself. Had the Chief of Staff made representations in his own capacity, or did he mention he was acting under ministerial direction? Also, as a panel member for the appointment of the Chief Director: Comms, had the DG been made aware of the irregularities concerning the two additional CVs.

DG Mweli stated that he had not been aware of the addition of the two CVs and was very shocked when he found out ex post facto. He clarified that the Chief of Staff appeared to be there under his own merits and did not mention the Minister.

The Chair asked Ms Khoza to confirm the authenticity of the 3 December 2025 email sent to her from the Chief of Staff. This email, which noted “as per my last email”, clearly does not stand alone and must be read with the previous email. She asked if Ms Khoza could provide the details of that previous email.

[The meeting ran out of time and was adjourned before several of these questions could be answered].

The Chair noted that the amending of the advertisement for the Chief Director: Comms position is new to the Committee, as it arose during this meeting. She asked the DG to shed light on the process and to identify any points of concern.

When asked about what changes were made to the advert, the DG noted that the mandatory orientation program required of all public servants was no longer there. This is especially concerning because, as the name implies, attending the orientation is mandated and cannot be omitted from the advert.

The Chair asked if the Minister amended the advert prior to its release. If so, then what HR expertise could the Minister have claimed to rely on to justify these changes. Essentially, what grounds or reason guided the Minister to make this decision instead of HR. More so, why were these changes not mentioned until the Committee unearthed them?

The Minister clarified that the orientation program was omitted from the advert for it to better appeal to persons outside of the Department. This was also done in an effort to make the advert itself look less generic.

Closing Remarks and Recommendations

The Chair reiterated that the Committee wants to find out which parties are flouting the correct HR channels. Specifically, if the previous Chief of Staff had acted as a backdoor for CVs from interested persons looking to get ahead of the competition by using personal connections within the Department to sidestep the appropriate channels.

The Chair remained unsatisfied with the Minister’s explanation on the changes made to the advertisement. The posting of adverts is done by the HR Office who are duly qualified and authorised to do so. The question then arises as to what qualifications, experience, or special skills the Minister has that justified her decision to change the advert.

The Chair was particularly concerned that the DG did not know about the changes to the advert. Further, the advert had been printed and was in circulation before anybody knew that it had been changed. It points to a lack of coordination between members of the Department.

In closing, the Chair stated that she remained unconvinced about the Minister’s denial of political interference. She pointed to the numerous deviations from accepted procedure and the convenient lack of documents which may help explain this.

Mr Shikwambana suggested that this Committee meeting had been deliberately sabotaged by the delegation, who in their preparation for this meeting all came to the consensus not to include documents. Those persons who were involved but are not present at this meeting will be forced to attend a future sitting of this Committee to explain themselves.

On recommendations for the way forward, the Chair agreed that a forensic investigation, as called for by Mr Mkhize, is appropriate in these circumstances. Legal advice will be sought on the matter in order for members to understand how best to proceed. The documents need to be provided by the Department. Should any item mentioned in the Committee’s letter of request not be provided then there needs to be an accompanying letter explaining why that document could not be handed over. Following this, Ms Maimela thanked all for their attendance and adjourned the meeting.
