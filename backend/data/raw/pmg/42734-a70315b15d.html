KZN & NW PEDs Annual Performance Plans, implementation of AGSA recommendations & anti-bullying strategies; with the Minister and Deputy Minister

Basic Education

31 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committee discussed the strategic and annual performance plans (APPs) presented by the Provincial Education Departments (PEDs) of KwaZulu-Natal and North West, noting that the purpose of the engagement was to strengthen oversight and assess alignment with national education priorities. Members observed that both provinces had outlined strategies to improve educational outcomes, address socioeconomic challenges, implement anti-bullying measures, and enhance governance and accountability. The Minister and Deputy Minister emphasised the importance of focusing on measurable outcomes, accountability, and coordinated responses to challenges such as bullying and audit findings.

Members noted that KwaZulu-Natal’s plans focused on Early Childhood Development (ECD), foundational learning, and vocational pathways, while also aiming to improve governance and social cohesion despite challenges such as budget constraints and infrastructure damage. In contrast, the North West Department presented a more budget-aligned approach, outlining measures to address audit issues, improve admissions systems, and strengthen anti-bullying interventions. Overall, the Committee acknowledged the efforts made, but stressed the need for realistic planning, improved implementation, and sustained accountability.

The discussion focused on a range of concerns and observations raised by the Committee regarding the planning, budgeting, and implementation of education priorities in the provinces. Members noted that insufficient and unclear data on key challenges, such as dropout rates and literacy levels, were limiting the ability to design targeted interventions. They expressed concern about the lack of alignment between budget allocations and strategic priorities in the APPs, particularly in areas such as infrastructure and ECD.

The Committee highlighted weaknesses in implementation, pointing to missing operational details, unclear timelines, contractor management failures, and persistent supply chain issues. It was further noted that recurring problems, including irregular expenditure and audit findings, were not being adequately addressed in the APPs. Members stressed the need for improved teacher development, better resource allocation, and stronger consequence management to enhance learning outcomes.

Concerns were also raised about the effectiveness of programmes such as the National School Nutrition Programme, including supply chain failures and service provider challenges, as well as funding gaps for scholar transport. The Committee questioned infrastructure budget spending, under-expenditure, and plans to address contractor non-performance, while emphasising the importance of proper situational analysis and baseline data to support planning across infrastructure, staffing, transport, and digitisation.

The Committee was critical of KwaZulu-Natal’s APP, raising concerns about poor communication and responsiveness from the provincial departments, as well as labour disputes involving unions, principals, and teachers that were affecting recruitment and school functionality in the province.

In the North West, questions were raised about collaboration with mining companies to mitigate social impacts when mining activities cease. The Committee emphasised the need for greater involvement of the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation to improve the quality and alignment of APPs.

Additional queries were raised regarding targets for National Senior Certificate pass rates, ECD subsidies, school infrastructure challenges, bullying prevention strategies, and disaster management planning, particularly in relation to storm damage. Members highlighted ongoing issues of overcrowding, dilapidated schools, lack of maintenance, and insufficient new infrastructure in KwaZulu-Natal.

The discussion underscored the importance of stronger community engagement and the effective functioning of school governing bodies to address social challenges and improve school governance. While the Committee acknowledged some positive developments, such as improved matric results and the employment of new Grade R teachers, it called for clearer, more detailed plans, including timelines and accountability measures, as well as follow-ups on commitments made during oversight visits.

Meeting report

Opening Remarks by the Chairperson

The Chairperson welcomed the provincial education departments (PEDs) from KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) and North West, and said the meeting was intended for them to present their strategic and annual performance plans so the Committee could understand the provincial priorities, daily operational pressures, and how their plans aligned with the National Development Plan (NDP) and Medium Term Development Plan (MTDP), while also appraising performance targets, educational delivery, and governance management.

The virtual platform helped the Committee to appraise governance within the PEDs and ensured accountability where governance lapses were identified. It also allowed Members to understand the broader education system and the socioeconomic factors influencing educational outcomes across provinces. She emphasised the importance of the meeting, urging the PEDs to highlight key areas in their presentations to enable effective oversight.

Deputy Minister's opening remarks

Dr Regina Mhaule, Deputy Minister of Basic Education (DBE), said the purpose of the meeting was to listen to the strategic plans of the two Departments, and acknowledged that the Committee had already reviewed the strategic planning documents and the annual performance plans (APPs). She highlighted that issues such as bullying and anti-bullying policies, as well as matters raised by the Auditor-General (AG), had been noted, and she believed that the Departments and the Committee were aligned on these issues.

She said that from the perspective of the DBE, the Department remained in a healthy state. She expressed appreciation for these engagements, as they provided insights from the provinces that the Department might not have identified on its own, and she looked forward to the discussions ahead.

Minister's opening remarks

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, stressed that while inputs such as budgets, plans, policies, and programmes mattered, they were not sufficient on their own. The sector must also remain relentlessly focused on outputs and results, asking whether learners were learning; whether children were being placed on time; whether school environments were becoming safer; whether bullying interventions were effective; whether provinces were responding meaningfully to audit findings, and whether plans were translating into better performance, administration, and educational outcomes. She emphasised that this should be the standard by which the sector’s work was measured.

She said the inclusion in the day’s agenda of issues such as planning alignment, responses to the Auditor-General of South Africa's (AGSA's) recommendations, bullying incidents, anti-bullying policies, and admissions and placements, was necessary and timely, stressing that these matters were not peripheral. She highlighted that bullying and school safety remained a serious concern across the sector, explaining that a protocol with the South African Police Service (SAPS) had been established to support a coordinated response to violence, intimidation, bullying, and other threats in schools. She said that the value of such interventions would ultimately be measured by practical outcomes, such as whether learners and teachers felt safer and whether incidents were prevented.

She concluded by affirming the importance of accountability. She expressed confidence that the presentations from KZN and North West would assist the Committee in exercising its oversight role meaningfully, encouraging a frank, constructive, and solution-focused engagement.

KwaZulu-Natal Department of Education

Mr Nkosinathi Ngcobo, Head of Department (HOD): Education, presented the Department’s 2025–2030 strategic plan and APP, aligned with the NDP and provincial priorities. He highlighted the key focus areas including early childhood development (ECD), literacy and numeracy in the foundation phase, vocational and Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) education, social cohesion, and inclusion for learners with special needs.

He emphasised the importance of corporate governance, accountability, and fraud prevention, alongside infrastructure improvements, digital learning, and teacher development. He acknowledged challenges such as budget cuts, staff shortages, and storm damage, noting mitigation through a fiscal recovery and audit turnaround plan. He confirmed strengthened performance reporting, data management via the Education Management Information System (EMIS), and said that APP targets for National Senior Certificate passes, vocational skills and other outcomes, would be monitored and adjusted as needed. Anti-bullying initiatives were also included in the Department’s plans.

He reported that bullying in schools was a serious challenge, affecting learners’ growth and career prospects, with broader socioeconomic impacts. The Department worked with the Departments of Health and Social Development, and civil society, guided by the National School Safety Framework and the Curriculum Assessment Policy Statement (CAPS) life orientation curriculum, to prevent and address bullying.

He noted that the Khula and Lembe schools had the most reported incidents, but low numbers elsewhere did not mean bullying was absent. Interventions included deploying learner support agents, running counselling and behavioural sessions, forming support groups, and conducting community dialogues.

He emphasised that prevention was the main focus, with ongoing training for learners and educators, anti-bullying campaigns, and collaboration with the SAPS to ensure safer school environments.

North West Province Department of Education

Ms Viola Motsumi, Member of the Executive Council (MEC) for Education in the North West, said the presentation would focus on the 2025–2030 strategic plan and the APP, ensuring alignment with the budget to improve implementation. She emphasised efforts to address past audit findings, including material irregularities, and measures to prevent unnecessary expenditures, such as improving pension and contract termination systems.

She also discussed ongoing initiatives to strengthen anti-bullying policies, establish hotlines for reporting incidents, and engage parents through webinars. On admissions, she acknowledged challenges with school placement transparency, particularly in rural areas, and stressed the Department’s commitment to fairness and protecting learners’ access to education.

Finally, she noted progress on asset management, with 95% of documents covered, and expressed appreciation for the continued support of the Auditor General (AG) and other stakeholders.

Mr Lengane Bogatsu, HOD, North West Education Department, said the Department’s presentation focused on strategic planning, aligning with its mandate under the Maintenance Capital Efficiency Programme (MCEP), strategic priorities, and medium term development goals.

He highlighted the alignment of the APP with strategic objectives and national and provincial outcomes, noting the role of oversight structures. Key priorities included poverty reduction, addressing the high cost of living, and maintaining educational quality across ordinary, independent, and special schools, with standardised indicators across programmes covering administration, school performance, infrastructure, examinations, and learner services.

He reported progress on programmes such as multi-grade resources, special schools, early childhood development, and technical monitoring and evaluation, while acknowledging challenges in staffing, budget allocation, and operational capacity. He outlined steps to implement the APP, ensure compliance, conduct audits, digitise records, and address social risks, bullying, and child safety through multi-department collaboration and planned hotline services. Finally, he provided updates on school admissions management and the development of an online enrolment system, emphasising the Department's continued focus on performance, governance, and education quality.

Discussion

Mr S Louw (ANC) indicated that one of the key challenges he had identified in the North West presentation was related to data and its disaggregation, which limited a clear understanding of specific educational challenges across the province. He said insufficient data on dropout rates and Grade 4 literacy levels made it difficult to target interventions effectively. He said there was a lack of clarity regarding how budget allocations aligned with the strategic priorities outlined in the APP. Many of the plans presented, particularly in infrastructure and ECD, appeared ambiguous and potentially exceeded available resources. He stressed the need for realignment, especially as departments often cited insufficient resources as a constraint.

He highlighted the importance of improving budget alignment and addressing implementation weaknesses. He pointed out that operational details necessary for effective implementation, such as timelines and cost plans, were often missing. There was inadequate clarity on how contractor management failures and supply chain issues were being addressed, which emphasised the need for detailed and practical solutions.

He also raised concerns about the limited focus on teacher development and resource allocation, noting that insufficient emphasis was placed on addressing performance challenges. He stressed the importance of consequence management and the need for tangible responses with clear indicators to improve learning outcomes.

He said that limited frameworks for evaluating the implementation of the APP hindered accountability and the ability to make data-driven decisions. He referred to issues identified by the school governing body (SGB), including irregular expenditure, supply chain management (SCM) weaknesses, and audit findings from AGSA. He pointed out that the APP lacked concrete strategies to reduce irregular expenditure, which remained a recurring issue, and called for accountability in addressing it.

He expressed concern about the lack of detail on how the Department planned to improve SCM processes to avoid service delivery delays, and emphasised the need for clear mitigating mechanisms. Regarding audit findings, he noted that recurring financial management weaknesses and inadequate controls highlighted by the AGSA were not sufficiently addressed in the APP. He said that these observations were intended to assist the province in strengthening alignment with key programmes.

He then posed several questions for clarification. He asked what steps were being taken to prevent supply chain failures in the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP), noting its importance and past success. He also inquired about the number of learners qualifying for scholar transport and the funding gap between 2025 and 2026. He asked whether the sanitary dignity programme had reached all targeted schools. He requested information on the percentage of the infrastructure budget spent annually, including details on underspending and its causes. He also asked how many schools still lacked access to essential services such as water and electricity, and what plans were in place to address contractor non-performance.

In conclusion, Mr Louw expressed appreciation to the Department, stating that it had performed well and had shown improvement over time. He said his questions were intended to strengthen and support their work. He also congratulated the province on its matric results, and acknowledged progress in teacher appointments in response to the Portfolio Committee’s calls.

Ms T Magagula (ANC) said her main focus would be on KwaZulu-Natal. She noted that additional funding had been provided to assist schools affected by storm damage. She asked whether the Department had established interim plans to ensure that repairs in schools would not interfere with teaching and learning.

She further inquired about ECD programmes, noting that the APP prioritised expanding access to them. She asked how the Department would ensure quality alongside increased access and ensure that the required human resource personnel were recruited.

On teacher development, she observed that the APP aimed to prioritise teacher development despite prevailing financial challenges, and asked how the Department planned to ensure that targets were met without setting low quality targets.

She raised concerns regarding bullying. She noted that the Department’s plan seemed reactive, addressing bullying after it had occurred, and asked whether the Department’s strategy included collaboration with other partners who shared an interest in preventing bullying. She requested information on how these partners could assist in investigating and analysing the causes of bullying and in planning rehabilitation for affected learners. She explained that some learners may bully others as a result of being bullied themselves, or due to environmental factors. She suggested that collaboration with institutions such as the Department of Social Development (DSD) could help curb such behaviours.

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) began by confirming that he was visible on the platform and made a light remark about ensuring that his background appeared appropriately formal. He starts by acknowledging the technical complexity involved in developing (APPs).

He expressed concern that the APPs had been developed prior to the confirmation of funding allocations, questioning how Departments could effectively plan activities without certainty regarding available resources. He noted that this placed Departments at a structural disadvantage and suggested that this approach should be reconsidered in future planning cycles.

He indicated that many of his inputs would apply to both provinces under discussion, as the technical requirements for effective planning were largely consistent across provinces, particularly where improvements in administration were intended. He posed a critical question as to whether a proper situational analysis had been conducted to establish clear priorities and performance baselines. He emphasised that such an analysis should be grounded in real conditions on the ground rather than assumptions about anticipated budgets.

He asked whether there was comprehensive, real-time data available to inform planning, including the number of new schools required, schools in need of repairs, those lacking access to water and sanitation, and those still reliant on inappropriate sanitation systems. He stressed the importance of knowing how many schools required fencing, libraries, and learning materials, as well as how many additional educators needed to be employed to address staffing shortages and improve educational outcomes.

In addition, he queried whether departments had data on the number of learners and schools requiring scholar transport, the extent of digitisation needed across schools, and the number of full-service schools in rural, township, and farm communities that were not adequately resourced. He illustrated this concern by referring to a specific example of a full-service school that lacked proper infrastructure and adequately trained teachers.

He also raised concerns about the distribution of technical schools, observing that such institutions were often located in more developed areas, rather than in rural or underserved communities where technical skills were most needed to address poverty. He asked how many such schools currently existed in underserved areas, and how many more were required.

He inquired about access to the NSNP, noting that many schools were still excluded, and questioned how school infrastructure challenges -- particularly the widespread state of dilapidation -- were being prioritised in planning. He also asked how the employment of additional educators was being treated as a priority, given the existence of both staffing shortages and unemployed qualified individuals.

Turning specifically to KwaZulu-Natal, Mr Mkhize criticised the inclusion of five-year targets within an APP, stating that APPs should focus strictly on a 12-month period with clearly defined quarterly targets to enable effective oversight and accountability. He argued that presenting longer term targets within an annual framework undermined the ability to properly track performance. In this regard, he added that this was within the “Ramaphosa administration." He stressed that the matter should not be treated as political, but rather as an issue of proper governance and accountability.

Mr Mkhize said that he wanted clarity on the factors contributing to poor planning, as identified by oversight bodies. He asked whether these had been addressed to prevent recurring issues such as irregular, fruitless, and unauthorised expenditure. He emphasised that success required learning from past failures and putting effective corrective measures in place.

He expressed concern that responses to admitted weaknesses, including poor performance information and unspent infrastructure funds, were inadequate, and questioned what strategies were being implemented to address these gaps. He further asked about measures to combat corruption in the NSNP, improve inefficiencies in learner transport, ensure better infrastructure monitoring, and appoint competent contractors.

He criticised the KZN Department for being unresponsive to Committee correspondence, including matters relating to flood victims, school transport, and other community concerns. He stressed that improving responsiveness should form part of performance improvement strategies. He also highlighted cases where the Department had failed to act on urgent issues, including a learner’s expulsion and ongoing labour disputes affecting schools.

In addition, he raised concerns about instability caused by conflicts among unions, school management, and teachers, and urged the Department to address these issues, as they negatively impacted education outcomes.

Regarding the North West province, he asked how it was working with mining companies to mitigate the social and economic impact of mine closures on communities and schools.

Finally, he questioned the role of the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) in the APP process, suggesting that stronger involvement was needed to address technical weaknesses such as misaligned priorities and poor performance indicators.

Ms P Mngadi (MKP) inquired about the target percentage of learners expected to pass the National Senior Certificate examination in 2026/27 in KZN, and how this compared to performance in 2023 and 2024, which was 88%. She also asked how many children were targeted to benefit from the ECD subsidy in 2026/27, and what the baseline number of registered ECD programmes was.

Referring to the departmental plans, she highlighted that the document indicated 50 public schools were to be provided with water services, and 3 350 public schools renovated over five years, and asked what challenges, such as budget cuts or storm damage, might impede these plans. She also queried the reported reduction in bullying incidents, asking what factors had contributed to the decline and why certain districts continued to experience higher levels of bullying in primary schools. She emphasised the need to ensure sufficient resources were available to support children, noting that some schools had not been able to report on these matters.

She asked about the provincial rollout of the sanitary dignity programme -- specifically the total number of beneficiary schools and learners in North West, and the percentage this represented of the total eligible population. She queried the cost per learner, given the budget allocation, and how this compared to the actual annual need per learner, particularly regarding consistent monthly access to sanitary products. She also asked how many schools in the province currently had adequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities, and what proportion still faced a lack of sanitation, water access, and disposal systems that could undermine the programme’s effectiveness.

Finally, she asked about the status of safe schools across the North West Province, and how many were fully functional.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) referred to recent media reports indicating plans to appoint 500 new Grade R teachers in KZN. She asked from which programme the funding for this initiative would come, and whether any other core services might be adversely affected due to this expansion. She also inquired how these teachers would be funded, and whether any challenges in paying their salaries were foreseen in the next financial year.

She asked how both provinces were addressing the provision of nutrition for Grade R learners. Specifically for KwaZulu-Natal, she wanted to know how many service providers for the NSNP were currently appointed or planned to be appointed, and whether there had been any challenges in paying and providing meals on time, and if such challenges were anticipated in the coming year.

She recalled that the North West province had been affected by disasters during previous oversight visits, and asked whether repairs following those disasters had been addressed. She also asked both provinces about forward planning for disaster management, noting that KZN was often affected by disastrous weather.

She acknowledged that training on the National School Safety Framework was taking place in the North West, and asked whether this also applied to KZN. She inquired whether both provinces had been rolling out the school safety protocol, what the progress was, and whether the programme was found to be impactful.

She requested statistics on the provision of Grade R in both provinces. She concluded by asking whether either province had experienced, or expected to experience, any post or personnel cuts in the next year.

Mr L Komane (EFF) expressed concern about bullying in schools and noted that while progress had been shown in the presentation, he emphasised that proper planning and directives were essential to sustain any anti-bullying programmes. He observed that much of the presentation had focused on child-to-child interactions, but he expected to hear about teacher-to-child and child-to-teacher interactions as well, and hoped that future presentations would include concrete resolutions and strategies for tackling bullying.

He highlighted that anti-bullying strategies should not rely solely on teachers or the Department, as bullying often originates from families and households. He suggested that identifying the children responsible for bullying and involving their families, along with social workers, would allow for tracing the causes and addressing them at the root.

He also criticised the KZN presentation for not adequately reflecting the province's specific context. He gave the example of new boarding school construction while existing infrastructure in the province was dilapidated, citing Ulundi Ward 23 Primary School, which had been damaged by wind for five years without sufficient departmental intervention. He questioned the credibility of presenting plans for new schools when existing schools were not properly maintained.

He questioned whether the leadership of the Department of Education in KwaZulu-Natal had accurate information about their schools, citing Babanango Primary School as an example, where children were disrupted by goats entering the school grounds, and where mobile classrooms had been in place for eight years. He demanded clarification on the funds allocated for proper school construction and the apparent delays and mismanagement regarding facilities such as toilets.

He raised concerns about KZN's response to flood-affected schools, urging the Department to provide a clear plan detailing how affected schools would be assisted, including funding allocations and logistics. He insisted that planning should be detailed from start to finish so that the Committee could understand and potentially assist.

Finally, he criticised KZN's transport arrangements, highlighting children from rural villages travelling long distances to Nauti Combined School and the financial burden on parents, questioning where the provincial transport allocations were made. He called for a strategy and explanation on how children in mobile classes or damaged classrooms were currently being accommodated and taught.

The Chairperson commented that there were issues cutting across both provinces. She raised the issue of early childhood education and access to ECD programmes, pointing out that while the provinces aimed to improve both access and quality, reports indicated that about one million to 1.3 million children aged three to five did not access such programmes in the country. She asked whether the provinces had statistics on how many of these children were in their respective provinces, to determine whether their strategic plans aligned with the identified problem. She emphasised that knowing the baseline would help the Portfolio Committee to assess if annual performance targets addressed the magnitude of the problem.

She then addressed infrastructure challenges, specifically the eradication of pit latrine toilets. She noted that KZN had 1 300 pit latrines, with 54 remaining, but the province’s APP did not clarify when these would be eradicated. She sought clarity on whether the 54 represented a backlog, and when they would be addressed. She also asked the Northwest province how many pit latrines remained, and their plans for eradication.

She expressed serious concern about KZN's overall infrastructure, highlighting that no new schools, classrooms, or ECD centres were planned in the APP due to budget cuts. She questioned how access could be increased without new infrastructure, and emphasised the crisis posed by overcrowding and dilapidated classrooms. She called on the DPE to engage on this issue.

She also raised the matter of scholar transport in KZN, noting that about 500 transport projects were being discontinued, affecting poor communities and undermining children’s right to access basic education. She stressed the urgency of addressing both infrastructure and transport issues to ensure safe and conducive learning environments.

She then discussed social cohesion and nation-building priorities, noting that planned community engagements to combat racism, sexism, hate speech, and other forms of intolerance were minimal. She argued that community engagements did not require significant funds, and that school governing bodies could lead these initiatives. She criticised the target of only 60% of functional SGBs in the North West, arguing that all SGBs should meet minimum functionality criteria.

Regarding the number of learners subsidised in registered independent schools, she asked for the criteria used to determine which learners were subsidised, and whether the approach was aligned with areas of greatest pressure.

On financial management, she referred to AGSA recommendations on irregular, unauthorised, and wasteful expenditure, noting that the three-year strategic plan lacked specific timelines and detail.

She emphasised the need for further engagement with the DPE on the three-stream model, as current targets and objectives were vague. She stressed that the Department should provide clarity on which schools would be targeted, and how awareness and reporting platforms would be implemented.

Finally, she acknowledged successes, applauding the Department’s visibility, academic performance, and the employment of 500 Grade R teachers. She concluded by noting follow-up concerns from a school in KZN, where commitments on classrooms, infrastructure, and major maintenance had not been implemented. She requested the Department’s comments on this issue.

Responses

KwaZulu-Natal

Mr Sipho Hlomuka, MEC for Education, welcomed the inputs raised by Committee Members, and indicated that the Department had already prepared a detailed report addressing many of the issues discussed. He emphasised that the province continued to receive support from the national Department, particularly in relation to infrastructure programmes.

Infrastructure and scholar transportation

The MEC explained that the Department’s presentation, specifically under Programme 6 (Infrastructure), outlined several ongoing and planned projects. These included the construction of three new ECD centres, which had already been awarded to implementing agents approximately two to three months earlier. In addition, he noted that 25 ECD centres had been maintained in the current financial year, including the centre previously visited by the Portfolio Committee in the uThukela District.

He said plans were in place to construct additional specialist classrooms as part of broader infrastructure development. He highlighted that infrastructure funding was allocated across various priorities, including new school construction, the maintenance of existing schools, and addressing storm damage. As an example, he referred to a planned special school in Nongoma, which had experienced delays due to procurement challenges at the national level.

He also noted that new allocations had been received to address sanitation challenges, particularly the provision of toilets, which would be implemented in the upcoming financial year. He added that additional funding had recently been made available to respond to storm-damaged schools, although limited resources meant not all affected schools could be assisted.

He acknowledged that budget constraints remained a significant challenge, especially in relation to infrastructure delivery, as the cost of construction limited the number of projects that could be undertaken.

On scholar transport, he agreed with concerns raised by Members, and described the issue as a major ongoing challenge. He explained that although the Department had budgeted approximately R658 million over the past three financial years, midterm budget cuts had resulted in a shortfall of over R280 million. This had created difficulties, as transport services had already been committed and could not easily be reduced. The matter was being actively discussed with the Provincial Treasury, the Office of the Premier, and National Treasury, with the hope of securing additional funding.

MEC Hlomuka clarified that the Department had not returned any funds due to underperformance and had, in fact, received additional allocations based on its infrastructure performance.

Regarding planning, he said the Department operated with an approved APP, while also conducting medium term planning over the period of three years. He acknowledged that any reference to a five-year plan may have been an error.

On stakeholder engagement, he raised the issue of school governing bodies, commenting that there was a need to clarify whether their activities should be included in departmental targets. He explained that targets must be measurable and within the Department’s control, but indicated that further engagement with the national Department would be undertaken, guided by the Portfolio Committee.

In conclusion, he assured Members that concerns raised about specific schools would be followed up on. He emphasised that circuit managers operated under the authority of district directors and ultimately reported to the HOD, ensuring accountability for resolving issues.

The Chairperson thanked the MEC for the clarification, noting that upon revisiting the slide, she now understood that the infrastructure issue related to the 2025/26 period, while new indicators had been introduced for the 2026/27 financial year. This clarification alleviated her earlier frustration, which had stemmed from observing the extent of the need.

She asked the HOD to respond to the specific questions that had been raised. She highlighted, in particular, the concern shared by Members regarding a case in which a learner had been discriminated against on the basis of sexual orientation, emphasising that this matter required attention.

She indicated that where the MEC might not have immediate access to specific figures, it would be acceptable for those details to be submitted in writing at a later stage.

HOD's response

Mr Ngcobo thanked the Chairperson and Members for their questions and for the clarity of their contributions. He noted that most of the issues raised had already been addressed in the summary response provided earlier. He indicated that with regard to specific schools that had been named, the Department was attending to those matters through ongoing investigations.

In response to Mr Mkize, he said that the Department would review the communication previously sent, while also acknowledging that some of the issues raised were new. He committed that the Department would provide detailed, issue by issue written responses, as addressing each matter verbally would be time consuming.

Discrimination based on sexual orientation

He said that the Department would respond in writing to the case of a learner who was reportedly discriminated against on the basis of sexual orientation. He confirmed that the matter, which had been raised earlier by a Committee Member, was under active investigation. He said he was personally overseeing follow-ups with the district, which had been tasked with investigating the matter, and that he was receiving regular updates. He had instructed that the full report be finalised within the week so that there would be clarity on the matter when schools reopen. Once concluded, a formal response would be provided to the Member who had submitted correspondence.

On the issue of infrastructure baselines and the basis for APP targets, he indicated that the Department would provide a written explanation, including the sources of their data and how these aligned with the financial resources available for each financial year.

Regarding infrastructure spending, he reiterated the MEC’s earlier response, emphasising that the Department had not underspent its infrastructure budget to the extent of returning funds to the National Treasury. On the contrary, over the past five years, the Department had consistently received additional allocations due to its strong spending performance.

He asked a member of the Department to address any remaining issues not covered in the earlier responses, particularly those related to infrastructure.

Ms Weziwe Hadebe, Deputy Director-General (DDG): Institutional Development and Support, reported that the Department had allocated an implementing agent for the Macambini area in Ilembe, and that significant work had already been undertaken. For the particular school in question, new classrooms would be added, existing structures refurbished, and new administrative facilities provided. She noted that the tender process was anticipated to be completed by the end of September, and that a detailed management plan outlining other activities and milestones would be submitted in writing, as indicated by the HOD.

Budget allocation and funding

Regarding Babanango School, she acknowledged and accepted the issues raised by Members concerning the school’s condition, but explained that no funds had been allocated due to budget cuts in the equitable share. The programme had relied on the Accelerated Infrastructure Grant (AIG), and a new school would be constructed by the Department of Public Service and Administration (DPSA), with construction tenders, including classrooms and administrative blocks, scheduled to be advertised on 17 September. She added that a written management plan would follow for Committee Members.

On the matter of disaster-related funding, she explained that the Department’s plans had been guided by the timing of the allocation, which occurred in November. Regarding partnerships in the bullying programme, she confirmed that the Department worked closely with the government school development team, the staff, the Department of Justice, civil society, and the South African Depression and Anxiety Group (SADAG), noting that such partnerships had helped mitigate potential increases in cases.

She addressed questions on learner transport, noting that the MEC had already provided coverage, and confirmed that under the National School Meal and Welfare Programme (NSMWP), there were 1 765 service providers according to the latest forecast. She clarified that repairs to schools affected by storms had already been completed where applicable.

Regarding Xikamwane Primary School, she reported that interim interventions had provided four classrooms to prevent disruption to teaching and learning, while the implementing agent prepared procurement documents to begin construction in the next financial year. She added that the province did not have technical schools in most rural and township areas, but mentioned technical schools in Harry Gwala and Tugela as examples.

She said that approximately 2.5 million learners, representing 87.5% of the learner population, were benefiting from the NSNP across 5 390 schools. She also reported that the finalisation of the remaining 54 facilities had been delayed due to contractor corrections, but was expected in the first quarter of the next financial year.

She emphasised that monitoring and management of infrastructure programmes were supported by a comprehensive plan, including internal programme project managers, district works inspectors, and professional teams from implementing agents. She said the Department conducted institutional analysis when planning infrastructure, taking into account enrolment trends, school utilisation, curriculum packages, migration patterns, and district prioritisation processes. She said detailed responses to other questions would be provided in writing through the Secretary.

North West Province

MEC Motsumi said concerns about the alignment of the budget to the APP had been considered in the Department’s presentation, and that the budget-informed targets had been adjusted to ensure compensation and non-personnel expenditure ratios were in line with available funds, to avoid failing to achieve targets. She confirmed that the budget was aligned with the APP and that some targets had been reduced accordingly.

Underspending and sanitation

Responding to questions on SCM and Unauthorised, Irregular, Fruitless and Wasteful (UIFW) issues, she said the Department’s post-audit action plan included strengthened monitoring and evaluation, with SCM improvements in place, and that detailed plans with timeframes for implementation throughout the financial year would be submitted for Members' review.

She also explained the quintile system for schools in the province, noting that quintiles one to three covered non-fee paying public ordinary schools benefiting from the NSNP, with 751 847 learners accounted for as beneficiaries. She pointed out that these learners received both breakfast and lunch, in contrast to some other provinces, where breakfast provision remained a challenge, and that there were 1 298 such schools in the province.

On the issue of sanitary products, she confirmed that 102 464 learners in the province were benefiting from the provision of sanitary towels.

Regarding findings by the AG, she said the Department had developed and implemented standard operating procedures and strengthened oversight, which had helped to clear previous audit findings. She acknowledged that scholar transport was the responsibility of the Department of Community Safety and Transport Management (COSATMA), and that the Education Department submits lists only of learners in need of transport. Decisions on the numbers accommodated depended on COSATMA’s budget and capacity, meaning the Department could not directly control transport provision.

MEC Motsumi continued by responding to the issue regarding how many schools needed to be built in the province. She indicated that they had a Table B-5, which was a register listing the number of schools requiring construction, based on needs on the ground. She clarified that this did not necessarily mean that all those schools would be built at once, as it would depend on the available budget or grant. Once the grant was received and estimates were made, they could determine, for that financial year, how many schools -- perhaps five or six -- they were ready to build. She added that this was when procurement would take place, including advertising and other processes. Finally, she emphasised that Table B-5 was not stagnant.

She noted that the North West Province, being predominantly rural, provided technical education through several schools located in rural communities. Specifically, she confirmed that there were six technical schools in the north west area, which consisted mostly of townships rather than villages.

Regarding sanitation, she acknowledged that some schools in the province still used pit latrines. However, this did not necessarily indicate a lack of proper sanitation. She explained that challenges such as water shortages and underground water issues sometimes necessitated the use of pit latrines. She emphasised that the Department was committed to replacing pit latrines because they posed risks to learners. She also clarified confusion around ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrines, which were often used in areas with underground water challenges.

Contract management and ECD

On contract management, she reported that 114 schools affected by storm damage had contractors on site, and all processes were being followed to ensure compliance with the AG's audits. She highlighted that the Department often relied on support from mines and other private sector partners to supplement the provincial budget in school construction projects.

She said some schools faced ongoing challenges due to limited budgets. She explained that prioritisation decisions were necessary -- for example, providing mobile classrooms for immediate needs took precedence over constructing fully equipped science labs for very small numbers of learners. She assured the Committee that while improvisations such as visual labs using projectors were currently in use, proper science labs would be built in the future as funds allowed.

She confirmed that 500 registered ECD centres existed across the province, with 65 000 children accessing these programmes. Of these, 57 832 children received ECD subsidies. She stressed ongoing efforts to improve compliance among ECD programmes so that more children could benefit. According to Statistics SA, around 30% of children in the province did not currently access ECD, and the Department was actively working to reduce this gap.

She also addressed the cost per learner for sanitary provisions, which was R45 per quarter. Regarding school governing bodies (SGBs), she clarified that the 60% functionality target referred to SGBs recently elected or assisted -- some SGB members were serving multiple terms and did not require new capacity building workshops. Nonetheless, the expectation was that every SGB in each school should be functional, as they were responsible for governing schools.

Chairperson's closing remarks

The Chairperson thanked the MEC and the departmental team for their presentations and responses, noting that all questions had been addressed. She acknowledged the attendance and participation of everyone present, and requested that any follow-up questions be submitted in writing. Emphasising the seriousness of bullying, she urged the Departments to strengthen their interventions.

The meeting was adjourned
