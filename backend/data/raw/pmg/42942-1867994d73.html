Textbook procurement for the Foundation Phase (Grade 1-3); with Ministry

Basic Education

12 May 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committee on Basic Education met to consider urgent concerns raised regarding the Department of Basic Education’s (DBE) development, implementation, and release of the Foundation Phase (Grades 1–3) National Catalogue for learning and teaching support materials, amid allegations of governance weaknesses, procurement irregularities, and risks to transparency and accountability.

Members welcomed the Department’s delegation, including the Minister, Deputy Minister, Director-General (DG), and senior officials, and expressed concern about the late submission of documentation and the need for stronger preparedness.

The central focus of the meeting was the Foundation Phase National Catalogue, which had generated significant public and parliamentary concern. Members raised extensive questions about whether the catalogue process effectively functioned as a procurement mechanism, the legality of National Treasury deviations, and the adequacy of governance safeguards. Concerns were also raised about the reduction of approved textbook titles from eight to three, potential market concentration among publishers, the role and independence of subject advisors, and the transparency of the “blind screening” evaluation process. Members further questioned the involvement and due diligence of entities such as Lighthouse, beneficial ownership structures, and the adequacy of internal controls, audit processes, and conflict-of-interest safeguards.

The Minister of Basic Education stressed her constitutional oversight role, clarifying that she did not participate in procurement but was responsible for ensuring accountability and lawful processes. She stated that concerns had been escalated to National Treasury and internal audit structures, and that preliminary findings warranted further scrutiny. She denied allegations of interference or external relationships with implicated entities and emphasised that her actions were aimed at safeguarding compliance and protecting provincial Departments from potential irregular expenditure.

After extensive deliberation, Members raised continued concerns about transparency, governance integrity, procurement classification, transformation in the publishing sector, and the sufficiency of oversight mechanisms. Several Members called for full disclosure of documentation, independent forensic investigations, suspension of implementation pending review outcomes, and clarification of financial implications and market impacts.

The Chairperson concluded that the Committee lacked sufficient clarity on key aspects of the process and supported further independent investigation. The Committee resolved to prepare a draft referral to the Office of the Public Protector and to consider a separate referral to the Public Service Commission (PSC) regarding recruitment-related concerns. It was further agreed that the Committee would await outstanding reports from National Treasury and the Internal Audit Unit before taking further decisions.

The meeting concluded with the Chairperson emphasising the need for improved departmental coordination, clearer communication, and protection of public confidence in the education system.

Meeting report

The Chairperson welcomed Members, the Department of Basic Education (DBE) delegation, support staff and stakeholders. She noted that the meeting had been convened urgently following requests from three Members of the Committee for the Department to account and clarify issues raised in the media.

She informed Members that the Committee had secured a full-day programme in order to accommodate both the urgent engagement with the Department and the scheduled afternoon session with the Education Labour Relations Council (ELRC) on its preliminary headcount report. Members were urged to be concise in their engagements due to time constraints and the Department’s scheduled appearance before the National Council of Provinces (NCOP) sitting later in the day.

She also stated that the Committee was worried about the impact of the developments on public confidence in the Department and on the education sector generally. She stressed the importance of stable leadership and cohesion within the Department to ensure focus on core mandates such as curriculum delivery, infrastructure challenges and learner support. The Committee further raised concern about reports relating to the Foundation Phase National Catalogue and the possible impact on the timely provision of textbooks to learners.

The Chairperson appealed to the Minister and Deputy Minister to address leadership challenges within the Department and restore confidence in its operations. She also criticised the late submission of presentations to the Committee, noting that presentations should be submitted at least five days before meetings to allow Members adequate time to prepare and engage meaningfully with the material.

MrLlewellyn Brown, Committee Secretary, conducted the roll call and confirmed attendance. An apology was received from Ms L Ngobeni (Action SA), who was attending a Home Affairs Committee meeting and would join the afternoon session.

Consideration of the draft minutes

The Committee proceeded to consider the draft minutes of its previous meeting. The Chairperson invited amendments and corrections. The minutes were duly adopted.

Report of the Portfolio Committee on Basic Education on Budget Vote 16: Basic Education

The Committee proceeded to consider the recommendations contained in its Budget Vote Report. The Chairperson reminded Members that the report would form the basis of the Committee’s Budget Vote process and subsequent tabling before the Announcements, Tablings and Committee Reports (ATC). Members were encouraged to ensure that they had thoroughly engaged with the contents of the report.

The Committee focused specifically on the recommendations section, and the Chairperson invited Members to propose any amendments or areas for improvement. No amendments or objections were raised.

The Budget Vote Report was duly adopted by the Committee. See full report here https://pmg.org.za/tabled-committee-report/6595/

Following the adoption of the report, the Chairperson invited the Minister of Basic Education and the departmental delegation to proceed with the presentation on the development, implementation and release of the new National Foundation Phase Catalogue.

Opening remarks by the Minister of Basic Education

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, addressed the Committee and apologised for the late submission of the Department’s presentation. She explained that the invitation to the urgent meeting had only been received on the preceding Thursday, which limited the Department’s preparation time.

The Minister welcomed the opportunity to appear before the Committee to address concerns relating to the development, implementation and release of the National Catalogue for Foundation Phase Grades 1 to 3. She acknowledged that the matter had attracted significant public concern and media attention, raising questions about the lawfulness, fairness, credibility and defensibility of the process followed by the Department.

The Minister emphasised the importance of the Foundation Phase in shaping the future of the education system, stating that the quality of learning and teaching support materials directly affected literacy, numeracy, and long-term learner outcomes. She stressed that the matter could not be viewed as a minor administrative issue, as it related to the broader objective of improving foundational learning in South Africa.

Clarifying her role, the Minister stated that Ministers should not be directly involved in procurement processes, including the selection of publishers or textbooks. She explained that her responsibility was to exercise executive oversight by asking questions, ensuring accountability, and intervening where concerns arose. She indicated that she had questioned the DG on the lawfulness, fairness, educational soundness and defensibility of the process, and had also raised concerns relating to potential financial risks to provinces should the procurement process later be found to be flawed.

The Minister informed the Committee that some concerns had been escalated to National Treasury and that the Department’s internal audit structures had been engaged to assess aspects of the process. She stated that preliminary internal audit processes had identified concerns requiring further scrutiny but cautioned that these findings remained subject to the Department’s governance and assurance processes before any conclusions could be reached.

She maintained that any allegations relating to procurement irregularities, governance failures, or educational shortcomings had to be treated seriously and investigated thoroughly. According to the Minister, oversight required scrutiny and accountability rather than silence where concerns arose.

The Minister further raised questions regarding whether the process had appropriately balanced cost and quality, whether genuine choice for teachers and schools had been unduly limited, and whether sufficient safeguards relating to compliance, fairness and integrity had been applied throughout the process. She also highlighted the potential financial and audit risks that provinces could face if the process was later found to be materially flawed.

The Minister stressed that, irrespective of the outcome of the ongoing scrutiny, the Department still had a responsibility to ensure that learners received the required learning and teaching support materials for the 2027 academic year.

Concluding her remarks, the Minister stated that she was not before the Committee to shield the Department from scrutiny or provide political cover for any weaknesses in the process. Instead, she said her objective was to ensure that accountability was enforced and that the Committee received full and transparent answers. She reiterated that decisions affecting the country’s 13.7 million learners had to be made in the best interests of learners and with full public confidence.

The Minister thereafter handed over to the DG to lead the Department’s presentation and respond to Members’ questions.

DBE Presentation: The Development and Release of the National Foundation Phase Catalogue

DG’s Introductory remarks

Mr Mathanzima Mweli, Director-General, Department of Basic Education, greeted the Chairperson, Members of the Committee, the Minister and the Deputy Minister. He apologised for experiencing technical difficulties in activating his camera and indicated that he would continue attempting to resolve the issue during the meeting.

He thanked the Minister for her introductory remarks and indicated that, without delay, the Department would proceed with its presentation. He informed the Committee that the Department had included the relevant line-function officials responsible for the development of the national catalogue, who would lead the presentation.

He further advised the Committee that the Chairperson of the Audit Committee and the Head of Internal Audit were also present during the meeting to assist with any questions relating to governance and audit matters.

The DG explained that only one presentation would be made during the session because the internal audit process had not yet been finalised. He stated that the Internal Audit unit had indicated that its report was still incomplete and therefore not yet ready for consideration by the Committee. However, he noted that the report could be made available to the Committee at a later stage once the relevant governance processes had been concluded, as previously indicated by the Minister.

Mr Mweli concluded by confirming that the Department’s presentation on the development, implementation and release of the new National Foundation Phase Catalogue would proceed under the leadership of the responsible line-function officials, while the audit structures remained available to respond to any questions arising from the engagement.

Presentation by the Department of Basic Education

Mr Seliki Tlhabane, Chief Director: Mathematics, Science, and Technology (MST) and Curriculum Enhancement Programmes, DBE, briefed the Committee on the development, implementation and release of the new National Foundation Phase Catalogue for Grades 1 to 3. He indicated that the presentation sought to provide the Committee with the background, rationale, methodology, and implementation process relating to the catalogue.

The Department stated that the initiative was aligned with the objectives of the National Development Plan (NDP) 2030 and the sector plan, both of which emphasised improved access to quality learning and teaching support materials (LTSM). According to the Department, the new catalogue was developed as a strategic intervention aimed at promoting equity, efficiency, and improved quality in the provision of educational materials.

The presentation outlined the historical development of textbook procurement in South Africa, noting that prior to 1994, the country had 19 separate education Departments with fragmented procurement systems. Following the establishment of the Department of Basic Education and provincial education Departments, procurement remained a provincial competency, resulting in continued inconsistencies and inefficiencies in the provision of LTSM.

The Department explained that a ministerial task team had previously recommended that the DBE take responsibility for the development of national catalogues in order to centralise the evaluation of learning materials. Earlier catalogues focused primarily on quality and did not consider pricing, which the Department said contributed to high procurement costs.

Reference was made to the 2012 Limpopo textbook crisis, during which the DBE intervened and negotiated reduced prices with publishers, resulting in significant cost savings. The Department argued that this demonstrated the benefits of a more centralised and coordinated approach to textbook procurement.

The Committee was informed that the Foundation Phase catalogue currently in use had originally been developed in 2011 and was intended to be reviewed in 2014 and again in 2017. However, these reviews did not occur, resulting in the continued use of a 14-year-old catalogue. The Department indicated that the new Foundation Phase catalogue had now been finalised and that preparations were underway for the development of catalogues for the Intermediate Phase, Senior Phase and Further Education and Training (FET) Phase.

The Department provided a comparative analysis of the old and new catalogues. It stated that the new catalogue introduced price as an evaluation factor alongside quality considerations. According to the Department, this had resulted in substantial cost reductions. An example provided was that a Grade 1 reader, which previously cost approximately R55 under the old catalogue, now costs approximately R16 under the new system.

The Department further explained that the previous catalogue allowed up to eight titles per category, while the new catalogue limited approved titles to three. The evaluation process under the new catalogue relied primarily on subject specialists within the education sector, rather than external experts, partly due to financial constraints.

The presentation also addressed concerns regarding market concentration among publishers under previous catalogues. The Department stated that several publishers operated under common ownership structures, resulting in dominant market shares for certain companies.

Members were informed that National Treasury had approved a deviation from standard procurement procedures in 2017 due to the specialised and complex nature of textbook catalogue development. The approval was granted on condition that the principles of fairness, equity, transparency, competitiveness and cost-effectiveness, as required by section 217 of the Constitution, would be upheld. The Department indicated that strict record-keeping processes had also been required.

The Department emphasised that the catalogue process did not constitute a conventional tender process. Instead, the catalogue functioned as a list of approved materials from which provincial education Departments would make final procurement decisions. It stated that the process was open to publishers, non-governmental organisations (NGOs), academics, and other contributors.

The Committee was informed that a “blind screening” process had been used during evaluations. Publishers were required to remove all identifying information from submissions so that evaluators could assess materials anonymously. Approximately 150 officials from all nine provinces participated in the evaluation process, using standardised rubrics to assess compliance with the Curriculum Assessment Policy Statements (CAPS) requirements, age appropriateness, and educational value.

The Department explained that all materials meeting the required quality standards were subsequently evaluated on price, with the three most affordable qualifying titles being included in the catalogue. It described this process as objective and free from subjectivity.

The Department also addressed public allegations relating to the company Lighthouse and its relationship with Class Act, an NGO involved in educational material development. It stated that both organisations had communicated with the Department to explain their respective roles and business arrangements. However, the Department indicated that it had not yet formally engaged with Lighthouse regarding these explanations.

The Committee was informed that the Minister had requested National Treasury to scrutinise the process, while the Department’s Internal Audit unit had initiated a preliminary quality assurance review. In addition, the DG had instructed that an independent investigation be conducted, not only into Lighthouse but into all entities included in the catalogue, to ensure fairness and consistency.

Regarding implementation, the Department stated that procurement by provinces had been temporarily placed on hold pending the completion of Treasury’s review. Procurement processes were now expected to take place between June and September 2026 to ensure that learning materials would be delivered before the start of the 2027 academic year.

The Department acknowledged that certain languages, including isiNdebele, Tshivenda, Xitsonga and Siswati, had insufficient submissions in some subjects such as Mathematics, Life Skills, Coding and Robotics. A supplementary call for submissions would therefore be issued to address these gaps.

The Committee was further informed that provincial education Departments had been advised on options for centralised procurement to achieve economies of scale and further cost reductions. The Department indicated that procurement and delivery processes would continue to be guided by the annual LTSM sector plan, which sets timelines for verification, ordering, delivery, and monitoring.

The Department outlined its communication strategy, which included media engagements, opinion pieces, informational videos, rapid-response communication teams and stakeholder consultations with provincial heads of Department, unions, school governing bodies, principals’ associations, the Auditor-General (AG) and other stakeholders. The Department concluded by requesting the Portfolio Committee to receive the presentation, engage on the issues raised and provide guidance on the way forward.

Concluding the Department’s presentation, the DG stated that the Department had previously been involved in the development and release of national textbook catalogues, but that the current process had attracted unprecedented public interest and scrutiny from stakeholders across the education sector.

He noted that concerns had emerged following media reports suggesting that the textbook catalogue process had been converted into a tender process. According to the DG, the Department had issued public statements to clarify that the development of the catalogue remained an expert-led educational process rather than a conventional tender system.

Mr Mweli emphasised that the process was driven by subject specialists and education professionals selected from all nine provinces. Approximately 150 evaluators participated in the screening process, with quality remaining the primary consideration. He stressed that learning materials first had to meet a minimum quality threshold of 70% before price considerations could be introduced.

The DG also explained that the Department had learnt lessons from previous catalogue processes, particularly the earlier system in which pricing had not been adequately considered. He argued that publishers had historically been able to charge significantly higher prices for textbooks and that the Department had sought to introduce greater cost-effectiveness without compromising educational quality.

Reference was made to the Department’s experience in developing mathematics and science textbooks in partnership with organisations such as Sasol and the Zenex Foundation, which the DG described as examples of high-quality educational materials that demonstrated that textbook development costs could be reduced while maintaining standards.

He further referred to the 2012 Limpopo textbook intervention, during which the Department negotiated reduced textbook prices with publishers. According to Mr Mweli, textbooks that had initially cost between R300 and R500 had, in some instances, been reduced to approximately R50. He stated that the Department’s current objective was similarly aimed at reducing the financial burden on taxpayers while ensuring quality educational resources for learners.

The Director-General acknowledged that the catalogue process had historically not been subjected to conventional procurement structures such as bid specification committees, bid evaluation committees (BECs) or bid adjudication committees (BACs). He stated that the Department had continued with this approach because previous catalogue processes had not encountered significant problems requiring a shift to a stricter tender-based model.

He informed the Committee that he had engaged with officials from National Treasury regarding the Department’s deviation from standard procurement procedures. According to the DG, National Treasury had confirmed that the Department’s earlier approval for deviation remained valid and that Treasury Instruction No. 3 of 2021/2022 allowed accounting officers discretion to determine which procurement processes could be exempted from standard tender procedures, provided that Treasury prescripts were followed.

The Director-General reiterated that the Department’s Internal Audit unit had been instructed to conduct a quality assurance review of the catalogue process. He stated that the Department retained the authority to remove any publisher or entity from the catalogue should irregularities or non-compliance be identified before provincial procurement processes commenced.

He concluded by thanking the Minister, Deputy Minister, Chairperson and Members of the Committee for the opportunity to present and engage on the matter.

Discussion

Ms D van der Walt (DA) welcomed the Minister's intervention following the emergence of allegations surrounding the Foundation Phase catalogue process. She remarked that where "there is smoke, there is fire". She argued that the seriousness of the matter justified the Minister's actions, particularly given the large amounts of public funds involved and the impact on millions of learners.

She noted the Department’s repeated emphasis on the quality of learning materials during the presentation. She added that quality concerns should also extend beyond textbooks to include other learning materials such as pencils and stationery, which in some schools and provinces were reportedly of poor quality.

She raised several questions regarding the procurement and approval process. She questioned the extent and scope of the National Treasury approval granted in the letter dated 3 March 2017, asking whether the approval related to the entire procurement process or merely constituted a limited exemption. She further referred to the subsequent Treasury instruction issued in 2021 and queried its relevance to the current process.

She asked whether all successful providers had complied fully with the terms of reference at the time of submission and whether any site inspections had been conducted to verify the delivery capacity of the entities involved.

She also questioned why the procurement model appeared to prioritise price over teacher choice and professional discretion in the classroom. In addition, she sought clarity regarding the relationship between Lighthouse and Class Act, asking whether this relationship had been transparently disclosed from the outset or whether it had only emerged after public scrutiny and media reports.

She also expressed concern that the presentation had focused extensively on historical background rather than the specific details of the current process under scrutiny. She proposed that the Committee should receive a detailed timeline setting out each stage of the process, including who presented what information and when each decision had been taken.

Lastly, she indicated that she intended to submit a written question requesting the names and qualifications of the approximately 150 officials involved in the evaluation process, as well as details of their subject expertise and professional backgrounds.

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) raised concerns regarding the integrity, transparency, and governance of the National Foundation Phase Catalogue process. While welcoming the Minister’s opening remarks and oversight approach, he argued that the Department should not minimise the seriousness of the process by distinguishing it from a tender process, given that it involved approximately R1.6 billion in public funds.

He contended that, although the Department described the exercise as a catalogue screening process, it effectively operated as a procurement mechanism because it ultimately resulted in publishers receiving substantial public contracts. He warned that the scale of the funding created opportunities for corruption, political patronage and undue influence, and stressed that the process should therefore be subjected to the same level of scrutiny as a conventional tender process.

Mr Mkhize questioned whether Lighthouse Publishers had, in fact, been awarded the contract and raised concerns regarding reports that the company had only been registered a few months before being selected. He asked what factors convinced the Department that a newly established company without a proven track record possessed the technical expertise, operational capacity, equipment, personnel and delivery capability required to undertake work of such magnitude. He further requested clarity on the due diligence undertaken by the Department before approving the company.

He also expressed concern about the possibility of conflicts of interest and political interference in the process. Referring to media reports alleging tensions between Minister Siviwe Gwarube and Director-General Mweli, Mr Mkhize asked whether the Minister had interfered in procurement matters and requested the DG to clarify the nature and extent of any alleged interference, as well as its impact on the functioning of the Department.

In addition, he questioned how the Department intended to safeguard educational quality while significantly reducing textbook costs. He cautioned that excessive focus on affordability could undermine the quality of learning materials and negatively affect learner outcomes and broader social development objectives. He therefore requested detailed explanations on the mechanisms used to ensure that reduced prices would not compromise educational standards.

Mr Mkhize concluded by urging the Department not to normalise existing procurement practices merely because they had been followed historically, arguing that weaknesses and risks identified in the system should be addressed decisively to prevent corruption and protect public confidence in the education sector.

Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) expressed serious concern regarding governance, transparency and accountability in the DBE’s catalogue process, arguing that public concern had escalated beyond worry to anger due to recurring controversies within the Department. He referred to previous incidents, including examination paper leaks and allegations involving Departmental officials, stating that the current controversy further undermined public confidence in the Department.

He argued that the matter before the Committee extended beyond administrative compliance by a single company and instead raised broader concerns about whether the Department had weakened transparency safeguards, concentrated approvals among fewer publishers, prioritised pricing over educational quality, and created conditions vulnerable to manipulation and unfair advantage. He further criticised the Department for being reactive rather than proactive, noting that serious scrutiny only appeared to intensify after media reports emerged.

Referring to sections 195 and 29(1)(a) of the Constitution, he argued that the catalogue process implicated constitutional principles of accountability, transparency, professional ethics and the right to basic education. He stressed that procurement decisions affecting foundational learning materials for millions of predominantly poor and working-class black learners required the highest level of constitutional scrutiny. He also cautioned against historical patterns of unequal educational provision being reproduced through cost-driven procurement decisions.

Addressing Minister Siviwe Gwarube directly, he asked when she first became aware of concerns relating to Lighthouse Publishers, who briefed her on the matter, and why the Department only appeared to intensify oversight after media exposure. He further questioned whether concerns had been raised internally regarding the revised catalogue model, particularly the reduction from eight approved titles to three, and the implications this could have for market concentration and procurement vulnerability.

He requested that the Minister provide the Committee with all correspondence between her office and the Department relating to the catalogue process, the Lighthouse matter, Treasury referrals, and ongoing investigations. He also requested disclosure of all adjudication records, scoring sheets, moderation reports, declarations of interest, evaluator comments, due diligence documents, and minutes related to the process.

In addition, he questioned whether the Department had verified Lighthouse Publishers’ operational, financial, warehouse, production and delivery capacity before approvals were granted, and whether enhanced scrutiny measures were applied, given that the company was reportedly newly established at the time of approval. He further requested disclosure of beneficial ownership structures, subcontracting arrangements and associated entities linked to approved publishers, as well as investigations into any links between publishers, politically connected individuals, officials or office bearers.

He proposed several recommendations for the Committee, including requiring the Department to submit all records related to the catalogue process within a fixed timeframe, requesting a direct briefing from National Treasury on its investigation methodology and timelines, and compelling disclosure of beneficial ownership structures and subcontracting arrangements linked to approved publishers. He further suggested that the Committee consider suspending implementation of the catalogue pending the outcome of oversight and investigative processes, while acknowledging concerns that delays could negatively affect learners if textbook delivery was disrupted.

He concluded by calling for a dedicated briefing on the educational rationale behind reducing approved titles from eight to three, particularly regarding the relationship between cost considerations and educational quality and reaffirmed that ministerial accountability remained applicable regardless of any Treasury investigations underway.

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) noted that many of her intended questions had already been covered by Mr Shikwambana, particularly regarding procurement concerns and oversight of the catalogue process.

She then raised issues:

On timing and basis of the independent investigation:

  • Why was an independent investigation only initiated after media reports emerged, rather than through internal proactive controls?
  • Whether this indicated weaknesses in internal governance and early warning systems.

On accountability for irregularities:

  • Who would be held personally accountable if the investigation confirmed irregularities in the process?

On the implementation of the catalogue system:

  • Whether the Department supported implementing or releasing the catalogue before the independent investigation was concluded.
  • What was the rationale for proceeding (or not proceeding) while oversight processes were still ongoing?

Ms P Mngadi (MKP) began by greeting the Chairperson, Minister, Deputy Minister, Departmental officials, and fellow members of the Committee. She grounded his remarks in the NDP, noting its objectives of improving access to quality early learning and achieving strong numeracy and literacy outcomes by Grade 4. She then highlighted what he described as a serious literacy crisis in the country, pointing out that a large proportion of Grade 4 learners were unable to read for meaning. She expressed concern about the long-term consequences of this deficit on learners’ academic progression and overall educational development.

She asked the Department to explain what shortcomings were identified in the previous textbook catalogue system and whether there was any evidence linking those shortcomings to the current literacy challenges. She further requested clarity on what improvements had been introduced in the new catalogue and how the Department intended to measure its impact on foundation phase learning outcomes. In addition, he sought to understand whether teachers were being adequately trained and supported to implement the new catalogue effectively.

Turning to procurement and cost issues, Ms Mngadi referred to the presentation’s indication that the new catalogue was significantly cheaper on average than the previous one. She asked what factors accounted for this reduction in cost, whether the previous catalogue might have been overpriced, and what specific changes in the procurement or design process led to the savings. She also requested an estimate of the total financial savings the government expected to realise and asked for clarity on the budget allocated for learner and teaching support materials under the current system.

She then raised concerns about market structure and competition, noting that major publishers such as Oxford and Macmillan appeared to continue to hold a dominant position in the sector. He asked how the Department ensured that such dominance did not restrict fair competition, and what measures were in place to mitigate the risks of over-reliance on a small number of large publishers. She also requested updated information on the current market share distribution under the new catalogue, particularly in comparison to the previous system, where historically advantaged publishers reportedly controlled the majority of the market. He further asked what role, if any, the Department played in supporting or developing publishers, especially those that were historically disadvantaged.

Concerns were raised regarding language diversity, with reference being made to supplementary calls for submissions in languages such as Venda, Xitsonga, and Siswati. She asked if targeted support for historically disadvantaged publishers could assist in improving the availability of learning materials in African languages and contribute towards more equitable access to quality educational resources. Questions were also raised about quality assurance in translation processes, specifically how the Department ensured that accuracy and meaning were preserved when materials were developed or translated into languages outside a publisher’s primary area of expertise. Further concerns were expressed regarding how the Department guaranteed that learners whose home language was not English would not be disadvantaged in comparison to English-speaking learners.

Ms Mngadi also posed questions regarding the sequencing of curriculum reforms, particularly in relation to the introduction of new focus areas such as coding and robotics. While the importance of digital transformation and future skills development was acknowledged, concern was expressed as to whether it was appropriate to prioritise these areas given the ongoing challenges relating to basic literacy and numeracy. She emphasised the importance of ensuring that foundational skills were adequately developed before more advanced subjects were introduced, so that learners were properly prepared for progression into higher phases of education.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) opened her remarks by greeting the Chairperson, members of the Portfolio Committee, the Minister, Deputy Minister, and officials from the DBE. She stated that her contribution would focus on concerns relating to procurement governance and the importance of strong political leadership that responded decisively and transparently when allegations of irregularities arose. She remarked that it was appropriate for the Committee to be convened on the matter and commended what she described as a proactive approach by the Minister in encouraging the National Treasury to investigate the allegations and ensure accountability where necessary.

Ms Jordaan further stressed that, while value for money was an important consideration, it should not come at the expense of quality. She accordingly requested that the Department explain how it had satisfied itself that the approved materials represented the best educational practice for the foundation phase literacy and numeracy. She also sought clarity on how "value for money" had been assessed, specifically whether the evaluation had been based solely on the lowest price or whether it had also taken into account educational impact relative to expenditure, including how quality had been measured beyond cost considerations.

Turning to issues of internal oversight and control, Ms Jordaan asked whether the Department’s internal audit processes had identified any weaknesses in compliance or control mechanisms during their preliminary findings, and whether there had been instances where controls were absent, inadequate, or inconsistently applied. She requested that the Committee be briefed on these preliminary findings. She additionally raised concerns regarding fiscal risk, particularly the possibility of irregular expenditure for provinces should the process later be found to be non-compliant. In this regard, she asked what the potential impact would be on provinces already experiencing financial constraints.

Ms Jordaan also referred to the submission fee typically charged to publishers per title submitted for evaluation, which was ordinarily intended to cover evaluation costs. She questioned why this fee appeared to have been removed in the current process, who had authorised the decision, and what the fiscal implications of that decision might be. Finally, she asked why the catalogue had been released before the Minister had formally seen or reviewed it. She sought clarity on the procedural sequence and accountability within the approval process.

Ms L Ngobeni (Action SA) began her contribution by apologising to the Chairperson for a brief technical disturbance on her side and confirmed that she would proceed despite the interruption. She stated that many of her concerns had already been raised by previous speakers, but that she nevertheless wished to seek clarity on three key issues.

Her first question related to procurement integrity and what she described as the principle of “blind screening” within the catalogue process. She noted that the so-called Lighthouse matter had been widely discussed in the media and referenced during the meeting. She asked the Department to explain how it reconciled its assertion that the process was entirely objective and free from subjectivity with the subsequent concerns that had emerged following evaluation, including issues relating to ownership structures, editorial partnerships, and possible indirect market consolidation. She further questioned what safeguards were in place to detect sophisticated forms of beneficial ownership concealment or corporate structuring prior to approval being granted, rather than only identifying such issues after public scrutiny or media reporting.

Her second question concerned the balance between cost reduction and educational quality. Referring to the discussion on significant price reductions in foundation phase learning materials, including reductions in unit costs and the narrowing of approved titles from eight to three, she asked whether the Department could provide empirical assurance that the drive for affordability would not unintentionally reduce educational diversity. She linked this concern to earlier contributions relating to linguistic diversity, teacher flexibility, and the needs of poorer schools that relied heavily on state-provided materials. She therefore sought clarity on how the Department ensured that cost-saving measures did not negatively affect the richness and quality of teaching and learning resources.

Her third and final question related to market dominance and transformation within the textbook publishing sector. She observed that, while the presentation had acknowledged historical consolidation within the sector and introduced measures relating to beneficial ownership disclosure, the current catalogue still appeared to be concentrated amongst a small number of dominant publishers. She therefore asked what measurable transformation indicators the Department was using beyond participation figures to determine whether the catalogue system was genuinely reducing historical market dominance, rather than merely redistributing procurement opportunities within the same established corporate ecosystem.

Ms Ngobeni concluded by stressing the importance of genuine transformation that would enable meaningful participation by new entrants while still maintaining quality and integrity within the process. She cautioned against insufficient scrutiny that could result in irregularities similar to those previously raised in relation to the Lighthouse matter. She noted that many of her concerns had already been addressed by earlier speakers and thanked the Chairperson.

Ms T Magagula (ANC) stated that she had a number of focused questions arising from the report before the Committee. Her first question related to the structural implications of reducing the number of approved textbook titles from eight to three. She asked how the Department intended to ensure that this reduction would not inadvertently create new monopolies or supply vulnerabilities within the system, particularly in circumstances where one of the approved suppliers failed to deliver on time. She requested clarity on the risk mitigation measures in place to prevent disruption in textbook delivery under such a concentrated supply model.

Her second question focused on transformation and inclusion within the publishing sector. She asked how many small, black-owned, or emerging publishers had been excluded from the final catalogue because of the price-based selection model, and what mechanisms existed within the Department to ensure that transformation objectives were protected alongside procurement efficiency.

Ms Magagula also referred to the issue of cost savings, noting the Department’s statement that the average cost of Grade 1 readers had decreased significantly from approximately R55–R60 to around R16. She asked what the total projected savings would be across all provinces over the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework period, including associated costs such as logistics, warehousing, and replacement expenses, in order to determine the real net fiscal impact of the new model.

She further raised concerns regarding indigenous language materials, specifically referencing languages such as Venda, Xitsonga, and Siswati. She noted that these materials appeared to remain incomplete at that stage and asked what assurances the Department could provide that learners using those languages would not be disadvantaged in 2027 due to delays or gaps in provision.

Ms Magagula acknowledged that the issue regarding the timing of the independent investigation had already been addressed by other Members and therefore did not pursue it further. She concluded by asking for the Department's position on whether the catalogue should be implemented before completion of the independent investigation. She requested that, should implementation proceed, the Department clearly explain the reasoning behind such a decision.

Mr S Louw (ANC) thanked the Chairperson and stated that, as the final speaker, he intended to raise concerns relating to transparency and accountability within the process. He reminded the Committee of the President’s commitment to rooting out corruption and emphasised the importance of improving literacy and numeracy outcomes in the foundation phase. He expressed concern regarding the controversy surrounding the procurement of the National Catalogue. He questioned the Department’s repeated assertion that the catalogue process did not constitute a tender process, arguing that inclusion in the catalogue effectively determined access to substantial provincial procurement opportunities and therefore functioned as a gateway to significant public expenditure.

Mr Louw also raised concerns regarding perceived inconsistencies in public communication, particularly differences between media statements and the departmental presentation. He questioned whether the Minister had been properly briefed by the administration before engaging with the media. In addition, he sought clarity on the respective roles and understanding of the Minister, Deputy Minister, and Director-General regarding whether the catalogue process constituted a tender process, stating that these inconsistencies had contributed to public confusion.

He further questioned governance alignment within the Department and asked whether there were internal divisions or miscommunication affecting decision-making and public messaging. He called for clarity regarding the relationship between senior officials and various publishers referenced during the process and requested full disclosure concerning approvals and deviations, including whether Treasury authorisations dating back to 2017 remained applicable to the current catalogue process.

Mr Louw concluded by recommending that the Committee consider referring matters to oversight bodies, including the PSC and the Public Protector, for investigation into recruitment processes, departmental communications, and the conduct of the Minister, Deputy Minister, and DG in relation to the catalogue process. He stated that, in his view, the seriousness of the allegations and inconsistencies warranted urgent independent scrutiny in order to restore clarity and public confidence.

The Chairperson indicated that the Department would be afforded an opportunity to respond, but first provided a summary of the concerns raised by Members. Concern was expressed that the Committee might conclude the meeting without obtaining the level of detailed clarity required regarding the development, implementation, and release of the Foundation Phase National Catalogue.

It was noted that Members had consistently requested specific information, including clarity on who had been involved in the process, the role and timing of National Treasury correspondence and exemptions, how the process had been initiated, and which entities and individuals had participated at various stages. The Chairperson stated that, while the presentation had provided some contextual information, it nevertheless fell short of the detailed and structured information necessary for effective parliamentary oversight and decision-making.

The Chairperson further expressed support for the proposal made by Members that the matter should be formally referred for independent investigation, specifically to the Office of the Public Protector. It was explained that such a referral would assist the Committee in obtaining a comprehensive and authoritative report on the process, including issues relating to procurement compliance, the “blind screening” methodology, the role and credibility of subject advisors, and broader governance concerns raised during the meeting.

The Chairperson clarified that, although the Committee would still receive responses from the Department, an independent investigation was considered necessary to properly substantiate the facts and guide further oversight work. It was further emphasised that scrutiny extended collectively to the Minister, Deputy Minister, and DG, irrespective of administrative or Executive distinctions, and that accountability ultimately rested with the Executive Authority.

Further concern was expressed regarding the overall structure and integrity of the catalogue process, including issues relating to transparency, potential fragmentation within the Department, and public confidence in the procurement system. The Chairperson remarked that inconsistent communication and unresolved allegations of “meddling” had contributed to confusion and undermined confidence in the process.

The Chairperson concluded by reiterating that the Committee required a coherent and unified departmental position, together with a clear factual basis on which to proceed, and stated that, in his view, the matter warranted referral for formal investigation while still allowing the Department an opportunity to respond to outstanding questions.

Ms van der Walt attempted to raise a procedural point regarding the manner in which the Committee should handle responses and follow-up engagement. She indicated that, given the length of the discussions and interventions by Members, it was important to ensure that once responses had been provided, the Committee adopted a structured and fair process for dealing with them.

Although her statement was interrupted mid-sentence, it appeared that she was raising concerns relating to the need for an orderly process for managing Departmental responses and ensuring that Members were afforded an adequate opportunity to engage with answers provided during the meeting.

The Chairperson responded by noting that Ms van der Walt had initially chosen voluntarily to keep her remarks brief and that no formal time limitation had been imposed on Members during the initial round of questions. It was explained that other Members had utilised the opportunity to ask extensive questions and that this had not resulted from any restriction imposed by the Chairperson. She was nevertheless permitted to proceed with a follow-up question, although she was requested to keep it brief.

Ms van der Walt then asked a focused follow-up question, seeking clarity on whether any deviations from approved processes had been identified during the preliminary review and whether the internal audit had found instances of absent, inadequate, or inconsistently applied controls. She further requested clarity on when the Committee and the Minister would receive the full report before indicating that she would leave the matter there.

The Chairperson then invited the Minister to respond.

Responses

Response by the Minister

Minister Gwarube began by responding to concerns about her role and involvement in the matter, particularly the suggestion that she was distancing herself from the Department. She clarified that her approach was grounded in the importance of each actor in government understanding and fulfilling their constitutional and legal responsibilities. She referenced the lessons of state capture, arguing that it was enabled in part because political leaders failed to properly exercise oversight and understand their roles. In her view, her responsibility as Minister was to lead the Department together with the Deputy Minister and DG, but also to ensure that when there were processes she was not fully convinced about, she was entitled, indeed obliged. to seek independent assurance that those processes were properly followed.

The Minister explained that the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA) established a clear separation between the roles of the executive authority and the accounting officer, and stated that her request for oversight or external review was not to be interpreted as interference or conflict with the Department. Rather, she described it as part of the checks and balances required by law. She stressed that she did not participate in procurement processes, which were the responsibility of the accounting officer, but that as the executive authority she was still required to account for outcomes, particularly in matters involving large-scale procurement affecting millions of learners. She emphasised that her actions were motivated by the scale of responsibility involved and stated that the futures of 13.7 million children depended on the integrity of such processes.

The Minister further addressed concerns relating to allegations of “meddling”, arguing that seeking an independent internal audit or requesting the Minister of Finance and the Chief Procurement Officer to verify compliance did not constitute interference, but rather responsible oversight. She indicated that preliminary internal audit findings had already identified areas of concern, which in her view justified further scrutiny. She rejected suggestions that her actions reflected a breakdown in the relationship between herself and the Department, insisting instead that they demonstrated proper accountability mechanisms at work.

Addressing the question of timing and awareness of the allegations, the Minister stated that she had not only become aware of concerns after media reports had emerged. She explained that both she and the Department of Basic Education had previously received allegations from the Publishers Association of South Africa, which had prompted an earlier forensic investigation that had not conclusively found corruption. The process had therefore continued. She added that she had personally requested to review the catalogue, but that it had already been released to provinces by the time she became aware that it had been finalised. She stated that it was only once media reports emerged that she became aware there was a broader public controversy requiring urgent attention.

The Minister also responded to concerns that the matter was being “prosecuted through the media”, stating that she had not initiated media engagement and that many of the questions she had responded to originated from press inquiries. She suggested that the emergence of the story had not been under her control and stated that she could not definitively explain how the information had entered the public domain.

She further explained that once concerns regarding the integrity of the process had become clear, she escalated the matter to the Minister of Finance and engaged the Chief Procurement Officer in order to seek an independent assessment. She indicated that correspondence exchanged between herself, the Director-General, and the Minister of Finance could be made available to the Committee if required. However, she noted that the Committee was still awaiting the formal findings of the Chief Procurement Officer and that the internal audit process remained ongoing. She added that she would allow the internal audit team to outline their role and process in order to provide further assurance to the Committee that the matter was being treated seriously and handled through the appropriate institutional channels.

Minister Gwarube then addressed a number of remaining questions, beginning with clarification regarding allegations about her alleged relationship with News24 and Via Africa. She categorically denied having any relationship with either entity and stated that she did not know Via Africa or the individuals connected to the matter.

She then turned to concerns that ongoing scrutiny of the catalogue process could result in delays to textbook procurement and delivery. Responding to remarks suggesting that she appeared “panicked” in her capacity as both a parent and a lawmaker, she rejected the suggestion that her actions were intended to delay implementation. Instead, she explained that her concern was centred on ensuring that provinces did not proceed with procurement based on a process that might later be found to be flawed, thereby exposing them to adverse audit findings and financial risk. She stressed that her objective was to protect provincial departments and ensure compliance with proper procedures.

She further stated that the Department was awaiting completion of the internal audit process, as well as guidance from National Treasury, before provinces could proceed with procurement. She indicated that once these processes had been concluded and the necessary assurance had been provided, provinces would be in a position to proceed in a compliant manner. She stressed that the Department remained within the appropriate timeframe to ensure delivery of books by January 2027, but cautioned that due process needed to be followed in order to avoid future irregularities or consequences.

Finally, she addressed the broader question raised by members regarding whether the catalogue process constituted a tender or a procurement process. She stated that she would defer to the Director-General for a technical explanation of the distinction, but explained that this was precisely the issue that had been under clarification with National Treasury. She stated that her position was that she required formal confirmation from Treasury that the classification and procedures applied to the process were correct and compliant. She added that only once such assurance had been received would she have been comfortable allowing the process to proceed fully. She concluded by inviting the Deputy Minister and DG to provide further input before proceedings continued.

Response by the Deputy Minister

Dr Reginah Mhaule, Deputy Minister of Basic Education, began by acknowledging the Minister's remarks and greeting the Chairperson and Members of the Portfolio Committee. She expressed regret that the situation had escalated to the point of requiring such intense scrutiny, noting that the matter had originally been requested by the Portfolio Committee as far back as March. She suggested that had it been addressed earlier, the current level of controversy and procedural escalation might have been avoided. She reiterated that the Department had been prepared to present on the matter when it was first requested.

She then urged that the various ongoing processes be allowed to proceed, particularly the engagements between the Minister and National Treasury. However, she cautioned that any delay in receiving outcomes from these processes could jeopardise the Department’s core objective of ensuring that learners, particularly in the foundation phase, have access to textbooks by January 2027. She stressed that this outcome remained a shared priority and warned against allowing investigative processes to derail timely delivery.

The Deputy Minister expressed support for the Chairperson’s proposal that the matter be referred for investigation, noting that such a step was appropriate given the concerns raised. At the same time, she emphasised that this should not halt procurement processes entirely, as doing so could have negative consequences for provinces and learners. She suggested that while investigations should proceed, procurement timelines should continue in parallel where possible, especially if certain findings or assurances from Treasury were received earlier.

She further reflected on the need to strengthen internal communication within the Department, stating that some of the challenges and perceived inconsistencies arose from insufficient coordination between officials and leadership. She emphasised that improved communication between the Minister, Director-General, and other senior officials would help ensure that issues were addressed internally before escalating into public controversy.

Finally, she reiterated that better internal coordination could prevent misunderstandings and reduce the risk of conflicting messaging. She concluded by expressing confidence that, with improved communication and continued engagement with oversight bodies, the Department would be better positioned to manage both accountability requirements and its service delivery obligations effectively.

Further responses by the Department

Mr Mweli explained the relationship between the Accounting Officer and the Executive Authority, describing it as separate but complementary, and noting that in practice, there were occasions where the Accounting Officer and the Minister might disagree. He emphasised that his role as head of administration was to focus on operational implementation, while the Minister provided political leadership, and that internal communications between them, such as warnings that boundaries might be crossed, were routine governance matters that were never intended for public circulation. He also addressed concerns regarding leaked communications, stating that such correspondence was internal, securely handled, and not sourced from his side.

He then turned to the broader controversy surrounding the textbook catalogue process, defending it as not a traditional “tender” but a more complex procurement arrangement authorised through a deviation approved by the National Treasury. He argued that involving the Minister too directly in the catalogue process would blur the separation of roles and potentially compromise procurement independence. He further noted that releasing detailed procurement materials widely could expose the Department to intellectual property disputes from publishers, and therefore, such materials were only made available for scrutiny under controlled conditions.

Finally, he addressed the implications of a possible Public Protector investigation, warning that it would delay procurement and consequently delay learners receiving textbooks. He also argued that claims of projected financial savings, such as R1.6 billion, were speculative, as school-level textbook choices and invoices had not yet been finalised. Regarding the structure of the catalogue itself, he explained the shift from eight titles to three as a deliberate strategy aimed at reducing market dominance, increasing participation by smaller and previously disadvantaged publishers, and lowering costs. He maintained that quality remained the dominant evaluation criterion, accounting for approximately 70%, with price considered thereafter. He concluded that the reforms were intended to improve access to textbooks while balancing affordability, quality, and market fairness, and he then invited Mr Tlhabane to assist in responding to the remaining questions.

Mr Tlhabane explained that the subject advisors used in the evaluation process were experienced curriculum specialists already responsible for supporting teachers and monitoring curriculum implementation. He indicated that these officials were carefully selected and regarded as highly competent. He further noted that safeguards were in place to manage conflicts of interest, including requiring any subject advisor who had contributed to the writing of textbooks to declare such involvement and recuse themselves from evaluation processes where applicable.

He stated that most publishing houses utilised teachers and subject advisors as writers, but emphasised that individuals involved in content development were excluded from evaluation panels to ensure impartiality. He also reiterated that only a selected group of highly experienced subject advisors were appointed for evaluation duties.

Mr Tlhabane further explained that the catalogue process was open to both established and new entrants in the publishing sector, and that the timing of company registration was not a determining factor. He argued that the process focused on the quality and compliance of submitted materials rather than the experience or history of the publisher. He added that experienced publishers were not the only source of quality materials, noting that some new entrants also produced high-quality submissions.

He further indicated that publishers were required to meet delivery obligations once included in the catalogue, and that failure to comply could result in corrective action, including possible delisting. He emphasised that monitoring and enforcement of delivery requirements occurred throughout the lifecycle of the catalogue, not only at the point of selection.

The presentation concluded with an indication that further responses on outstanding matters would be provided Mr S Sibiya.

Mr S Sibiya, Director: Learning and Teaching Support Materials (LTSM), thanked the Chairperson, Minister, Deputy Minister, and Members of the Committee, and indicated that he would briefly conclude on one aspect relating to the credibility of subject advisors.

He stated that the subject advisors appointed by the Department were the same professionals trusted with curriculum delivery and implementation. He explained that their responsibilities included day-to-day curriculum monitoring and providing support to teachers. He further noted that many publishing houses employed teachers and subject advisors as authors of textbooks. However, he emphasised that individuals who participated in the writing of textbooks were required not to serve on evaluation panels to avoid conflicts of interest. He indicated that declarations were required from subject advisors confirming any prior involvement in textbook development. He stressed that subject advisors were highly competent professionals and described the selected group as among the “cream of the crop,” appointed specifically for evaluation purposes.

He further responded to questions regarding Lighthouse’s registration, stating that the process had been an open call for both established and new entrants. He explained that the date of company registration had not been a determining factor in participation. He added that experience alone did not determine quality, as new entrants could also produce high-quality materials. He reiterated that evaluation was based strictly on the quality and compliance of submitted materials rather than the profile of the publisher.

He further explained that some experienced publishers had previously failed to deliver on commitments, and in such cases, corrective measures, including possible delisting, had been applied. She noted that delisting could occur at any stage of the catalogue cycle, including during implementation years one or two, depending on performance. He stated that the Department worked with industry associations to support delivery and ensure compliance throughout the lifecycle of the catalogue process.

The DG thanked the Chairperson, Minister, Deputy Minister, and Members of the Committee. He requested that the Chairperson and the Head of Internal Audit address issues raised by Members relating to quality assurance. He indicated that it would be appropriate for these matters to be dealt with by the relevant oversight and audit structures to provide clarity and assurance to the Committee.

Reponses by the Chairperson and the Head of Internal Audit

Prof Daniel van der Nest, Chairperson of the Audit Committee, greeted the Chairperson, Minister, Deputy Minister, DG, and officials from the Department.

He explained that the Audit Committee consisted of five independent members and indicated that the Committee had been engaged on the matter since it first arose. He further stated that a briefing meeting had been held with the AG in the preceding week.

He confirmed that the Audit Committee supported a proper investigation into the matter. He further noted that an internal audit process was currently underway within the Department, led by the Chief Audit Executive, Ms Mola, who was present in the meeting.

He explained that the internal audit process was not yet finalised, as preliminary findings still required engagement with management to obtain responses, explanations, and supporting evidence. He stated that an internal audit report would only be considered complete once management inputs and evidence had been incorporated.

He further indicated that the audit process covered a lengthy period of more than a year and required comprehensive population testing. He stated that the Audit Committee would monitor the process to ensure its full completion.

He added that, given the significance of the Lighthouse matter, a request for quotations (RFQ) process had been initiated to appoint an independent firm to conduct a specific review of that appointment, in addition to the internal audit process.

He emphasised that the Audit Committee would continue to exercise oversight and support the Department, the Minister, Deputy Minister, and DG in ensuring that a fair and transparent process had been followed in the development of the catalogue. He further stated that the audit would verify that all publishers included in the catalogue had undergone proper procedures and that no controls had been bypassed or undue influence exerted.

He noted that any findings of irregularity would be subject to appropriate consequence management, including removal of affected publishers or officials where necessary. He concluded by stating that the Audit Committee would continue to monitor the process independently and would report through the appropriate channels, including to the Minister where required, in terms of the Treasury Regulations.

The Minister thanked the Chairperson for the platform to respond to concerns and handed over to the chairperson.

Further discussion

The Chairperson thanked the Minister, Deputy Minister, Director-General, and the Departmental team for their responses.

She stated that, based on the responses received, she remained of the view that the Committee needed to conduct its own form of investigation. She noted that the Committee did not have the necessary capacity to undertake such an investigation independently and therefore supported referring the matter for further investigation.

She further indicated that, apart from concerns relating to the potential impact on the delivery of textbooks and the upcoming procurement processes, the matter required further discussion by the Committee.

The Chairperson noted that the Committee was awaiting various reports, including a report from the National Treasury regarding the deviation process, as well as the ongoing internal audit investigation into the Lighthouse matter. She added that Members were invited to provide input on the proposed way forward.

The Chairperson then opened the floor for follow-up questions, emphasising that these should be limited strictly to matters not yet addressed. She further ruled that follow-up questions would be limited to one minute per Member and should not introduce new issues but should relate directly to unanswered questions and the proposed way forward. She noted that only two Members had indicated a desire to speak.

Mr Mkhize stated that current practices were not absolute. He emphasised that the Committee should investigate the veracity of the deviation process, as it could expose the Department to risks of favouritism and corruption. He argued that the process should be treated as a tender process and noted that any deviation should be carefully scrutinised.

He clarified his earlier reference to registration timelines, stating that he had referred to a three-month registration period rather than a three-case registration period.

He further submitted that involving educational experts in the screening process did not eliminate the risk of corruption or favouritism, but rather primarily supported the quality and effectiveness of materials and improved educational outcomes. He stressed that the Committee’s main concern should be allegations of corruption.

Mr Mkhize further questioned the basis on which publishers were assessed and how confidence could be established in the capabilities of awarded publishers, given that subcontracting was common in the sector. He expressed concern about whether the awarding process adequately ensured quality assurance and effective control mechanisms.

He also raised concerns regarding the effectiveness of the so-called “blind screening” process, stating that content could potentially reveal the identity of the creator, and therefore the process might not be fully anonymous. He requested that the Department take these concerns into consideration.

Mr Louw said that the Committee should continue engaging relevant authorities in relation to the matter.

He proposed that a fully independent forensic investigation be conducted into the Lighthouse matter, including any related concerns regarding beneficial ownership and the disclosure of ownership structures of approved publishers.

He further recommended that a comprehensive financial impact assessment be undertaken to determine the actual savings across the full procurement chain over the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF) period, rather than relying solely on unit cost comparisons.

He added that the Committee should proceed to consolidate recommendations to identify the core issues, stating that the responses suggested possible fragmentation in Departmental relationships, including concerns that the Minister might not be fully adhering to administrative advice in the execution of processes.

Ms Jordaan thanked the Chairperson and addressed a question to the DG, requesting clarification on why provinces were advised to procure 37% from the cheapest supplier, 33% from the second cheapest supplier, and 30% from the third cheapest supplier, regardless of teacher choice and quality considerations, as referenced in the DG’s earlier response.

She further stated that she was confused by what appeared to be contradictory statements between the Chairperson of Internal Audit and Mr Tlhabane. She noted that Mr Tlhabane appeared to indicate that the preliminary internal audit report had found no defects, while the Chairperson had indicated that red flags had been identified.

Ms Jordaan requested clarity on whether Mr Tlhabane stood by his statement that no defects had been found. She further requested that the Committee be informed of the nature of any identified defects, or alternatively that such details be provided once the final audit report had been concluded.

The Director-General indicated that he was responding to concerns raised by Members, particularly Ms Jordaan. He explained that the Department’s approach was aimed at achieving greater value for taxpayers and was informed by constitutional and legislative prescripts, including Section 217 of the Constitution and Section 38 of the PFMA, with emphasis on cost-effectiveness.

He further stated that considerations relating to quality had been addressed within the Department’s framework.

The DG explained that deviations from recommended procurement approaches were permissible but required strong motivation and justification by accounting officers. He emphasised that such deviations would be subject to monitoring.

He expressed concern regarding historical practices in the sector, alleging that publishers had previously provided undue benefits to officials. He stated that the Department was seeking to prevent similar conduct from recurring.

He further indicated that there was a risk of publishers attempting to influence procurement decisions by approaching schools or provincial officials, particularly where more expensive titles were selected over cheaper alternatives offering comparable value. He stated that such actions would be inappropriate and would require strong justification if they occurred.

The Independent Chairperson of the Audit Committee further clarified that there was no final internal audit report at that stage.

He stated that, as indicated by the Minister, the Audit Committee had not yet received a completed internal audit report. He explained that while certain issues and concerns had been raised during the process, these remained preliminary in nature.

He further indicated that he had held informal discussions with the Chief Audit Executive due to the seriousness of the matter. He noted that additional documentation had been requested, which was not available at the time of the initial review, particularly in relation to certain publishers.

He explained that the internal audit process was still in its early stages and that the Chief Audit Executive had indicated that further audit procedures would be undertaken. These included additional verification of publisher experience, assessment of the quality of submitted titles, and review of evidence relating to the involvement of subject specialists, as well as checks aimed at identifying possible collusion.

He emphasised that the audit process was ongoing, and that only a few days had elapsed since its initiation. He stated that management would provide further information in the following week, while the audit process continued. He concluded by confirming that no final report had yet been issued, and that the matter remained under active investigation.

The Chairperson thanked Members and indicated that all follow-up questions had been addressed. She informed the Committee that a draft proposal would be prepared to refer the matter to the Office of the Public Protector. The draft referral would be circulated to Members for input and refinement to clearly define the scope of the investigation required.

She emphasised that the referral would be drafted in a manner that considered the urgency of ensuring that learners received textbooks by January of the following academic year, which was identified as a key concern for the Committee. She stated that the draft referral would be shared with Members for finalisation before formal submission.

Regarding outstanding reports, the Chairperson requested that the Department submit any pending reports to the Committee once finalised and ensure that the Committee was formally notified upon receipt so that such reports could be shared with Members. She thanked Members and confirmed that all follow-up questions had been responded to.

The Chairperson reiterated that the Committee would draft a proposed referral of the matter to the Office of the Public Protector. She stated that the draft referral would be circulated to Members for input and refinement, particularly to clearly define the scope of the investigation.

She emphasised that the referral would be developed in a manner that considered the urgent need to ensure that learners receive textbooks by January of the following academic year.

The Chairperson indicated that the draft proposal would be shared with Members for finalisation before submission.

The Chairperson then addressed a matter raised by Mr Louw regarding recruitment processes within the DBE. She recalled that he had previously committed to obtaining legal advice on the matter following earlier engagements with the Committee.

He reported that attempts had been made to consult the Parliamentary Legal Advisor; however, the legal team was not available due to workload constraints.

The Chairperson stated that she had engaged the Committee’s support staff, including the content advisor and researcher, to explore alternative approaches. She referenced a similar process followed by another parliamentary committee, which had referred a matter to the PSC for investigation. She noted that advice obtained from the support team indicated that referral to the PSC was a viable option.

The Chairperson therefore proposed that the Committee refer the matter concerning recruitment processes and alleged interference in the DBE to the PSC for investigation. He indicated that the PSC would be requested to investigate and report back to the Committee on the recruitment process and related appointments.

She invited Members to provide brief comments on the proposal. No objections were raised, and the Chairperson indicated that consensus had been reached.

Closing Remarks

The Chairperson thanked the Minister, Deputy Minister, Director-General, and Departmental officials for their participation.

She reiterated that the Committee expected submission of outstanding reports, including those from National Treasury and the final internal audit report. She indicated that a further sitting would be scheduled once the reports were available to consider their findings.

The Chairperson further advised the Department to strengthen internal communication and coordination to ensure effective institutional functioning. She stressed that differences within the Department should be managed professionally and ethically, in a manner that protects the credibility and public image of the institution. She cautioned that public disputes and media narratives involving senior officials negatively affected institutional confidence among stakeholders, including learners, parents, and school governing bodies.

The Chairperson encouraged the Department to resolve internal challenges constructively and to ensure that communication did not undermine public trust.

Closure of Session

The Chairperson indicated that the Committee would proceed to its next engagement with the Education Labour Relations Council (ELRC) at 14:00 to receive a report on the headcount.

She announced a short break of approximately 50 minutes before the next session.

The meeting of the first session was then adjourned.
