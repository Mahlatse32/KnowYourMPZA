Proposed Executive Ethics Code amendments; Review approach for Members' Conduct and Interests Disclosure; Public Participation Programme

Joint Committee on Ethics and Members’ Interest

29 May 2026
Chairperson: Mr J Britz (DA, Eastern Cape) & Ms N Mvana (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Joint Committee on Ethics and Members' Interests convened on 29 May 2026 to consider three main items: proposed amendments to the Executive Ethics Code, an overview of the review of Parliament's Code of Ethical Conduct, and a draft public participation programme.

The first presentation dealt with proposed amendments to the Executive Members' Ethics Act of 1998. Presenters from the Department of Justice and the Presidency outlined changes intended to modernise the existing framework. These included raising the gift and hospitality threshold from R1 000 to R2 500, a figure approved by Cabinet in October 2024 after years of delay since an earlier Cabinet decision in 2010. The amendments also introduced an electronic disclosure platform to replace manual declarations, incorporated requirements of the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA), expanded the definition of assets to include a broader range of vehicles, and brought the Code into compliance with a 2022 Constitutional Court judgment in the AmaBhungane case, which had found it unconstitutional for failing to require disclosure of donations to internal party leadership campaigns.

Discussion among Committee Members raised several gaps and ambiguities in the proposed amendments. Members questioned whether the Code adequately covered assets such as cryptocurrency, livestock, and trusts. Concern was also expressed about the lack of clarity on who authorised gifts received by the President or Premiers. A broader procedural question was raised about whether the executive ethics framework and the parliamentary ethics framework should be more closely integrated. However, legal advisers clarified that the constitutional design deliberately kept these two frameworks separate.

The second presentation provided an update on the review of Parliament's own Code of Ethical Conduct and Disclosure of Members' Interests for Assembly and permanent Council Members. The administration reported that a public comment process had been conducted between March and the end of March 2026, yielding nine written submissions. Key themes from those submissions included calls for clearer definitions, stronger penalties and complaints procedures, accountability beyond office tenure, expanded oversight, regulation of social media conduct, and the introduction of universal lifestyle audits.

The third item covered the draft public participation programme. Virtual public hearings were scheduled for 5 June 2026 on the Executive Ethics Code and 12 June 2026 on the Parliamentary Code, with both submission reports earmarked for adoption on 19 June 2026. Members emphasised the need for strong public communication around the hearings, given that Ethics Committee meetings were ordinarily held in private and these sessions represented an unusual opportunity for public engagement. The administration committed to circulating relevant documents and coordinating with the Department of Justice and the Presidency in preparation for the upcoming hearings.

The Chairperson stated that the Joint Committee on Ethics and Members’ Interests will undertake a comprehensive review of the conduct of both Members of Parliament and members of the Executive. The purpose of this review is to strengthen accountability and ensure that ethical standards are upheld.

Presentation of the Draft Proclamation: Executive Members' Ethics Act, 1998 (Act No. 82 of 1998) Amendment of the Executive Ethics Code

The presentation outlined the approach being taken to review Parliament's Code of Ethical Conduct and Disclosure of Members' Interests. The review is being conducted by the Joint Committee as part of its mandate under Joint Rule 161(1)(c). The Code was originally adopted in 1998 and amended in 2014 and 2024, with the current 2026 review representing a comprehensive revision process.

Key Issues

  1. Regular Review is Required

The presentation said that regulatory instruments such as ethics codes should be periodically reviewed to ensure they remain relevant and effective. The review is framed similarly to legislative scrutiny processes.

  1. Two Types of Scrutiny

Pre-Implementation Scrutiny

  • Occurs before a code is adopted.

  • Focuses on identifying:

    • The problem requiring regulation.

    • Appropriate ethical standards.

    • Conduct that should be prohibited.

  • Similar to the policy-development stage of legislation.

  • Typically occurs without implementation data.

Post-Implementation Scrutiny

  • Evaluates how the Code functions in practice.

  • Assesses:

    • Real-world impact.

    • Effectiveness.

    • Enforceability.

    • Whether the Code still achieves its intended purpose.

  • Relies on oversight, public feedback, and evidence gathered through implementation.

  • Seen as a mechanism for improving democratic accountability and regulatory quality.

  1. Methodology for the 2026 Review

The review is informed by:

  • Standard drafting principles (plain language, gender neutrality, concise structure).

  • Public participation through written submissions and hearings.

  • Comparative legal research from other jurisdictions and international bodies.

  • Committee deliberations and lessons from past practice.

  1. Purposes of the Code

The Code serves three main functions:

  1. Establishes standards against which Members' conduct is assessed.

  2. Provides the benchmark for disclosure of interests.

  3. Regulates the work of the Joint Committee and the Office of the Registrar.

  1. Considerations for Reform

The review should ensure that the Code:

  • Keeps pace with technological developments.

  • Reflects changes in the parliamentary environment.

  • Updates definitions and aligns with evolving jurisprudence.

  • Closes loopholes and addresses accountability gaps.

  • Remains practical and enforceable.

  • Responds to changing societal expectations.

  • Strengthens public trust in Parliament.

Members were informed that the review adopts a post-implementation scrutiny approach, focusing on whether the Code remains effective, enforceable, and relevant after years of practical application. It draws on public participation, comparative research, drafting best practices, and committee experience to assess whether the Code continues to fulfil its functions of regulating Members' conduct, disclosure obligations, and oversight processes. Particular attention is given to modernising the Code to address technological change, evolving parliamentary practices, accountability gaps, and public expectations in order to strengthen trust in democratic institutions.

This presentation also explained proposed amendments to South Africa's Executive Ethics Code, which governs the ethical conduct and financial disclosures of Cabinet Ministers, Deputy Ministers, and MECs. The amendments aim to modernise the Code, implement long-delayed Cabinet decisions, comply with court judgments, and facilitate electronic disclosure systems.

  1. Why the Amendments Are Being Made

The Executive Members' Ethics Act of 1998 requires the President to publish an ethics code promoting accountable and transparent government. The current Executive Ethics Code was promulgated in 2000.

A key issue prompting amendment was a Cabinet decision made in 2010 to increase gift and hospitality thresholds from R1 000 to R1 500. That decision was never formally implemented through a presidential proclamation.

Officials later argued that the thresholds had become unrealistically low and generated unnecessary administrative burdens because Members frequently needed permission to retain relatively modest gifts.

  1. Changes to Gift and Hospitality Rules

The Department initially proposed raising the threshold to R2 000 in line with inflation (CPI). Cabinet ultimately approved a higher threshold of R2 500 in October 2024.

The amended framework means:

  • Gifts and hospitality up to R2 500 may generally be accepted.

  • Gifts above R2 500 require approval from the President or Premier.

  • Even when approval is granted, gifts must still be disclosed.

  • Disclosure obligations remain an important accountability mechanism.

  1. Introduction of Electronic Disclosure

A major reform is the move from manual to electronic disclosure of financial interests.

Key features include:

  • Creation of an online disclosure platform.

  • Continuous public access to the public portion of the register.

  • Removal of restrictions limiting access to office hours.

  • Creation of a new clause (paragraph 7A) governing electronic disclosures.

  • Provision for manual disclosures when the electronic system is unavailable, with later uploading once systems are restored.

  1. Protection of Personal Information

The amendments incorporate the requirements of South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA).

Changes include:

  • Adding a formal definition of the Act.

  • Making the Ethics Code expressly subject to POPIA requirements.

  • Ensuring disclosure systems protect personal information appropriately.

  1. Expanded Asset Disclosure

The Code introduces a new definition of "vehicle", covering conveyances on land, water, and air.

This broadens disclosure requirements beyond traditional motor vehicles and improves transparency regarding Members' assets.

  1. Constitutional Court Compliance

The amendments also respond to the landmark Constitutional Court case: AmaBhungane Centre for Investigative Journalism NPC v President of the Republic of South Africa

In 2022, the Court ruled that the Code was unconstitutional because it did not require disclosure of donations made to campaigns for leadership positions within political parties. Parliament was directed to remedy the defect.

The revised Code therefore introduces disclosure requirements for internal party campaign donations and brings the Code into compliance with the judgment.

  1. Broader Review Process

The presentation noted that government has already begun a wider overhaul of both the Ethics Act and the Ethics Code, partly in response to recommendations from the Public Protector regarding weaknesses in the existing framework. However, these immediate amendments are viewed as necessary before the larger reform process is completed.

  1. Way Forward

The proposed process is:

  1. Parliament considers and approves the amendments.

  2. The amendments are submitted to the President.

  3. The President publishes them in the Government Gazette.

  4. The Presidency continuously monitors implementation and compliance.

In summary, the presentation spoke to proposed targeted amendments to South Africa's Executive Ethics Code to modernise disclosure systems, raise outdated gift thresholds from R1 000 to R2 500, introduce electronic declarations of interests, strengthen privacy protections under POPIA, expand asset disclosure requirements, and comply with the Constitutional Court's AmaBhungane judgment requiring disclosure of donations to internal political party campaigns. These changes are presented as interim reforms while a broader overhaul of the Ethics Act and Code continues.

Discussion

Chairperson Mvana noted that the presentation appears to focus primarily on members of the Executive, particularly regarding the process through which executive members obtain permission and make declarations. She expressed concern that the presentation does not adequately address the broader category of public office bearers or public representatives beyond the Executive.

She further sought clarity on the increase in livestock.

Ms M Makesini (EFF, Free State) wanted to check the definition for assets, whether they include electronic assets such as cryptocurrencies, etc. (parts of input inaudible)

The presenter explained to Chairperson Mvana that it mainly applies to the executive members of government.

The presenter explained that the proposed threshold amount has been increased. He noted that one of the lower amounts previously under consideration was R2 000, but when the proposal was submitted to Cabinet, Cabinet recommended increasing the threshold to R2 500.

He also responded that he understood the issue being raised but was not entirely clear on all aspects of it. Referring to a newly introduced definition (possibly relating to gifts or benefits), he explained that he had been trying to capture the different items that had been mentioned during the discussion. In particular, he sought clarification on what was meant by "other gifts" so that these could be appropriately covered within the definition.

Ms Makesini followed up on her question again about the definition of assets.

The presenter explained that a vehicle includes a broad range of transportation means that could take you from point A to point B, be it a jet, a car, a boat, etc.

Mr H Van den Berg (FF+, Northern Cape) followed up on Ms Makesini’s question on how cryptocurrency may be taken into account.

The presenter acknowledged that and indicated that they may need to specify those types of cryptocurrency.

Mr B Farmer (PA, Western Cape) asked who gives permission to the President if the President receives gifts since members of Cabinet need to receive permission from Cabinet.

The presenter explained that the President would need to disclose gifts to the Secretariat of Cabinet (inaudible).

The Chairperson asked a similar question - then who gives permission to the Premiers?

The presenter confirmed that the Code does not explicitly state those scenario, but he would assume the Secretariat of Cabinet would have to give permission to Premiers.

He noted that before becoming members of the Executive, individuals were first Members of Parliament. She argued that there was a shortcoming in the wording of the Code because it focused only on members of committees, as though they were not also members of government. She suggested that the Code should be amended to reflect this broader status and ensure that all relevant office-bearers were appropriately covered.

She further raised concerns about the process for obtaining permission in certain circumstances. Using the example of a Premier, she indicated that a Premier should not necessarily be required to seek permission from the..., but rather from the Secretary of Parliament or the Speaker of the Legislature. She proposed that the wording be corrected to clarify the appropriate authority responsible for granting such permission.

Lastly, Ms Makesini highlighted the issue of livestock and questioned how it should be classified for declaration purposes. She noted that livestock can hold significant monetary value, yet the current provisions did not explicitly mention livestock or similar assets. She argued that these assets should also be declared because they could be used to conceal wealth or be acquired improperly. She recommended that livestock and similar assets be specifically included in the Code’s provisions on declarations of interests.

Adv G Breytenbach (DA) sought clarity on the process surrounding declarations made by Members to the Secretary of Parliament or the relevant authority. She asked what happened to these declarations once they had been submitted, whether they were available for public viewing, and how they were managed.

She further questioned who was responsible for verifying or scrutinising the declarations to ensure their accuracy and compliance with the Code. In addition, she asked what happened to gifts that were declared by Members, seeking clarity on how such gifts were handled and whether there were procedures governing their disposal, retention, or management.

The presenter explained that the Code required Members to obtain permission regarding whether a gift could be retained. Once permission had been granted to retain the gift, the Member could keep it. Regardless of whether the gift was retained, it would still be recorded in the register as a gift that had been received. This ensured that there was an official record of all gifts received by Members and that the declaration requirements were complied with.

Ms A Kumbaca (ANC) added on the process relating to gifts. She explained that Members were required to seek permission regarding gifts they had received. If the President granted permission for the gift to be retained, the Member could keep it, provided that it was properly declared. If permission to retain the gift was not granted, the gift would have to be returned to the person or entity that had originally given it.

She also responded to concerns raised by other Members regarding trusts, financial interests, cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, and similar assets. She noted that the Code already contained provisions dealing with shares, trusts, and other financial interests. While she acknowledged that there might be room for improvement in the wording or scope of these provisions, she emphasised that the existing Code did address these categories of interests and required their disclosure.

An official from the Presidency informed the Committee that additional information had been provided regarding the viewing of disclosures. She explained that the current Code allowed for both public and confidential disclosures. The public portion of the register was open for inspection by any person who requested access.

She stated that requests to view disclosures were typically submitted to the Register of Interests, which was administered by the Secretary of Cabinet. Once a request had been received and processed, arrangements would be made for the individual to view the relevant disclosure records.

The official further explained that the public register included information such as the gifts declared by Members. As a result, members of the public who were granted access to the register could view the list of gifts that had been disclosed by Members in accordance with the Code.

Chairperson Mvana reflected on the issue of how the register of interests was managed and reviewed. She noted that there had been changes over time in who administered the register, moving from an executive secretary to the current secretary, according to her understanding. She emphasised that, regardless of the specific administrative structure, it was important to strengthen and clarify the system of oversight and safety.

She further argued that the current arrangements needed improvement to ensure that the register remained functional and accessible. In her view, if the system was not properly maintained or consistently updated, public access to the information would be of limited value. She therefore suggested that clearer administrative safeguards and processes be included to improve the effectiveness of the register.

Finally, she indicated that her remarks were not a question but rather a comment on the importance of proper management of the register, including how it is maintained and what information is required to be recorded.

Mr M Gana (Rise Mzansi) clarified that the focus of concern was declarations made within Parliament itself, rather than declarations made by executive members to the President or other authorities. He explained that, as Members of Parliament, individuals were duty-bound to make declarations in Parliament, and that these declarations were accessible to all Members of Parliament.

He further stated that the purpose of the Code was not to create an overly extensive or complex system, but rather to ensure basic and consistent disclosure. In his view, the framework was designed around the principle that Members would declare their relevant interests in Parliament, where such declarations would be formally recorded and made available in accordance with parliamentary procedures.

Mr Gana clarified his point further, emphasising that the discussion should be correctly reflected in the minutes of the meeting. He stated that although members of Cabinet are also Members of Parliament, the Code under discussion did not replace the existing parliamentary duty to declare interests.

He explained that his argument was not about processes “on the other side” of government, but rather about ensuring consistency and accuracy in how the Code is understood and recorded. He reiterated that Members of Parliament remain duty-bound to declare their interests in Parliament, and that this obligation was separate from any executive-level processes.

He further indicated that his earlier reference to ministers not declaring in Parliament was used as an example to highlight gaps in compliance, and not to shift the focus of the discussion away from the core issue. He concluded by signalling that the next item on the agenda would follow from this clarification.

The presenter explained that the Executive means Cabinet Ministers, deputy ministers and a member of the provincial Executive.

Mr Van den Berg wanted to know if it is possible to include the President in this Code.

Mr Van den Berg sought clarity on whether the President or the Premiers are included in this Code, or whether the Code only applies to deputy ministers.

An official from the Presidency explained that, when members of Cabinet or other persons covered by the Code assumed office, they were formally informed of their obligation to declare interests. She stated that, within the first week of appointment, a letter was issued, typically through the Cabinet Office, advising them of their duty to declare interests within a specified number of days and to disclose all relevant interests in which they were actively involved.

She further noted that compliance with this requirement was a separate issue. While the obligation to declare was clearly communicated, whether members actually complied in practice varied. She suggested that this challenge in enforcement and follow-through potentially reflected the concerns raised earlier by Chairperson Mvana regarding the effectiveness and reliability of the register system.

Mr Farmer asked if this reviewing process should include the review of due process. He recommended reviewing the whole process rather than subjecting Cabinet members to a second process.

Adv Anthea Gordon, Register of Members' Interests in Parliament, explained that the Parliamentary Code of Ethics is fundamentally derived from section 45 of the Constitution, which governs the joint business of Parliament and the joint rules that set the parameters of the Code. She noted that the Code is contained in a schedule to the joint rules and applies to all Members of Parliament.

She further clarified that the Code does not extend in the same way to ministers and deputy ministers, as the Constitution makes provision for them to be governed under separate legislation, namely the Executive Members’ Ethics Act. In her view, this reflects a deliberate constitutional design that distinguishes between Members of Parliament and members of the Executive.

Adv. Gordon added that while the question of whether processes between these frameworks should be merged was an interesting one, it required careful consideration. She emphasised that the Executive has broader responsibilities, including oversight of government departments, which differ fundamentally from the role of ordinary Members of Parliament. She concluded that the current legislative framework was structured in recognition of these distinct roles and obligations.

Mr Gana pointed out that section 98 of the Constitution clearly provides for the Executive to have its own separate ethical framework. In his view, this separation was intentional, as it ensured that the Executive and Parliament were governed by distinct codes appropriate to their different roles and responsibilities.

He further argued that Parliament included various categories of members, and therefore a single uniform approach could create complications. He suggested that maintaining clear institutional separation between the ethical codes of the Executive and Parliament was necessary to preserve constitutional order and functional clarity.

Overview of the approach of the review of the Code of Ethical Conduct and Disclosure of Members' Interests

Dr Barbara Loots, Parliamentary Legal Advisor, and Adv Gordon took the Committee through this presentation.

The presentation provided an update on the public participation process relating to proposed amendments to the Executive Ethics Code and the Code of Ethical Conduct and Disclosure of Members’ Interests for Assembly and Permanent Council Members.

Regarding the Executive Ethics Code, the amendment process stems from the requirements of the Executive Members' Ethics Act, 1998, which mandates the President to publish a code of ethics for members of the Executive after consultation with Parliament. The matter was referred to the Joint Committee on 27 June 2025. A call for public comments was issued on 12 February 2026, with submissions closing on 31 March 2026. Seventeen submissions were received, including a late submission from the Office of the Public Protector. Virtual public hearings were scheduled for 5 June 2026, while a report on the public submissions was planned for adoption on 19 June 2026. Key themes emerging from the submissions included strengthening definitions and enforcement mechanisms, tightening conflict-of-interest provisions, enhancing accountability and oversight, improving transparency, ensuring accountability beyond a Member's term of office, introducing timely and proportionate sanctions, modernising the legal framework, and recognising indigenous ethical traditions.

For the Code of Ethical Conduct and Disclosure of Members’ Interests for Assembly and Permanent Council Members, the public comment period ran from 3 March to 31 March 2026. Nine submissions were received, including a late submission from #UniteBehind. Virtual public hearings were scheduled for 12 June 2026, with provision made to accommodate any overflow from the earlier hearings on 5 June 2026. The public submission report was also scheduled for adoption on 19 June 2026. Key issues raised in submissions included expanding and clarifying definitions, updating structures and terminology, strengthening complaints and penalty procedures, addressing social media conduct, closing perceived accountability gaps, introducing universal lifestyle audits, enhancing oversight and transparency, ensuring accountability beyond a Member's term of office, and considering targeted reform proposals.

Overall, the update highlighted strong public interest in improving ethical governance, accountability, transparency, and enforcement mechanisms within both the Executive and parliamentary ethics frameworks.

Discussion

Mr M Billy (DA, KZN) requested clarity on the procedural roadmap for the process that was about to unfold. He asked for an indication of the timelines involved, the stakeholders who would be consulted during public participation, and the expected sequence of steps following those consultations.

He further inquired about what would happen after the consultation process had been completed, particularly in relation to finalising the revised or newly implemented Code. He emphasised the importance of having a clear plan, noting that the Committee may be starting the process relatively late and that this could impact whether it would be completed within the current term.

He concluded by asking for a structured outline of what was being brought to the table, including key milestones and an indication of the expected completion process.

Adv Gordon explained that the question would be responded to in the next agenda item.

Mr Farmer asked where an MP fits in this public participation process, in a code that will be ultimately enforced on them?

Adv. Gordon explained that the review process had already been initiated and had progressed into a structured engagement phase. She noted that the administration had opted to undertake a formal review of the Code and had communicated this decision through the Chief Whips’ Forum, ensuring that all relevant stakeholders were informed that the process was underway.

She stated that stakeholders were given an opportunity to make submissions as part of the process, and that engagements had included presentations and input from various parties. She added that public participation had also formed part of the process, which included opportunities for Members of Parliament to be involved and contribute through the appropriate channels.

Adv. Gordon further reminded the Committee that Members had at all times been entitled to submit inputs directly to the administration, and that some Members had indeed taken up this opportunity to provide submissions. She clarified that the formal period for submissions had now closed, and the process was moving forward accordingly. However, an initial invitation had been extended to the Chief Whips' Forum and other stakeholders earlier in the process.

Discussion on the draft public participation programme

Both Adv Gordon and Dr Loots provided further details to the public participation programme.

Adv. Gordon explained that, under agenda item 6.1, the administration had begun scheduling key Committee dates based on initial comments received. She stated that two Committee dates had been identified, Friday 5 June and Friday 12 June, and that permission had been obtained to convene on those days.

She noted that the 5 June session would focus on public comments relating to the Executive Members’ Ethics Act, while the 12 June session would focus on the Parliamentary Code of Ethics. The 12 June engagement would be conducted virtually, due to budget constraints and the costs associated with bringing stakeholders together in person. She added that this virtual platform would allow participation from stakeholders who had submitted written representations on both the Executive Ethics Act and the Parliamentary Code.

Adv. Gordon further explained that Dr Lewis would provide additional detail on the presentation process under item 6.2, including how amendments to the Executive Members' Ethics Act had progressed through consultation and referral stages. She outlined that public comments on the executive ethics framework had been received between February and March 2026, resulting in approximately 17 written submissions, including a late submission from the Public Protector, which had been accepted.

She reported that key themes emerging from those submissions included the need to strengthen definitions and enforcement mechanisms, improve conflict of interest rules, enhance accountability and oversight, modernise the legal framework, and consider the inclusion of indigenous ethical traditions. She added that similar themes had emerged in submissions on the Parliamentary Code, including concerns about clarity, penalties, transparency, and systemic accountability weaknesses. One submission had even proposed a “universal lifestyle audit,” while others raised issues of accountability beyond office tenure and improved performance regulation.

Adv. Gordon concluded by clarifying the difference between the two processes: the Executive Ethics Code was being processed through a formal referral system, while the Parliamentary Members' Code was being driven directly by the Committee under its joint rule mandate. She emphasised that timelines for the Members' Code were flexible and dependent on the Committee's level of engagement, depth of inquiry, and whether further research or revisions were requested. Ultimately, she stated that the Committee itself would determine the pace and direction of the work, with the administration responding accordingly.

Discussion

Mr Billy emphasised that the purpose of the register process was to ensure proper transparency and accountability, particularly in relation to public disclosure. He noted that there appeared to be practical constraints affecting the process, including logistical and resource-related challenges that needed to be considered.

He suggested that the Committee and supporting administration should provide clear guidance on how the public participation process would be conducted, including whether engagements would be virtual or in-person. In his view, such decisions should be made in a way that supports meaningful participation and maintains public interest in the process.

Mr Billy further stressed that the exercise was an important one and should be given adequate time and resources to ensure it was properly implemented. He concluded by indicating that careful planning and sufficient support would be necessary to make the process effective and credible.

Mr Gana supported the earlier comments and emphasised the importance of proper public communication around the process. He noted that there was a responsibility on the Committee to ensure that the public was clearly informed about the hearings, in the same way that public notices had been issued for earlier calls for submissions.

He explained that Ethics Committee meetings are generally held in private, and therefore these public hearings represented a rare opportunity for public engagement with parliamentary processes. He suggested that the Committee, working with Parliament's communications team, should actively publicise the sessions to ensure broader awareness and accessibility.

Mr Gana further argued that communication around the hearings should be strengthened and modernised, reflecting the changing context in which parliamentary work is conducted. He concluded by requesting that the communications team clearly announce that the Ethics Committee would be holding public hearings on the specified dates, so that the public could be properly informed and encouraged to participate.

emphasised the importance of issuing a clear public announcement before the scheduled dates of the hearings. Mr Gana noted that the use of a virtual platform was particularly beneficial, as it allowed broader participation and made it easier to accommodate a larger number of stakeholders than an in-person meeting would allow.

He requested that the communications maximise publicity of the hearings so that as many stakeholders and members of the public as possible could be informed. He also encouraged Members of Parliament to actively assist by identifying and notifying any stakeholders who might otherwise be overlooked, stressing that it was a shared responsibility to ensure inclusive participation.

Chairperson Mvana further agreed that the virtual format was advantageous, noting that it enabled wider access compared to bringing all participants into a single physical venue, which she implied would be impractical given capacity constraints.

She concluded by noting that the proposed dates had been adopted and seconded. She indicated that there appeared to be no further outstanding items on the agenda, suggesting that the Committee had concluded its discussion on the matter.

Adv. Gordon thanked the Co-Chairperson and indicated that, from the administration's side, the matters raised would be taken forward in the coming week.

She stated that relevant colleagues from the Department of Justice and the Presidency would be engaged as part of the preparation process. Once the list of stakeholders was confirmed, and their mode of participation clarified, whether they would be making presentations or relying on their written submissions, the necessary documents would be circulated to the Committee in line with the agreed meeting schedule.

She concluded by noting that these preparatory steps would be undertaken ahead of the upcoming Committee meetings to ensure proper organisation and readiness.

Chairperson Mvana concluded by thanking all participants for their engagement and contributions, expressing appreciation for their time in what she described as an important process. She emphasised that, while the matters under discussion may appear technical in nature, there were practical aspects that still required clarity and careful handling, particularly regarding disclosure forms and their completion.

She noted that some Members had experienced challenges with completing the forms and suggested that an extension had been granted to allow further submissions. She acknowledged those who had already complied and submitted their disclosures, commending them for doing so.

She ended her remarks on a note of gratitude to all involved and adjourned the meeting.
