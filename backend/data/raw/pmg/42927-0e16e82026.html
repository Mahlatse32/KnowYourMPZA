Department of Agriculture Annual Performance Plan 2026/27; with Minister

NCOP Agriculture, Land Reform and Mineral Resources

07 May 2026
Chairperson: Mr P Mabilo (ANC, Northern Cape)
Share this page:

Annual Performance Plan (APP) of Government Departments & Entities 2026/27

The Select Committee on Agriculture, Land Reform and Mineral Resources met to receive a briefing from the Department of Agriculture on its Annual Performance Plan and Budget Vote 29.

The Department of Agriculture presented its 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan, explaining that it outlined priorities, targets and strategic direction for the financial year. The Department stated that the Plan had been improved based on recommendations from oversight institutions and was aligned with national development frameworks and the Agriculture and Agro-processing Master Plan. It was also guided by government priorities aimed at inclusive growth, poverty reduction and building a capable state. The Department highlighted that its focus included improving food security, increasing agricultural productivity, expanding market access, strengthening biosecurity and supporting transformation in the sector.

The Department further explained that its strengths included institutional knowledge and established policies, while weaknesses involved weak private sector partnerships, reliance on consultants, slow digitalisation and corruption risks. Opportunities included digital transformation and improved partnerships, while threats included climate change, instability, biosecurity risks and budget constraints. It stated its vision as building a dynamic and sustainable agricultural sector for food security, supported by values of innovation, collaboration and growth.

The Department outlined key targets, including improved compliance with legislation, increased productivity across farming sectors, greater export growth, expansion of black producer participation and reduction of household food insecurity. It also presented sector data showing agriculture’s contribution to GDP and employment.

The Department then detailed programme plans covering administration, agricultural production and biosecurity, food security support, and trade and marketing. These included improving audit outcomes, strengthening research and surveillance systems, supporting farmers with technology and finance, expanding neglected crop production, and improving disease control measures.

Members raised concerns about the Department’s readiness to deliver on its Annual Performance Plan in the context of limited digital systems, uneven service delivery and ongoing operational challenges. They questioned the low level of digitisation, which was reported at about 4%, and argued that this limited the Department’s ability to detect inefficiencies, corruption risks and poor coordination across systems. They also raised concerns about weak oversight of provincial agriculture departments, inconsistencies in farming support projects, and tensions within communal farming structures. Related concerns were raised about whether the Department had a clear and consistent approach to supporting both commercial and subsistence farmers, and whether value for money was being achieved in provincial implementation.

Members further raised concerns about animal health management, particularly Foot and Mouth Disease, and asked if the Department’s response had been sufficiently strong given its impact on livestock production and trade. Questions were also raised about staffing levels, particularly in veterinary services, and whether the Department had sufficient capacity to carry out its regulatory and inspection functions effectively. Concerns were also expressed about funding allocations to entities within the agricultural system and whether certain institutions were being adequately supported to deliver on their mandates.

Members also questioned whether agricultural productivity targets and food security goals were realistic, given budget reductions in key programmes such as food security support, plant health and extension services. They expressed concern that administrative spending appeared to be increasing while frontline service areas faced reductions. Further concerns were raised about delays in agricultural input regulation processes, the slow implementation of extension services, challenges in blended finance delivery through financial institutions, and gaps in agricultural college funding and infrastructure support.

Members also raised concerns about whether

n and whether the cost of digital systems had been properly compared with the cost of using manual processes. Concerns were also raised about blended finance support, including how beneficiaries were selected and how funds were distributed between national and provincial levels.

Members further questioned progress in managing Foot and Mouth Disease, including whether vaccination efforts meant the disease was under control. They also raised concerns about funding cuts to key programmes such as food security, plant health, extension services and agricultural colleges, arguing that this weakened frontline delivery while administrative spending appeared to increase. Additional concerns were raised about veterinary shortages, delays in agricultural input regulation, weak implementation of extension services, and challenges in blended finance delivery through financial institutions.

In response to Members’ questions, the Minister of the Department of Agriculture confirmed a shift to a mass vaccination strategy for FMD, aiming to reach 80% national herd coverage. Increased vaccination is expected to strengthen disease control and help restore or expand access to international markets. He stressed that budget limits are affecting performance, called for increased funding—particularly for provincial veterinary services—and emphasised the need for stronger inclusion in the livestock and game sectors, noting the potential for new export opportunities if disease control improves.

The Chairperson requested written information on provincial support for new livestock farmers. He asked the Department to provide a detailed breakdown of support per province, including cattle, goats and sheep programmes.

The Chairperson then noted scheduling challenges but appreciated the Minister and the Department for attending and engaging with oversight processes.

Meeting report

Opening remarks

Mr P Mabilo (ANC, Northern Cape), the Acting Chairperson, opened the meeting by welcoming all participants and stated that the meeting had only one item on the agenda, which was a briefing by the Department of Agriculture on its Annual Performance Plan and Budget Vote 29. He indicated that he would hand over to the Minister to make his opening remarks and to indicate which officials would table the Annual Performance Plans (APPs) during the presentation.

Minister’s opening remarks

Minister John Steenhuisen, Minister of Agriculture (DoA), thanked the Committee for the opportunity to present the Department’s APP together with the budget for the 2026 financial year. He acknowledged that the presentation was substantial and indicated that it would be delivered by the Department’s officials. He informed the Committee that the Deputy Director-General (DDG): Agriculture Production, Health and Food Safety, Mr Dipepeneneng Serage, would lead the presentation and would guide the Committee through the different sections, while also indicating which officials would present specific parts. He concluded by noting that, once the presentation had been completed, the Department would hand back to the Committee for questions, comments, and inputs from Committee Members, and then hand over to Mr Serage to begin.

2026/27 Annual Performance Plan for the Department of Agriculture

Ms Mimi Molotsi, Acting DDG, DoA, presented the Department’s 2026/27 APP. She explained that the purpose of the presentation was to outline the Department’s plans, priorities, targets and strategic focus for the 2026/27 financial year. She stated that the APP had been improved following recommendations from the Auditor-General of South Africa (AGSA) and the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME). The APP was informed by the 2024–2029 Medium Term Development Plan and aligned with government's three strategic priorities of driving inclusive growth and job creation, reducing poverty and the high cost of living, and building a capable, ethical and developmental state. She further explained that the APP aligned with the Agriculture and Agro-processing Master Plan and the Department's 2025–2030 Strategic Plan, with implementation reflected through output indicators and targets. 

Ms Molotsi outlined the Medium Term Development Plan priorities of the Seventh Administration. She explained that the Department's strategic priorities focused on partnerships for growth, creating a modern legislative and regulatory environment, improving market access for South African agriculture, supporting viable and sustainable farming operations, strengthening biosecurity, promoting advocacy for agricultural growth, and improving food security. She presented the Department's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) analysis. She stated that the Department's strengths included sufficient knowledge, capabilities and expertise, strong internal policies, and close associations with international bodies. Weaknesses included weak partnerships with the private sector, overreliance on consultants, a slow pace of digitalisation, and corruption. Opportunities included digitalisation, partnerships with commodity organisations and strategic partners, and donor funding opportunities, while threats included political instability, climate change, disasters, biosecurity risks, cyber hacking and budget cuts. 

She explained that the Department’s vision was to create a dynamic and sustainable agricultural sector for a food-secure South Africa, while its mission was to enable inclusive economic growth, competitiveness and market access through collaborative partnerships, innovation, research and transformed legislation. The Department’s values were adaptation, growth, respect, innovation and collaboration. She also outlined the Department’s impact statement, which aimed to improve food security and market access through a sustained and responsive agricultural sector. The APP outcomes included improved governance and modernised service delivery, increased agricultural production, improved food and nutrition security, increased production by black producers to stimulate transformation, increased market access, and enhanced biosecurity and disaster risk reduction. 

Ms Molotsi then discussed the Department's Medium Term Development Plan targets. Under the priority of building a capable, ethical and developmental state, the Department aimed to achieve 100% compliance with legislative frameworks over five years, attain 90% achievement of key performance indicators, and modernise or digitalise 40% of departmental systems from a baseline of 18%. Under the priority of driving inclusive growth and job creation, the Department aimed to increase agricultural productivity growth rates to 2.6 metric tons in field crops, 0.6 metric tons in horticulture, and 0.6 metric tons in livestock. It also aimed for 100% compliance and enforcement of biosecurity policies, a 5% increase in agricultural exports, and an increase in the production share of black producers from 8% to 20%. Under the poverty reduction priority, the Department aimed to reduce severe household food insecurity from 17.5% to 12.5% over five years. 

She provided an overview of South Africa's agricultural sector. She explained that agriculture contributed approximately 2.8% to the country's direct GDP and employed between 920 000 and 950 000 people, representing about 5.6% of the national workforce. She presented trends in agricultural and agro-processing GDP contributions between 2020 and 2024. 

Ms Molotsi then discussed Programme 1: Administration. She explained that the programme provided strategic leadership, management and support services to the Department. The sub-programmes included Ministry support services, Departmental Management, Internal Audit, Financial Management Services, Corporate Support Services and Office Administration. She stated that the Department aimed to achieve an unqualified audit opinion for the 2025/26 annual departmental performance report, maintain 100% payment of valid invoices within 30 days, produce quarterly procurement reports on spending for designated groups, review and approve business processes, fill 147 funded vacant posts, and digitalise 4% of departmental systems during the year. 

She then addressed Programme 2: Agricultural Production, Biosecurity and Natural Resources Management. She explained that the programme oversaw livestock production, game farming, animal and plant health, natural resources and disaster management. The programme included inspection and quarantine services, plant production and health, animal production and health, natural resources and disaster management, biosecurity, transfers to the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), and Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP). She explained that the programme aimed to compile an approved annual research report on hemp adaptability, develop and gazette a Greenhouse Gas Emission Reduction Plan for the agriculture sector, support 50 farmers with water-efficient technologies, and produce a research report on sustainable land management practices. The Department also intended to develop two approved regulatory frameworks related to tariffs under the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act and the Preservation and Development of Agricultural Land Act. 

Ms Molotsi explained that the Department intended to promote ten neglected and underutilised species, including sweet sorghum, grain sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, bambara, cowpea, mungbean, taro, cassava and cucurbits such as calabash and melon. The Department also aimed to compile an approved research report on genomic identification and reproduction in non-descript smallholder livestock. She stated that the National Biosecurity Hub would be institutionalised through an approved report. In addition, the Department planned to conduct plant pest risk surveillance for exotic fruit fly, citrus greening and Banana Bunchy Top Virus across all four quarters, and similarly conduct animal disease surveillance for Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD), Peste des Petits Ruminants and Contagious Bovine Pleuropneumonia. 

She also presented information on hemp demonstration sites and training initiatives located across various provinces, including the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Northern Cape and Western Cape. These projects focused on cultivar evaluation and included GPS coordinates for the various sites. 

Ms Molotsi proceeded to Programme 3: Food Security and Support. She explained that the programme facilitated and promoted food security and agrarian reform programmes. The sub-programmes included Farmer Support, National Extension Support Services and Sector Capacity Development. She stated that the Department planned to develop a Second National Food and Nutrition Security Plan to be endorsed by the Executive Committee (EXCO), compile two approved biannual monitoring reports on support provided to household producers by Provincial Departments of Agriculture, support 100 producers through the Blended Finance Scheme, and develop an approved Farmer Field School Framework. 

She then discussed Programme 4: Economic Development, Trade and Marketing, which promoted economic development, trade, market access and international relations in the agricultural sector. The programme included International Relations and Trade, Cooperatives Development, Agro-processing and Marketing, and transfers to the National Agricultural Marketing Council. Ms Molotsi stated that the Department aimed to compile an approved annual AgriBEE Fund report, compile annual reports on bilateral and multilateral engagements, and capacitate 300 smallholder producers in agricultural marketing. 

Ms Molotsi further presented Annexure B on standardised indicators implemented by Provincial Departments of Agriculture through conditional grants. She explained that the 31 standardised indicators had been jointly developed by the national Department and provincial departments and were implemented across six provincial programme areas: Sustainable Resource Use and Management, Agricultural Producer Support and Development, Veterinary Services, Agricultural Research and Technology Development Services, Agricultural Economic Services, and Agricultural Education and Training. The Department provided oversight through quarterly Ministerial Technical (MinTech) working groups. 

She outlined detailed provincial targets for sustainable resource use and management, including targets for agricultural infrastructure, rehabilitation of agricultural land, green jobs, farm management plans, disaster risk reduction awareness campaigns, and assessments of early warning information uptake. She also outlined targets for producer support and development, including support for commercial and smallholder producers, training on the Smallholder Empowerment and Promotion programme, support for producer study groups, and support for household producers. Veterinary services targets included disease surveillance samples, veterinary interventions, export certificates, meat facility inspections, laboratory tests, and Performing Animals Protection Act licences. Agricultural research targets included research projects, scientific publications, peer-reviewed presentations, technology transfer presentations, development of new technologies for smallholders, and management of research infrastructure. Agricultural economic services target focused on support for agribusinesses, production economic services, Agri-BEE advisory services, agro-processing initiatives, and economic reports. Education and training targets included agricultural qualifications and sector skills development programmes. 

Mr Roewyn Danster, DoA, then presented the financial management section of the presentation dealing with the 2026/27 Estimates of National Expenditure. He explained that the Department’s total budget increased from R7.598 billion in 2025/26 to R7.842 billion in 2026/27, representing a 3.2% increase. Programme 1 received R1.034 billion or 13.2% of the total budget. Within this programme, Financial Management Services increased by 18.1%, Corporate Support Services increased by 8.3%, while Departmental Management declined by 9.5%. Programme 2 received R2.596 billion or 33.1% of the total budget. Inspection and Quarantine Services increased significantly by 41.3%, while Plant Production and Health declined by 15.5% and Natural Resources and Disaster Management declined by 5%. Transfers to the Agricultural Research Council increased slightly to R1.245 billion. Programme 3 received the largest allocation at R3.288 billion or 41.9% of the total budget, although Sector Capacity Development declined sharply by 36.3%. Programme 4 received R924 million or 11.8% of the budget. Cooperatives Development increased dramatically by 510.7%, while Agro-processing and Marketing declined by 89.8%. 

Mr Danster further explained the economic classification of expenditure. Provincial and local governments received the largest share at R2.397 billion, accounting for 30.6% of the budget. Compensation of employees amounted to R1.665 billion, while goods and services amounted to R1.576 billion. Departmental agencies and accounts received R1.296 billion, public corporations and private enterprises received R662 million, and spending on buildings and fixed structures more than doubled to R106.7 million. Household allocations increased by 92.2%, while software and intangible assets increased by 177.9%. 

He also presented the allocations for the LandCare Programme Grant, showing KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and the Eastern Cape as the largest recipients. Northern Cape experienced the highest percentage increase at 20.4%, while North West experienced a decline of 10.4%. Mr Danster then discussed the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme (CASP) infrastructure allocations, noting that KwaZulu-Natal experienced a 26.9% decrease while Northern Cape increased by 19.7%, Gauteng by 12.4%, and Western Cape by 9.6%. 

He further explained the CASP allocations for production inputs and mechanisation, where Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo received the largest shares, while Gauteng received the smallest allocation. He also outlined the CASP Extension Recovery Plan Services allocations, with Limpopo and Eastern Cape receiving the highest allocations and Western Cape receiving the lowest. 

Finally, Mr Danster discussed allocations to departmental agencies. The ARC received R1.245 billion, accounting for 96.1% of agency allocations, while the National Agricultural Marketing Council received R49.7 million. Allocations to the Primary Agriculture Sector Education and Training Authority operations, Onderstepoort Biological Products and the Perishable Products Export Control Board were either removed or reflected as zero allocations.

Discussion and responses

The Chairperson indicated that, in line with the usual order of engagements, the Minister would be given the final opportunity to respond after all questions had been posed. He invited Members to indicate who wished to participate.

Mr H Van den Berg (FF+, Northern Cape) explained that although some of his questions had been partially addressed during the presentation, there were still matters that required clear answers. He referred to slide 15 and expressed concern that only 4% of departmental processes had been digitised. He questioned how the Department would be able to detect issues such as double dipping, poor productivity, irregular acquisition practices, price controls, ghost workers, and interdepartmental silos without proper digitisation and a functional dashboard to monitor activities. He then referred to the challenges observed during oversight visits to state-funded farming projects and the contrast with commercial farming enterprises, as well as conflicts within Communal Property Associations over resources. He stated that the Department had not explained how it planned to address these challenges, which he regarded as a major factor holding back agriculture in South Africa. He further noted that the presentation had not addressed FMD, which he viewed as an important indicator of the Department’s service delivery and public image. He raised concerns about provincial agriculture departments, which he described as often inactive during oversight visits, and questioned how the Department ensured value for money when funding these departments. He also referred to slide 27 and asked if the Department regularly visited formal farmers and how it measured its relationship with and performance in that sector. Referring to slide 34 under Programme One, he noted that there was no expenditure reflected for Onderstepoort, despite issues observed during oversight visits where freeze dryers had not yet been commissioned due to outstanding accounts. He questioned why funds were allocated to the ARC but not to Onderstepoort and stressed the importance of the freeze dryers for vaccine production. Lastly, he referred to slide 38, where 21.5% of the budget was allocated to employees. He asked if this included veterinarians or if they were paid by provinces, and if the Department might be bloated in relation to its productivity.

Ms Lebogang Botsheleng, Acting DDG: Food Safety and Agrarian Reform, DoA, responded to the concern on the disparity in support between commercial farmers and subsistence or household-level farmers. She explained that the Department had different support packages for various categories of farmers and highlighted the Ilima/Letsema programme, which was specifically directed at households and subsistence farmers. She stated that funds were allocated to provinces through this programme based on their business plans to support these categories of farmers. She further explained that the Department had structured monitoring mechanisms in place for projects funded through conditional grants transferred to provinces, to ensure oversight over how the funds were utilised.

Ms Kgomotso Kgang, Chief Director: Human Resources Management and Development, DoA, addressed the question of whether the Department was bloated. She explained that after the split from the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development, the Department had applied a 60–40 principle in structuring itself. She stated that the current post establishment stood at 2 166, which classified the Department as small. She added that the Department was in fact experiencing capacity challenges on the ground in fulfilling its mandate. She indicated that the Department had finalised its fit-for-purpose structure and anticipated an increase in the post establishment to 3 443, but noted that even then, the Department would still be regarded as small and not bloated.

Mr Serage responded to several of the remaining questions. On digitisation, he indicated that this was a process that had recently begun, as the Department was still relatively new, and that a dedicated team was in place to monitor progress. He clarified the issue of the zero allocation to OBP. He explained that OBP was an incorporated company with the Department as the sole shareholder and that it was required to fund its own operations as a scheduled public entity. He stated that the zero allocation was a deliberate arrangement agreed upon with National Treasury, which allowed the Department, as shareholder, to contribute financially when necessary without having to undergo a lengthy approval process. He added that one of the freeze dryers referred to had in fact been purchased by the Department as part of its shareholder contribution to improve vaccine production.

He then explained why the ARC received an allocation of R1.2 billion. He stated that this was a parliamentary grant established several years ago after the entity had experienced serious financial challenges. He explained that when the ARC Act of 1990 had been enacted, no direct funding model had been provided for the entity, unlike other science entities, and that the parliamentary grant effectively addressed this omission by providing ongoing financial support, largely for salaries.

He further explained that the Perishable Products Export Control Board also reflected a zero allocation because it was a self-funding scheduled public entity that generated its own income through levies, and only required departmental funding should special projects arise in future.

Addressing the matter of veterinarians, he explained that the national Department employed a small number of veterinarians who mainly performed regulatory and policy functions, while implementation was carried out by provinces. He indicated that of approximately 4 000 veterinarians in South Africa, fewer than 300 were in state employment across national and provincial government, and that the national Department itself employed fewer than 30. He added that the Department received an annual special allocation of approximately R240 million for compulsory community service for newly qualified veterinarians, similar to medical doctors, and that this allocation did not appear in the budget reflected in the slides. He concluded by indicating that the questions raised by the committee member had been addressed.

Ms S Boshoff (DA, Mpumalanga) began by congratulating the Minister and the Department for vaccinating approximately 2.59 million cattle as part of efforts to curb FMD. She noted the receipt of two million vaccine doses from Turkey out of an expected six million, as well as a further five million doses from Argentina. She stated that this progress was important to acknowledge. She then directed a question to Mr Serage relating to biosecurity and disease surveillance, where she asked what immediate measures the Department was prioritising that year to strengthen the prevention and rapid response capacity, particularly with regard to FMD.

The APP identified slow digitalisation as a challenge. Yet the annual target systems digitalised remained relatively modest. What key digital reforms in the Department did Mr Serage believe would make the biggest operational difference that year?

Regarding the agro-processing and marketing allocation, how would the Department ensure continued support for value addition and market access opportunities for producers?

On hemp development: the APP referred to hemp adaptability research and demonstration sites across several provinces. Could Mr Serage indicate what the next practical step is in moving hemp from research and pilot projects towards meaningful commercial participation and opportunities for emerging farmers?

Regarding budget pressures: the Department’s overall budget increase for 2026/27 was, for Ms Boshoff, relatively modest at 3.2%. What measures were being put in place to ensure that service delivery and farmer support were still maintained under those fiscal pressures?

She observed that there was a notable increase in funding for inspection and quarantine services. Could the Department elaborate on how that additional allocation would strengthen the agricultural trade and biosecurity outcomes?

With significant allocations continuing towards farmer support and extension services, how would the Department ensure that support reached producers efficiently, and then translated into sustainable production growth?

Mr Serage responded by explaining that the Department conducted both active and passive surveillance for plant pests and animal diseases as part of its biosecurity system. He described how traps were placed at ports of entry and other high-risk areas to detect exotic pests that were not present in South Africa, such as exotic fruit flies, Citrus Greening Disease, and Banana Bunchy Top Virus. He explained that if such pests or diseases were detected and allowed to establish themselves, they would be extremely difficult to eradicate because there would be no natural enemies or prepared chemical controls. He stated that inspectors regularly serviced these traps as part of ongoing surveillance.

He then referred to animal disease surveillance, including FMD. He explained that the Minister had gazetted a Section 10 scheme aimed at improving coordination and management of the disease as part of a ten-year strategy. He stated that once widespread vaccination had been achieved, the Department would be able to move towards more targeted vaccination. He added that animal movement control measures under Section 9 of the Animal Diseases Act were being finalised, which would make it illegal to move animals without proper authorisation and notification, thereby strengthening surveillance. He also explained that surveillance was conducted for diseases not present in the country, such as Peste des Petits Ruminants in sheep and goats and Contagious Bovine Pleuropneumonia in cattle and buffalo, as these could have devastating effects if introduced.

On digitisation, he acknowledged that progress had been slow but highlighted that the Department had made significant advances in the export environment through the e-SAD system. He explained that inspections of orchards for export certification were now captured digitally through a platform known as e-Inspect, which allowed inspection reports to be uploaded immediately and phytosanitary certificates to be issued automatically through an international portal linked to the International Plant Protection Convention. This had replaced a cumbersome paper-based process and allowed faster certification for exports. He added that a livestock identification and traceability system had been fully developed and was ready for implementation, which would allow inspectors to record animal inspections and vaccinations electronically through scanning devices linked to a national system.

In response to questions about agro-processing and market access, he referred to the Agro-processing Master Plan, which now had the support of all role players in the sector. He explained that value chain round tables for commodities such as wool, horticulture, fruits and vegetables, and livestock were co-chaired by Department officials and industry representatives. These platforms addressed issues of market access and value addition and allowed challenges to be resolved collaboratively.

On hemp, he explained that while the Department had initially driven the hemp component of the cannabis master plan, responsibility had since shifted to the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (DTIC). The DoA’s role was currently limited to research, seed multiplication, and adaptation trials, after which reports were provided to enable further commercial steps. He noted that broader cannabis regulation depended on regulations still being developed by the Department of Justice (DoJ) following the enactment of the Cannabis for Private Purposes law. Until then the focus remained only on hemp with low tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) content.

On budget pressures, he stated that the Department was experiencing serious fiscal strain, particularly due to unplanned expenditure exceeding R300 million on FMD. He indicated that additional funding would be necessary to sustain service delivery. He explained that part of the increased funding for inspection services was intended to expand the e-SAD system to include import modules, which would allow integration with the South African Revenue Service (SARS) and the Border Management Authority. He emphasised that many inspections, including those for animals and food products entering the country, were still manual and required more inspectors on the ground. He stated that the Department needed significantly more inspection capacity to enforce the 13 pieces of legislation under the Minister’s authority and to strengthen biosecurity.

Ms Botsheleng responded to the question on farmer support. She explained that much of the funding reflected under farmer support consisted of conditional grants transferred to provinces through the Division of Revenue Act, based on business plans submitted by provinces. She stated that the Department used both its monitoring and evaluation unit and a system of rotating quarterly review meetings in provinces, where officials visited project sites on the ground rather than relying only on reports. This allowed the Department to verify that support was reaching the intended farmers and that projects reported by provinces were indeed taking place.

Mr K Montwedi (EFF, North West) raised a wide range of concerns about the Department’s APP and budget allocations for the 2026/27 financial year. He began by noting that while the mandate of the Department was expanding, the real resources available to it were shrinking. He referred to an overall real decline of about 4.56% and expressed concern that key frontline programmes such as food security and plant health were being reduced, while Programme One, administration, was increasing by 7.6%. He questioned how the Department justified an 11.2% real cut to food security support at a time when food insecurity was rising and when national commitments were expanding. He also highlighted an 18.2% cut to plant production and health, and asked how this would affect compliance with international phytosanitary standards in the context of increasing pest and climate risks.

He then referred to delays in the registration of fertilisers under Act 36 of 1947 and asked how the APP addressed this problem and if capacity was being built in the relevant unit. He raised concerns about extension services and stated that the Department was only committing to placing 1 000 agricultural graduates on farms, while previous announcements had referred to 10 000 extension officers. He asked where the budget for the remaining 9 000 extension officers was located, given the critical role they played in supporting provincial projects.

He questioned how the APP addressed a previously reported vacancy rate of about 45% in veterinary services, noting that the shortage of veterinarians had posed serious risks during the height of the FMD outbreaks. He also questioned the reference in the Plan to institutionalising the national biosecurity hub, pointing out that the hub had already been launched in 2022. He asked if this meant that the hub had not been functional since its launch or if the Plan misrepresented its status.

Mr Montwedi referred to agricultural colleges and reminded the Minister of a previous meeting with college principals where serious concerns had been raised about infrastructure, funding, and the lack of trading accounts. He questioned how the Plan addressed those challenges, especially in light of the Department’s decision to reprioritise funds away from agricultural colleges to address FMD. He asked how this decision was made when colleges were already struggling.

He then raised concerns about the ARC receiving only a nominal increase which amounted to a real decrease, and stated that the ARC was relying on farmers to support trials due to a lack of funding. He questioned if this situation was compromising the ARC’s ability to carry out its work.

On FMD, he asked if the Department had received any vaccines between 1 August 2025 and 31 December 2025 and requested that details of distribution be provided in writing. He also asked if the livestock industry had been consulted on the rollout of vaccines, particularly on concerns about vaccine efficacy and the need for boosters every few months.

He raised questions about the livestock identification and traceability system, noting that export partners such as China had previously required a farm-to-fork traceability system. He asked how far the Department was with amendments to the Animal Identification Act and if the system currently being discussed could be mandatory without such amendments.

He then turned to the blended finance programme. He expressed concern about delays at the Land Bank, stating that sellers were increasingly reluctant to enter agreements because of slow turnaround times, sometimes exceeding a year, and funding being released outside production cycles. He asked what the average time was to finalise a transaction once lodged with the Land Bank and if previous challenges with aligning reporting periods between the Land Bank and the Department had been resolved. He also asked if the Department had confidence in the Land Bank to implement the programme, how many transactions had been finalised through ABSA as an additional partner, and if the blended finance model was functioning as intended.

Finally, he asked about the Agri-BEE fund and requested information on what had been achieved in the previous financial year, noting that reports on this fund were seldom presented. He concluded by questioning how the Department intended to meet food security targets such as supporting 60 000 households and commercialising 370 smallholder farmers when funding for farmer support and sector capacity development had been significantly reduced, and asked which targets would likely be missed as a result of these cuts.

Mr Serage said that many of the challenges raised were linked to the Department's budget constraints. He explained that while members had observed that Programme One was growing while other programmes were declining, this was a direct result of the Department being newly established after the split. He said the Department was still in the process of setting itself up and did not even have a Chief Financial Officer at that stage. He explained that after the split, many positions were lost and the Department now had to re-establish posts in line with a newly developed fit-for-purpose organogram. This organogram had been consulted with the President and was in its final stages of consultation, but he said that if the Department was unable to fund it, the Ministry of Public Service and Administration and National Treasury would not concur with it. He said the additional budget in Programme One was therefore mainly to ensure that the Department could afford and cost this structure. He added that the Department was not satisfied with this reality and appealed for support in securing more funding.

He addressed the decline in plant production and plant health funding and linked this again to the overall budget situation. He explained that from the Department’s allocation of about R7 billion, large portions went to CASP and Land Care, leaving less than R5 billion for the Department's operational responsibilities. He reminded members that certain functions such as national resource management and plant health were not concurrent with provinces, meaning the Department had to maintain offices in places such as Stellenbosch, Gqeberha, the Northern Cape and other areas in order to place inspectors and operate effectively. He said the budget was not sufficient for these responsibilities. He acknowledged that if another serious plant disease outbreak were to occur, the Department would struggle to respond adequately due to limited funding.

On the question of how the APP improved the registration process under Act 36 of 1947, he clarified that the challenge was not necessarily a lack of capacity within the Department to register products, but rather the nature of the registration process itself. He explained that the process was complicated and time-consuming and required detailed scientific data such as toxicology and efficacy reports generated under South African conditions. He said the Department had recently introduced an online registration system as part of a digitisation process, which allowed applications to be lodged electronically rather than physically. However, he said this did not necessarily speed up the process because many applicants relied on consultants who submitted incomplete applications and then failed to respond to follow-up queries. He explained that the back-and-forth communication between the Department and applicants caused long delays, which created the impression that the Department was slow, while in reality the delays were caused by missing information and the need to generate seasonal data. He added that the Act, although old, had been updated through regulations and allowed the Minister to appoint additional scientists and specialists. He noted that the Minister had recently appointed additional technical advisors who could be called upon when needed, which meant that the Department did not have a serious capacity problem in this area.

He then spoke about veterinary services and the vacancy rate for veterinarians. He acknowledged that there were vacancies, although not as high as 45% at national level. He said that in the new structure, all required veterinary posts at national level would be funded. He also explained that the Department received a special allocation for compulsory community service veterinarians, with about 150 placed each year to assist with primary animal healthcare. He admitted that the Department was not at an ideal level of capacity and was managing to break even, and that more funding would improve veterinary services.

On the Biosecurity Hub, he explained that after its launch, the Department realised that it had not been properly institutionalised. He said that issues such as due diligence, allocation of responsibilities and process flows had not been fully designed at the time of launch. The institutionalisation reflected in the APP was therefore a corrective step to formalise roles and processes. He said a charter had since been developed which clearly outlined the roles of the Department, the University of Pretoria, other universities, the Department of Science, Technology and Innovation (DSTI), and the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR). This was intended to ensure accountability and allow the public to measure progress.

He addressed the impact of the shrinking budget on the ARC and said that it was negatively affected and not operating at an optimal level. He explained that the ARC relied on its parliamentary grant and the revenue it generated, but this was not sufficient. He noted that the Minister had approved the establishment of a commercialisation unit within the ARC to enable it to commercialise its intellectual property and improve its financial sustainability, in a similar manner to the CSIR.

On FMD, he said the Department would submit a detailed written report on the vaccines received, their sources and how they were used. He explained that the Minister had set a target to vaccinate 80% of the national herd, amounting to about 11.2 million animals, by the end of December. He clarified that the vaccine currently being used lasts up to six months, unlike the previous vaccine from Botswana, which required boosting after three months. He explained that booster vaccinations would begin after six months, particularly for animals such as dairy cattle that had longer lifespans. He noted that not all animals required boosters, as some would already have gone to slaughter, and that vaccination planning had to consider farming realities rather than simple numerical calculations.

Finally, he spoke about the Livestock Identification and Traceability System and said that the pilot had been successful and was ongoing. He explained that the Department had initially believed that the Animal Identification Act would need to be amended to enforce the system, but legal advice had since clarified that this was not necessary. He said the Minister was empowered under Section 9 of the Animal Diseases Act to issue a directive requiring all livestock to be registered on the system or on systems that interfaced with it. He added that once the server and final processes were handed over from the CSIR to the Department, the Minister would issue the necessary directive to implement the system nationally.

Ms Botsheleng responded to the questions from Mr Montwedi on the target of recruiting 10 000 extension officers. She confirmed that this target had carried over from the previous administration and had remained the same into the seventh administration. She stated clearly that there was no dedicated budget for the recruitment of these extension officers. She explained that even in the previous administration it had only been an announcement without funding, and the Department was in the same position at present. She said that the limited recruitment of extension officers taking place in the nine provinces was being done through provinces’ equitable share allocations and through internal reprioritisation by the Department, but confirmed that there was no funding available for the full realisation of the 10 000 target.

She then addressed the deteriorating infrastructure at colleges of agriculture and the complete budget cut for college infrastructure in the 2026/27 financial year. She said this decision had not been made by the Department and that officials had been equally shocked and disappointed when they received the allocation letter from National Treasury showing that the college revitalisation and infrastructure allocation had been removed. She explained that the Department had written to National Treasury to outline the serious consequences of having a year without infrastructure funding, particularly for multi-year projects where contractors were already on site under agreements with provincial departments. She said the Department had attempted to make internal arrangements through CASP funds to sustain some of these projects, but this had still had a serious impact on the colleges.

She explained that this situation had also affected how the Department approached its APP in relation to colleges of agriculture. In the previous financial year, the Department had included targets in the APP linked to recruitment of young people through these colleges. However, in the current year, because of the refocusing of resources and the infrastructure challenges, the Department had decided not to include APP targets that it would not be able to control financially or otherwise. She said that although infrastructure might seem unrelated to recruitment, in the long term it had a direct impact on the functioning of colleges and their ability to train students.

She also referred to the recent meeting between the Minister and principals of the colleges of agriculture, where the principals had raised the need for these colleges to be recognised or integrated into the national higher education system in line with the Higher Education Act and the Constitution. She reported that this process was underway and that Minister Manamela and Minister Steenhuisen had met in the previous month to consider an appropriate model and approach. She explained that if these colleges were integrated into the higher education system, many of their challenges would be resolved, including access to the National Student Financial Aid Scheme (NSFAS) for students and access to broader infrastructure funding, as the colleges would then operate as recognised juristic entities.

On blended finance, she responded to a specific question about transactions involving ABSA. She reported that in the previous financial year, 100 producers had been supported through blended finance implemented by Land Bank, ABSA and the Industrial Development Corporation (IDC). Of these, 28 transactions had been handled by ABSA, with the majority handled by Land Bank and the IDC. She acknowledged that there had been challenges with Land Bank due to a system hack which had affected alignment of reporting timelines between the Bank and government. However, she said this had since been resolved and that the issue had not affected the Department’s overall performance in blended finance. She added that the Office of the Director-General had arranged a meeting with the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of Land Bank and his team to address concerns around turnaround times and reporting.

She then addressed the question of food security and budget. She said that the only budget the Department could directly account for in relation to household food security was the portion of the conditional grant through Ilima/Letsema. She confirmed that food security as a function was not fully funded and echoed earlier remarks that additional resources were required to meet these critical targets.

Ms S Sithole (ANC, North West) asked questions relating to the SWOT analysis, particularly the Department’s weak relations with the private sector, its reliance on consultants, measures to deal with corruption, and current transformation targets in the sector.

Ms Molotsi responded by referring Members back to the SWOT analysis slides. She explained that the Department had identified weak private sector partnerships as a weakness, but had also identified this as an opportunity to be addressed. She said that the Department was seeking to form partnerships with commodity organisations, strategic partners and donors in order to improve collaboration with the private sector. She further referred to the sector strategic priorities in the Medium-Term Development Plan, where partnership for growth had been identified as a key priority for the five-year period.

On sector transformation, she explained that the Department had set an outcome of increasing the production share of black producers in overall agricultural production in order to stimulate meaningful transformation. She said this outcome was aligned with the Agriculture and Agro-processing Master Plan, which served as the sector's strategy for transformation and growth. She indicated that when the strategic Plan was developed, the baseline contribution of black producers stood at 8%, and the target was to increase this to 20% over five years. She added that there had already been improvement, with the share increasing from about 11% between 2015 and 2019 to 13% between 2019 and 2023, but she acknowledged that more still needed to be done as the Department continued to implement the Master Plan and work towards its targets.

Mr N Pienaar (DA, Limpopo) thanked the Department for the presentation and said that many of his concerns had already been raised by other Members. He chose to focus on a few areas where he felt more detail was needed. He referred first to Programme One and digitalisation. He asked if the 4% target referred to in the APP was starting from a zero base or building on an existing level of digitalisation. He wanted clarity on if this meant the Department was currently only at 4%, or if the 4% was an annual increase towards a larger goal. He also asked what the medium- to long-term Plan was to increase this figure over time. He further asked if the Department had conducted a cost analysis comparing the costs of digitalisation with the costs of using personnel, and what savings in money and working hours could be expected.

He then moved to Programme Three and the support of 100 producers through blended finance. He asked if this programme was implemented at national level or handed over to provinces, and what criteria were used to select the beneficiaries. He asked if selection was based on business plans submitted by provinces or directly by producers. He also requested a full financial breakdown of what constituted the blended finance programme. Lastly, he asked the Minister to comment on the progress made in managing FMD. He said that the distribution of 2.5 million vaccines was encouraging and asked if it was fair to say that the Department had the disease under control. He also asked what provinces could do better and how the NCOP could assist.

Mr Serage responded first to the question on cost analysis for digitalisation. He stated honestly that the Department had not yet conducted such an analysis and did not want to place inaccurate information on record. He explained that this comparison between digital systems and personnel, and if digitalisation was leading to efficiencies or simply adding costs, was still to be undertaken by the Department.

On FMD, he said that the Department believed it was gaining control of the situation. He explained that early delays had been due to the time taken to identify and match the circulating strains of the virus. He reported that the Department had now confirmed that only three strains were circulating and that these were not foreign to the Southern African region. This meant that vaccines could now be produced more reliably. He referred to the Minister’s recent media briefing where it was indicated that more than 14 million doses of vaccines would be imported by the end of the year. He added that the Department expected to receive about 2 million doses every month. He said that improvements to the regulatory framework, finalisation of movement control protocols and provisions for public private participation were all contributing to a more effective response. He indicated that the Department expected vaccination numbers to increase much faster in the coming months.

Ms Botsheleng then responded to the question on digitalisation. She explained that the digitalisation target was linked to the Medium Term Development Plan, which required departments to achieve 40% digitalisation over the five-year period. She clarified that the Department’s baseline at the time of planning stood at 18%. The 4% referred to in the APP was therefore an annual increase from this baseline towards the 40% target. She explained that the 4% target had also been set for the previous financial year and would continue as part of the gradual increase required to meet the five-year goal. She described digitalisation as the process of converting manual and paper-based systems into automated digital systems. She added that more detailed information on the relationship between digitalisation and staffing requirements would likely be available from the Chief Information Officer as part of the Department’s information and communication technology (ICT) strategy.

Mr Montwedi raised a follow-up question on the commercialisation of the ARC. He reminded the Department that this matter had been raised two years earlier during an oversight visit to the ARC centre in Cape Town and asked what progress had been made since then. He then turned to cooperative and enterprise development support, noting that the APP did not reflect any targets or budgets for this programme. He referred to an ongoing dispute between the DoA and the Department of Land Reform and Rural Development (DLRRD) over which Department should implement the programme. He asked the Department to confirm if, since the start of the current administration, no funds had been spent on this programme and if officials appointed to it had effectively been sitting without meaningful work. He proposed that the Committee should schedule a joint meeting with both departments to resolve the matter.

Mr Serage responded by acknowledging that there had indeed been a delay in implementing the commercialisation of the ARC. He explained that this delay had been caused by two main challenges. The first was that the ARC itself had been undergoing a restructuring process which had taken longer than expected. The second was the need to obtain concurrence from the Minister of Finance. He clarified that the primary delay had been on the Department’s side, particularly in ensuring that the ARC was appropriately restructured, which also required funding. He added that the Department had plans and designs in place for the construction of a FMD vaccine production facility, but lacked the R700 million required to begin. He stated that if this funding had been available earlier, the current challenges with FMD would have been less severe and the ARC could have generated significant revenue by supplying vaccines to the region. He confirmed that the necessary approvals for the commercial wing of the ARC had now been obtained and indicated that the Minister would soon announce the launch date.

On cooperative enterprise development, Mr Serage initially requested to respond in writing, which led to an exchange with Mr Montwedi and the Chairperson. The Chairperson instructed him to continue with a response. Mr Serage then clarified that while he did not have all the detailed figures available, he confirmed that Mr Montwedi’s concerns were largely correct. He explained that following the split of the two departments, there had been a dispute over which officials in the cooperative enterprise development unit should be allocated to the DoA and which to the DLRRD. This dispute had taken time to resolve and had even involved arbitration processes. During this period, he conceded that operations in this programme had not been smooth and that there had not been sufficient activity, although some work had continued. He confirmed that the decision had eventually been taken to allocate officials whose qualifications and work were agriculture-based to the DoA, and those whose work was not directly agriculture-related to the DLRRD. He acknowledged that the programme had not functioned effectively during this transition and accepted the validity of the concerns raised.

The Chairperson directed several questions to the Minister and the Department. He first asked about the game industry, noting that it was a multi-billion rand industry globally and in South Africa through safaris and related activities, but that it remained largely untransformed with minimal participation by historically disadvantaged individuals. He asked what practical and concrete interventions had been made to ensure more inclusive participation. He then asked if the country was on track to meet its obligations under the Sustainable Development Goals and the National Development Plan 2030, particularly in relation to reducing poverty.

He also asked the officials about the Department’s compliance with the 30-day payment rule for invoices and if the Department was meeting the required targets for inclusive participation in procurement for women, youth and persons with disabilities. He referred to smart agriculture and regenerative grazing and asked what the Department’s approach was in this regard. He further asked how the Department supported food gardens and rooftop gardens as part of food security. He referred to challenges observed during an oversight visit to Klerksdorp in relation to FMD. He asked if the Department had an active recruitment strategy to attract final-year veterinary students into the Department. He also asked if the Department planned to use exhibitions and exports to promote agriculture as a career and what role the Department played in supporting agri-parks from an agricultural perspective.

Mr Serage responded by first clarifying wildlife and game farming. He explained that a court judgement had removed the competency over wildlife management from the DoA and placed it under the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and Environment (DFFE). He stated that this was the reason why matters relating to wildlife and game farming did not appear in the Department’s APP. He explained that the Department’s only involvement in this space related to animal diseases, as the Animal Diseases Act still applied to wildlife. He said that the Department worked closely with the DFFE through a memorandum of agreement, particularly in relation to the game meat strategy, which had to comply with the Meat Safety Act administered by the Minister. He provided an example of how the Department was working to grant certain dispensations to accommodate the realities of game meat processing, such as allowing animals shot outside abattoirs to be processed within them. He indicated that the Department would have been able to offer broader support if wildlife management had remained within its mandate.

On the Sustainable Development Goals, he indicated that the Department aligned its programmes with international and national commitments, including principles promoted through South Africa’s G20 initiatives. He stated that while eradicating hunger completely remained difficult, the Department had made meaningful progress towards goals related to zero hunger and poverty reduction.

On smart agriculture, he stated that the Department had policy instruments promoting regenerative agriculture under the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act. He said that farmers were incentivised to adopt practices such as zero tillage and other regenerative methods. He added that the ARC had developed models for vertical and hydroponic farming which reused water efficiently, and that these were being promoted as part of food security innovations.

On veterinary skills, he explained that the challenge was not necessarily a shortage of skills but rather funding constraints, especially at provincial level. He said that all funded posts at national level were filled and that additional veterinary posts had been created within the Department’s structure. He noted that the Department annually took in more than 150 individuals to assist with primary animal healthcare. He added that there was a proactive arrangement with the University of Pretoria to reserve spaces for students from previously disadvantaged backgrounds to study veterinary science, subject to admission requirements.

On exhibitions and career promotion, he said that the Department participated in major agricultural events such as the South African Agricultural Trade Show (NAMPO, also known as the National Maize Producers’ Association), where it promoted agricultural practices, technologies and career opportunities, often together with the ARC. He noted that budget constraints sometimes limited participation in smaller events. On agri-parks, he explained that while agri-parks as a concept fell under the mandate of another department, the DoA supported farmers through grants such as CASP and Land Care without differentiating if they were linked to agri-parks or not.

Ms Botsheleng then added a response relating to blended finance and food security support. She explained that blended finance differed from other conditional grants because it combined a government grant portion with a loan portion from institutions such as the Land Bank, IDC and ABSA, and was targeted at medium to large scale farmers. She stated that the Department supported food gardens and rooftop farming through provincial programmes by providing tools, inputs and equipment, particularly to young farmers and communities with limited space.

She also explained that the Department ran a career awareness programme linked to its bursary scheme, working with universities and rural schools to promote agriculture as a career. On inclusive participation, she stated that the Department used norms and standards aligned to national and global targets to ensure participation by women, youth and persons with disabilities across programmes and procurement, and that these were monitored quarterly. She confirmed that the Department’s programmes were aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals as well as regional and international frameworks related to food security.

The Chairperson then indicated that the meeting would be handed back to the Minister for closing remarks before adjournment.

The Chairperson invited the Minister to give his closing remarks before the meeting was adjourned.

Minister Steenhuisen thanked the Members for what he described as a constructive engagement and said the Department had taken careful note of the issues raised. He addressed matters that had been directed to him, beginning with FMD. He explained that in the last quarter of the previous year the Department had announced a new strategy, which moved away from chasing outbreaks through disease management areas and instead focused on mass vaccination of animals across the country. He said this was the first time such a large-scale vaccination programme had been undertaken.

He explained that for the past twenty years South Africa had relied on vaccines from the Botswana Vaccine Institute, but during October Botswana informed the Department that it would not be able to supply the vaccines that had been ordered for November and December. This created a serious difficulty because South Africa no longer had its own vaccine production capacity. The Department therefore had to source vaccines internationally. He clarified that vaccines could not simply be bought off the shelf, as the field strains circulating in South Africa had to be sent to the Pirbright Institute for matching to ensure that the vaccines produced would be effective against the specific strains present in the country.

He said that once matching was completed, the Department secured vaccines from Biogenesis Bago in Argentina and from Dollvet in Turkey. He stressed that the Department had procured every available vaccine in the world that matched South Africa’s field strains. Initially, the Department received smaller batches, but production had since increased. Dollvet was now able to supply six million doses and Biogenesis Bago five million doses, with continued production on a monthly basis. By the end of May, the country would have imported fifteen million vaccines for the year, in addition to the two million used previously. He expressed confidence that this would allow the Department to scale up vaccination significantly, noting that two and a half million animals had already been vaccinated.

The Minister then turned to the challenge of provincial veterinary services. He explained that veterinary services fell under provincial legislative competence, while animal disease control was a national function. This meant the national Department depended on provincial veterinary capacity to implement disease control measures. He expressed concern about the state of veterinary services in some provinces and requested that the Committee assist in encouraging provinces to fill vacant veterinary posts so that a proper biosecurity network could be maintained.

He also referred to budget limitations. He stated that the Department’s budget of just under R8 billion limited what could be achieved, despite agriculture being one of the strongest performing sectors in recent quarters on economic growth and employment. He asked the Committee to assist in making the case to National Treasury for increased funding for agriculture. He noted that he had recently been appointed to the Minister’s Committee on Budget, where he intended to advocate for a greater allocation to the sector.

The Minister welcomed the Chairperson’s concern about the game sector. He stated his view that the court decision placing wildlife and game production outside the DoA had been incorrect. He believed that game breeding and game meat production fit naturally within agriculture, particularly given the links to animal health and improvement legislation. He noted that the barriers to entry into the game sector were high but believed more could be done to support inclusive participation if the function were returned to agriculture.

In concluding, he emphasised that the mass vaccination strategy had two goals. The first was to bring the current outbreak under control by vaccinating 80% of the national herd by December. The second was to gather the data and surveillance needed to apply to the World Organisation for Animal Health for FMD-free status with vaccination. He explained that this status would open access to 94 international markets for South African red meat products, markets that had been closed for decades. He said this would be transformative for local producers and would also promote greater inclusion and growth within the livestock sector, which already had a high level of participation by previously disadvantaged South Africans.

He ended by thanking Mr Serage and the Departmental team for their work and invited the Committee to submit any further questions in writing should clarity still be required. He thanked the Committee again for the engagement before the meeting was adjourned.

Closing remarks

The Chairperson concluded the meeting by indicating that he would hand over to Mr M Modise (ANC, Gauteng), who was the Chairperson of the Select Committee, to deliver closing remarks and a vote of thanks. Before doing so, he requested that Mr Serage provide a written response on the Department’s support for new entrants in livestock farming. He specifically asked for a detailed breakdown per province, showing how support had been given to beneficiaries in relation to cattle, goats, and sheep start-up programmes. He emphasised that this information should be disaggregated across the nine provinces and submitted in writing.

Mr Modise then delivered the closing remarks. He apologised to Members for the disruptions during the day, explaining that it had been a particularly busy parliamentary schedule. He said he had asked Mr Mabilo to chair part of the meeting so that he could attend to other parliamentary responsibilities, and he stressed that this arrangement had been made to avoid any impression of disrespect to the Committee.

He thanked the Minister for making time to attend the meeting on a Thursday afternoon, noting that the Committee normally met on Tuesdays. He acknowledged that the Minister and his team had to adjust their schedules to be present and expressed appreciation for that effort.

Mr Modise then raised an administrative concern on an outstanding report from a previous workshop held in Gauteng. He requested that the Minister engage the DG to ensure that the report was finalised and submitted. He asked that it be provided by the following Tuesday before the end of business, explaining that the Committee needed it in order to finalise its own report to Parliament. He stressed that such reports were necessary for oversight processes and plenary submissions.

He concluded by thanking all Members present, including those who were also participating in other parliamentary engagements such as the rural development indaba. He recognised that Members and officials had been managing multiple responsibilities at the same time. He expressed appreciation for the cooperation and effort that had made the meeting successful, and he closed the proceedings with thanks to everyone involved.

The meeting was adjourned.
