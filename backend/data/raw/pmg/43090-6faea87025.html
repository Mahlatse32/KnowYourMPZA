Briefing on African Liberation Programme Between South Africa & Angola and Audiovisual Co-Production Agreement between SA & Canada

Sport, Arts and Culture

22 May 2026
Chairperson: Mr J McGluwa (DA)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committee on Sport, Arts and Culture met to receive briefings from the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (DSAC) on two international agreements tabled in terms of section 231(3) of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996: the Audiovisual Co-production Agreement between South Africa and Canada, and the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) on the Roads to Independence: African Liberation Programme between South Africa and Angola.

The Chairperson stated that the Committee was considering an important agenda item reflecting its oversight role in the sport, arts and culture sector. He emphasised the importance of international cooperation in cultural preservation and heritage.

DSAC briefed the Committee on the Audiovisual Co-production Agreement between South Africa and Canada, which updates a 1997 treaty to reflect developments in digital media and production technologies. The Department reported that approximately 30 co-production projects had been implemented under the agreement, supporting skills development, job creation, market access, and cultural exchange. The agreement entered into force on 1 January 2026 following completion of ratification processes between the two countries.

On the MoU with Angola, DSAC explained that it provides for cooperation on the Roads to Independence programme, including the repatriation of remains of liberation struggle stalwarts and the development of memorial infrastructure. The Department reported that a technical assessment identified multiple burial sites in Angola, with implementation supported through a Presidential Task Team chaired by the Deputy President. Repatriation efforts are being implemented across selected African and international countries as part of a structured programme.

Members raised concerns regarding the classification of the agreements under Section 231(3) of the Constitution rather than Section 231(2), timing of parliamentary engagement, procurement processes, financial implications of the Angola project, and verification of forensic identification processes.

Members also requested clarity on the economic benefits derived from the co-production agreement and oversight of implementing entities, including the National Film and Video Foundation (NFVF).

DSAC confirmed that South Africa has limited capacity for complex forensic identification of ancient skeletal remains and therefore relies on specialised international laboratories where necessary. The Department further explained that initial cost estimates for the Angola project had escalated significantly, resulting in reconsideration of the project scope due to the absence of a confirmed sponsor.

Members also raised concerns regarding delays in the submission of information by federations and entities, noting that this undermined oversight effectiveness.

The Chairperson acknowledged the concerns and indicated that the matter had previously been escalated for engagement with the Minister and Director-General (DG) of DSAC.

Meeting report

The Chairperson welcomed Members and outlined that the Committee would receive a briefing from the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (DSAC) on two international agreements tabled in terms of s231(3) of the Constitution: the audiovisual co-production agreement between South Africa and Canada, and the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) on cooperation concerning the Roads to Independence African Liberation Programme between South Africa and Angola. He said Members would thereafter deliberate, seek clarity where necessary, consider the minutes, and conclude the meeting.

The Chairperson said the Committee was dealing with an important agenda item reflecting its oversight responsibilities in sport, arts and culture. He noted that engagements of this nature could inform future study tours and international oversight visits. He emphasised the importance of international cooperation in cultural preservation and heritage, stating that such agreements formed part of the Committee’s broader oversight mandate.

The Chairperson said the agreements between South Africa and Canada, and South Africa and Angola, reflected cooperation in the fields of culture and heritage. He noted that the Audiovisual Co-production Agreement with Canada would create opportunities for collaboration between filmmakers and cultural practitioners and support the international circulation of South African content. He emphasised Parliament’s role in interrogating such agreements to ensure legal soundness and effective implementation.

He further noted that the MoU with Angola on the Roads to Independence programme related to liberation heritage and memorialisation. He said liberation remained an important part of South Africa’s historical narrative and informed ongoing work in the sector. He invited Members to engage with the matter while guided by principles of transparency, accountability and transformation, before inviting the Department to brief the Committee.

Point of order

Ms M Mmolotsane (ANC) raised a point of order regarding entities that had reportedly not received their 2025/26 allocations from DSAC. She referred specifically to the South African Sports Confederation and Olympic Committee (SASCOC) and Cricket South Africa (CSA), and questioned how many other entities might similarly not have received funding. She said the Committee required a report clarifying the whereabouts of the funds, particularly as DSAC had earlier indicated during its Annual Performance Plan (APP) briefing that all entities had received their allocations. She further enquired whether the Chief Financial Officer (CFO) was present in the meeting.

The Chairperson agreed that the matter was serious and proposed that it be added to the agenda as an urgent item. He said the Committee could attempt to establish the whereabouts of the CFO and address the matter before the close of the meeting.

Ms Mmolotsane accepted the proposal. As no Members objected to the inclusion of the matter on the agenda, the Chairperson confirmed that the Committee would proceed accordingly.

Apologies

Ms Zoleka Kula, Committee Secretary, informed the Committee that the Minister had apologised as he was attending the inaugural Basketball Africa League (BAL) event in Rwanda. She added that the Director-General (DG) of DSAC had submitted a standing apology as she was attending the Regional Annual Sports Awards (RASA) meeting in Angola.

Briefing on the South Africa-Canada Audiovisual Co-Production Treaty and the MoU on Cooperation Relating to the Roads to Independence: African Liberation Programme between South Africa and Angola

Mr Vusithemba Ndima, DDG: Heritage Promotion and Preservation, Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (DSAC), briefed the Portfolio Committee on the South Africa-Canada Audiovisual Co-Production Treaty and the MoU between South Africa and Angola on cooperation relating to the Roads to Independence: African Liberation Programme.

South Africa-Canada Audiovisual Co-Production Treaty

Mr Ndima said South Africa and Canada shared longstanding bilateral relations rooted in Canada’s support during the anti-apartheid struggle, which had since expanded into trade, investment, science, education and culture. He explained that the original treaty, signed in 1997, remained one of South Africa’s most active co-production agreements and had resulted in 30 projects, with at least one co-production produced annually over the previous five years.

He said the treaty was updated in September 2024 to accommodate developments such as new media and augmented reality, while also ensuring that co-productions qualified for incentives in both countries. Benefits to South Africa included access to Canadian and North American markets, foreign direct investment, job creation, skills development, the promotion of indigenous languages and heritage, participation in international film festivals, and training opportunities for young and historically disadvantaged filmmakers.

Mr Ndima confirmed that Canada had completed its ratification process and that diplomatic notes had been exchanged through the Department of International Relations and Cooperation (DIRCO). In terms of Article 16.1, the treaty entered into force on 1 January 2026.

MoU on the Roads to Independence programme

Mr Ndima explained that Presidents Ramaphosa and Lourenço signed the MoU in December 2024. The agreement focused on the rehabilitation and construction of a monument at the Viana transit camp in Luanda, as well as the repatriation of the remains of liberation struggle stalwarts who died in exile.

He traced the initiative to commitments made by military veterans in 1994, which later culminated in a memorandum of grievances submitted to President Ramaphosa in November 2019. In response, the President established a Presidential Task Team chaired by Deputy President Mashatile. Under this structure, the Department of Military Veterans (DMV) and DSAC jointly chaired the Heritage, Memorialisation and Burial Support Workstream.

Mr Ndima said the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation (UNESCO) adopted the Roads to Independence programme in 2005, while the African Union (AU) endorsed it in 2011. South Africa’s Resistance and Liberation Heritage Route was approved by Cabinet in 2011 and involved 12 ministries. Cabinet further approved a national policy on the repatriation of human remains in 2021.

He reported that successful repatriations from Zambia and Zimbabwe took place in 2024, followed by a homecoming ceremony led by President Ramaphosa on 27 September 2024. A five-year implementation plan had since been developed. Countries identified for 2025/26 included Angola, Lesotho, Zambia and Zimbabwe, while Tanzania, Uganda, Eswatini and Mozambique were targeted for 2026/27.

Mr Ndima said a technical team visited Angola between 13 July and 4 August 2025 and identified between 48 and 89 graves across various cemeteries and camps. He explained that remains would be transported using Department of Defence aircraft and temporarily stored at One Military Hospital before forensic investigation by the Missing Persons Task Team laboratory.

Financial implications and challenges

Mr Ndima said refurbishment of the Viana transit camp was initially expected to be funded through private sector sponsorship. However, estimates provided by the Angolan Ministry of Culture ranged between US$3.78 million and US$4.24 million, equivalent to approximately R50 million to R70 million, which government considered excessive. Alternative proposals under consideration included scaling down the refurbishment project or constructing a smaller monument or memorial wall in central Luanda. These options would be submitted to the Minister for consideration.

Conclusion

Mr Ndima said DSAC remained committed to repatriating all identified remains or, where physical repatriation was not possible, pursuing spiritual repatriation. He recommended that the Committee note the presentation and acknowledge the progress made in implementing the MoU with Angola within the broader Resistance and Liberation Heritage Programme and the work of the Presidential Task Team.

Discussion

The Chairperson thanked Mr Ndima for the presentation and reflected on a personal family experience involving the return of a relative after many years away. He said such processes carried significant emotional weight for affected families and communities. He commended the Department for conducting the work in a manner that upheld dignity, human rights and cultural considerations, and stressed the importance of accountability, integrity and coordination in engagements involving international partners and sensitive historical contexts. He added that repatriation and memorialisation processes contributed to national dignity, social closure and South Africa’s standing in international partnerships. He then invited Members to raise questions of clarity.

Ms Mmolotsane commended the Department for its continued work and said it remained important for families to know where their relatives were buried. She asked whether the Department had encountered situations where families were financially able to repatriate remains themselves but required assistance with negotiations between South Africa and the country concerned. Referring to her family’s experience in repatriating a relative’s remains from Botswana in 2008, she said the process had been difficult due to repeated engagements with the Botswana Embassy. She asked whether the Department could assist families in similar cases by facilitating communication and negotiations between governments and relevant authorities, even where families covered the financial costs themselves.

Ms L Potgieter (DA) said she had several questions and sought clarity on the legal and financial implications of the agreements before the Committee. She questioned why the Committee was only being requested to note the agreements when they appeared to create financial and implementation obligations. She further asked why the agreements had been classified under Section 231(3) of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996, instead of Section 231(2), despite opinions having already been provided by the State Law Adviser. She requested clarity on the extent of parliamentary engagement envisaged and asked whether the Department could indicate the financial obligations arising from the agreements, particularly references to expenditure being funded through “available resources”.

Referring to the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) between South Africa and Angola, she questioned why South Africa would bear financial responsibility for monuments and memorialisation work in Angola. She requested projected costs relating to repatriation, monuments, consultants and related expenditure. Referring to quotations reportedly received from Angolan companies for refurbishment work at Viana, ranging between US$3.7 million and US$4.2 million, she asked who had sourced the quotations and whether their reasonableness had been independently verified.

Ms Potgieter further requested clarity on procurement processes and the legal framework governing bilateral arrangements between companies involved in the project. She questioned why South African companies or other competitive bidders were not being considered. Referring to a private sector sponsor mentioned in the presentation, she asked for details of the sponsor and whether any agreement had been concluded. She also asked who would appoint implementing agents on the South African side and contractors on the Angolan side, and which procurement regulations would govern those appointments.

Referring to previous international heritage projects undertaken by the Department, including the Delville Wood project, she noted that concerns regarding costs had previously been raised in Committee briefing notes and asked whether safeguards had been introduced to prevent similar challenges in the current project.

Ms Potgieter asked how many remains had been positively identified, how many remained possible cases and how many were still unidentified. She questioned the evidentiary basis used to estimate the number of graves reflected in the presentation and requested further information on forensic identification processes. Referring to previous international disputes regarding exhumation, she asked who ultimately determined whether remains would be exhumed or left in situ. She also requested clarity on the consent process followed with families before repatriation.

She further referred to the MoU requirement that Angola provide landmine maps and risk mitigation measures and asked whether these had been received and incorporated into project planning.

Turning to the South Africa–Canada Audiovisual Co-Production Treaty, Ms Potgieter referred to the 1997 treaty that had reportedly resulted in 30 projects and requested that the Department provide a list of those projects. She asked how many South African-led productions had been and whether South Africa had derived measurable economic benefit from the partnership or had mainly functioned as a production location. She further asked how any financial benefits compared with public incentives provided to support such productions.

Lastly, Ms Potgieter expressed concern that the agreement with Canada had reportedly come into effect on 1 January 2026 while the Committee was only being briefed several months later, despite financial and implementation implications. She stated that the Committee should ideally have been briefed when the agreement was concluded and requested clarity on the timing of parliamentary engagement.

The Chairperson thanked Ms Potgieter for her substantive questions and said the Content Adviser could later provide guidance on the procedural issue regarding the Committee being requested only to note the agreements. He added that the Committee retained discretion to compile a report to the House where concerns warranted further consideration, despite the Announcements, Tablings and Committee Reports (ATC) not specifically requiring one. He then requested the Department to respond to Members’ questions before proceeding further.

Responses

Mr Ndima confirmed that the Department could assist families with engagements between South Africa and other countries in repatriation matters. He said the President had written to several countries requesting cooperation whenever South Africa approached them on repatriation processes and compliance with applicable requirements. He stated that support from the Presidency had assisted the Department in carrying out its work.

He explained that although the Department could assist individual families, many preferred the process approved by Cabinet in 2021. He said government had introduced a “country-to-country model” in 2023 because it was more cost-effective to repatriate all remains from a particular country simultaneously, particularly where consent had already been provided by families. He stressed that the Department acted in accordance with the wishes of families and communities involved in the process.

Mr Ndima added that families generally preferred the government-led process because it restored dignity to deceased persons. He said that remains repatriated through official state processes were often received by the President or Deputy President, which accorded the process the necessary dignity and recognition.

On agreements and oversight

Responding to Ms Potgieter, Mr Ndima said the use of the word “note” in relation to the agreements was likely a matter of wording and was not intended to suggest that the Committee should not engage substantively with the agreements. He stressed that the Department remained accountable to the Committee and welcomed questions from Members. He added that some of the questions raised had highlighted issues and blind spots that the Department itself had not previously considered.

Viana Camp refurbishment and sponsorship

Mr Ndima explained that the Department had initially explored assistance from companies already operating in Angola because government did not have sufficient funds for the refurbishment project at the Viana camp. He said the Minister had attempted to identify companies willing to assist and that the intention had initially been to refurbish existing structures rather than rebuild them entirely. He noted that several structures, including the women’s dormitory, remained intact and only required restoration work.

However, once quotations had been received, the Department realised the projected costs were significantly higher than anticipated. He said the quotations suggested costs associated with constructing entirely new structures rather than refurbishment work. As a result, the Department had reconsidered the scope of the project and was exploring what minimal work could be undertaken to restore dignity to the site without incurring excessive expenditure. He added that no sponsor would realistically be able to fund a project at such elevated costs.

Landmines and graves in Angola

Mr Ndima confirmed that the Angolan government had agreed to assist South Africa with identifying and demining affected areas. He said the Department had maintained a positive working relationship with Angolan authorities, who had assisted with records and cemetery information.

However, he stated that the Department had discovered that some graves in Angola, particularly in Luanda, had been reused over time. He explained that this had complicated the process because some remains of South African heroes and heroines might no longer be recoverable.

Forensic investigations and DNA testing

Mr Ndima said the Department was working closely with the Missing Persons Task Team (MPTT) within the Department of Justice and Constitutional Development (DOJ&CD). He stated that the MPTT included forensic archaeologists trained to manage challenges arising during exhumation processes. Where identities could not immediately be confirmed, DNA testing was utilised.

He informed the Committee that some remains had already been sent to Argentina for testing before being handed over to communities. He stressed that the repatriation process remained highly sensitive and complex, but that the MPTT had provided critical assistance.

Mr Ndima further explained that the Department had initially assumed the DNA testing process would be straightforward but later discovered that South Africa did not possess the specialised expertise required to identify very old human remains consisting only of dry bones. He said the MPTT had identified Argentina as having the required expertise for this form of testing.

Budget allocations

Mr Ndima stated that the Department’s 2024 budget allocation amounted to R7.9 million and covered transport, accommodation, homecoming ceremonies and the transportation of remains within host countries. He added that detailed figures could be submitted in writing as the information was already publicly available in official departmental documents.

He further stated that Medium Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF) allocations were projected at R9 million for 2025/26, R9.4 million for 2026/27, R9.8 million for 2027/28 and R10 million for 2028/29.

Procurement processes and sponsors

Addressing procurement concerns, Mr Ndima acknowledged that Ms Potgieter had raised an important issue regarding procurement processes. He explained that because the Department had initially hoped to secure sponsorship support, government itself would not have conducted procurement for refurbishment work at the Viana camp.

He later confirmed that the potential sponsor referred to earlier had been the Trans Hex Group, which operated in Angola. He explained that the company had initially been willing to assist because projected refurbishment costs had been estimated at approximately R3 million to R4 million. However, once projected costs escalated substantially, the company was no longer able to continue with the proposal.

South Africa–Canada Projects

Mr Ndima confirmed that 30 projects had been implemented under the South Africa–Canada agreement and said the Department had the list available and could provide it to the Committee immediately.

The Chairperson interjected and requested that the Department also provide the financial value of the projects.

Mr Ndima responded that determining the value of the projects would require additional work but reiterated that the list itself could be provided immediately.

Use of local contractors

The Chairperson noted that questions still remained regarding implementing agents, the utilisation of South African service providers and the operational agreements for 2026.

Mr Ndima requested, through the Chairperson, that departmental colleagues respond to the remaining matters, particularly those relating to the Canadian agreement. Before handing over, he explained that the Department’s approach had been informed by practical challenges associated with transporting South African contractors to Angola. He said government generally preferred local procurement arrangements, even within South Africa. As an example, he explained that provincial projects would ordinarily utilise service providers sourced from provincial databases rather than companies from other provinces. He stated that these challenges became more complex in cross-border projects, but acknowledged that the matter still required further consideration.

Implementation of film production treaty

Ms Tinyiko Khosa, Chief Director: International Cooperation and Donor Coordination, DSAC, responded on the implementation of the Film Production Treaty. She explained that the Department had recently received updated information on progress following ratification of the agreement and had therefore not been able to include it in the original presentation circulated to the Committee.

Ms Khosa informed the Committee that a South African company, Giant Film Production, would commence implementation of the agreement on 25 May 2026. She stated that the project carried an estimated budget of R32 million. She explained that photography and filming would commence in Cape Town before production later moved to Canada and Ireland. She added that this was the latest implementation information currently available to the Department.

She further explained that the National Film and Video Foundation (NFVF) was responsible for full implementation of the agreement. However, she assured the Committee that DSAC would continue providing updates on progress once implementation formally commenced.

Repatriation estimates

Mr Ndima indicated that he required guidance from the Chairperson as he had been attempting to keep track of all the questions raised by Members. He recalled that one outstanding question related to the estimated number of remains still requiring repatriation. He thanked the Chairperson for identifying the outstanding issue and proceeded to provide figures available to the Department.

Mr Ndima stated that the Department’s current estimate stood at approximately 955 people whose remains still needed to be returned to South Africa. He explained that the figure included approximately 895 individuals located within the African continent, while around 60 were outside the continent in countries including the United Kingdom, the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR), Cuba, the German Democratic Republic (GDR) and Yugoslavia.

He added that the Department had already responded to questions relating to landmines, possible sponsors and project costings. He then invited departmental colleagues to assist with any remaining matters that he might not have covered.

Clarification on 2026 agreement

The Chairperson thanked Mr Ndima but pointed out that the issue concerning the agreement signed in 2026 still required clarification.

Ms Khosa clarified that the information she had presented related directly to the agreement that had come into operation in 2026, namely the Film Production Treaty Agreement. She explained that the projects referred to earlier in the meeting had been implemented under the original agreement signed in 1997. According to her, the film production treaty should not be viewed in isolation from the broader Memorandum of Understanding (MoU), as it flowed from the original agreement while specifically focusing on film production cooperation.

The Chairperson acknowledged the explanation and stated that the matter was now clearer.

Committee oversight responsibilities

The Chairperson referred to a point previously raised by Mr Ndima regarding the role of the Committee in overseeing agreements after they had already been signed and entered into force. Speaking on behalf of the Committee, he asked how the Department viewed the Committee’s oversight responsibilities once agreements were already operational.

Mr Ndima responded that while he might not be fully familiar with every procedural aspect relating to international agreements, his understanding was that agreements were tabled before Parliament so that Parliament remained informed of them. He stated that Parliament and the Committee could therefore call on the Department to provide updates and explanations on implementation. He added that where the Committee had concerns, questions or suggestions relating to agreements, it was fully within its oversight responsibility to raise such matters with the Department.

Further discussion

The Chairperson referred to the legislative framework governing such agreements and stated that he had briefly reviewed the relevant provisions. He explained that agreements of this nature generally bound the Republic upon signature because they were technical or administrative in character. He added that they did not require formal parliamentary approval before entering into force but still had to be tabled in Parliament in the interest of transparency. He further noted that such agreements did not automatically become domestic law unless incorporated through legislation. He remarked that the matter was particularly interesting.

Mr M Daniels (PA) stated that he did not have questions but wished to make a few remarks. He reflected on how perspectives changed once people were personally affected by certain issues. He commended Mr Ndima for a clear presentation and said it had deepened his understanding of the importance of repatriation and African liberation heritage.

Mr Daniels stated that he had recently read a book that further strengthened his appreciation of repatriation processes. He said repatriation restored dignity and demonstrated respect to people who had died far from their loved ones. He added that the return of remains reunited families and provided emotional and spiritual comfort. He stressed that repatriation should not merely be viewed as an administrative exercise or treaty process between countries, but as a deeply meaningful undertaking.

He expressed appreciation to the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (DSAC), the Minister and President Cyril Ramaphosa for recognising the importance of repatriation and ensuring that it took place. Referring to previous repatriation efforts undertaken in Scotland, he said such initiatives had assisted in restoring dignity to families and communities.

Mr Daniels became emotional when referring to the passing of his son, who would have turned 21 years old that day. He said personal experiences of loss created a longing to spend even a single moment again with loved ones who had passed away. He added that the experience had deepened his appreciation of the comfort and healing that repatriation brought to families. He stated that repatriation symbolically brought together the world of the living and the world of the dead, thereby providing peace and comfort to families. He strongly commended the Department for carrying out what he regarded as one of the most dignified responsibilities possible, namely returning people to their final resting places.

The Chairperson thanked Mr Daniels for his remarks and directed a further question to Mr Ndima on the forensic identification process. He asked specifically who was responsible for conducting the forensic work.

Further discussion

Mr Ndima responded that the forensic work was being conducted by the Missing Persons Task Team (MPTT), which consisted of forensic archaeologists with specialised expertise in analysing skeletal remains during exhumations. He explained that the specialists were able to examine body structures in detail in order to determine characteristics of the remains and avoid incorrect identification.

Mr Ndima began outlining one case involving freedom fighters who had drowned after a boat accident and whose remains had later been buried collectively due to the condition of the bodies at the time of burial. He stated that remnants of blankets used during the burial process had still been present during later exhumations and that the Department had ultimately been able to confirm that the remains were those of South Africans.

The Chairperson interjected and indicated that the Committee did not need further graphic detail on the matter. He clarified that the Committee’s concern related primarily to who was conducting the forensic work and whether those experts were based in South Africa. Referring to his attendance at one of the repatriation events, he said observing the emotional condition of families had highlighted the significant psychological impact these processes had on both families and officials involved in the work. He therefore asked whether psychological support was available for families and officials participating in repatriation projects.

Mr Ndima agreed that the Chairperson had raised an important issue. He explained that the Department of Justice and Constitutional Development (DOJ&CD) had a Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) unit which included a psychologist responsible for assisting affected families during these processes.

He added that the issue also affected officials involved in repatriation work. Referring to projects undertaken in Zambia and Zimbabwe, he said officials had initially managed the emotional demands of the work through a strong sense of urgency and responsibility. However, once projects concluded and officials had time to reflect on their experiences, many experienced emotional difficulties linked to the exhumation processes and the conditions they had encountered.

Mr Ndima stated that although the Department had general wellness services available for staff, officials often moved directly from one assignment to another without sufficient opportunity to process the psychological impact of the work. He acknowledged that post-traumatic stress among officials remained a serious concern. He concluded by stating that while psychological support services existed for both families and officials, the demanding nature of the work often limited officials’ ability to fully utilise those support mechanisms.

Mr G Motaung (MKP) welcomed the repatriation initiative between Angola and South Africa and said it would assist families who still did not know where their relatives had been buried. He noted that many families continued to experience unresolved grief because the remains of their loved ones had never been located.

Mr Motaung asked how production houses would be selected in both South Africa and Angola to document and film the repatriation process and related heritage sites. He further asked whether families of deceased persons would derive any benefit from documentaries or films produced about the repatriation process.

He also asked what arrangements would be made once the remains returned to South Africa and whether there would be a central memorial site for repatriated individuals. Referring to earlier repatriation processes involving the African National Congress (ANC) and uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) guerrillas, he recalled that families had previously received support to bury their loved ones and that ANC dignitaries had attended funerals to ensure burials took place with dignity.

Mr Motaung expressed concern that families often received no further support after funerals while documentaries and films relating to repatriation processes were later commercially distributed on platforms such as Netflix and Showmax. He questioned whether families would be required to sign consent forms where filming took place during funerals or repatriation ceremonies and whether families would receive any compensation linked to such recordings or documentaries.

Mr Ndima responded that filming undertaken by the Department was currently managed through the South African Heritage Resources Agency (SAHRA). He stressed that the purpose of the recordings was heritage preservation and archiving rather than commercialisation. He explained that the material was intended to document history for current and future generations.

He clarified that the Department was not utilising commercial film companies for profit-making purposes. He added that SAHRA had already produced documentaries relating to the first repatriation project and had continued documenting departmental visits to Angola and related activities.

Turning to memorialisation, Mr Ndima stated that the President had tasked the Minister on 27 April with exploring the establishment of a Heroes Arch. He informed the Committee that a feasibility study was already underway because a project of that scale required careful assessment before implementation. He added that the initiative remained at feasibility stage while government considered the most appropriate approach to implementation.

Addressing compensation, Mr Ndima clarified that compensation matters did not fall within the mandate of the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (DSAC). He explained that the Presidential Task Team included several workstreams dealing with matters such as pensions and educational support for children of fallen heroes and heroines, which fell under the Department of Military Veterans (DMV).

He stated that DSAC’s mandate related specifically to memorialisation, exhumation, reburials and ensuring that individuals who had contributed to South Africa’s liberation struggle were appropriately honoured.

While responding, Mr Ndima returned briefly to an earlier discussion concerning Zambia. He clarified that the case previously mentioned related to freedom fighters who had drowned in the Zambezi River near Livingstone. He further informed the Committee that the institution conducting DNA verification in Argentina was the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team Genetic Laboratory in Cordoba, Argentina.

Mr Motaung posed a follow-up question regarding the proposed Heroes Arch. Referring to the “Sarah Baartman saga”, he expressed concern about delays previously experienced in memorialisation projects and cautioned that similar delays could affect the proposed Heroes Arch initiative.

He asked what measures the Department would implement to ensure that families would not experience lengthy delays before promised memorial projects materialised. He further asked where repatriated remains undergoing forensic processes would be taken once returned to South Africa and whether an identified site already existed for the proposed Heroes Arch. He requested that plans relating to the project eventually be provided formally in writing, including details on the location of the memorial and entities appointed to undertake the work. He also asked whether proper vetting and auditing processes would apply to contractors appointed for the project.

Mr Ndima acknowledged the concern regarding delays in the Sarah Baartman matter but clarified that the repatriation process differed in important respects. He explained that once remains had undergone DNA verification and were returned to South Africa, families themselves would determine where their relatives would ultimately be buried.

He further clarified that the Heroes Arch was not intended to serve as a burial site for all repatriated remains. Instead, it was envisaged as a national memorial space honouring South Africans who had made significant contributions to the country across various sectors of society. He explained that the concept extended beyond liberation or military figures and could include scientists and other notable South Africans, depending on the wishes of families.

Mr Ndima stated that the Heroes Arch project remained at an early stage and that an inception report already existed. He assured the Committee that the Department would report back to Parliament as progress was made and would disclose appointed service providers once relevant processes had been finalised.

Ms Potgieter stated that she found the forensic process particularly interesting and remarked that Mr Daniels often shared unexpected personal information during discussions. She explained that she had previously considered studying forensics before ultimately pursuing law, although one of her electives had been forensic medicine.

Ms Potgieter suggested that the Committee consider conducting an oversight visit or engagement with the forensic task team in future, where practical and lawful. She believed such an engagement would assist Members in understanding the complexity of forensic identification processes and the lengthy procedures involved in identifying remains. She also raised concerns regarding the shortage of specialised forensic skills within South Africa and suggested that broader parliamentary engagement might be necessary to strengthen national capacity in this area. She added that developing local expertise would likely prove more cost-effective and efficient than continued reliance on foreign institutions.

Returning to budget allocations, Ms Potgieter observed that the amounts allocated appeared relatively low given the estimated 955 remains still requiring repatriation. She requested clarity on the approximate cost per repatriation and the average timeframe required for forensic processes. She stated that this information would assist the Committee in assessing what allocations such as R7.9 million or R10 million realistically meant in operational terms.

Ms Potgieter revisited the sponsorship issue relating to the Angola project. She noted that Mr Ndima had earlier indicated that procurement processes would not apply because sponsorship support had been anticipated, but had subsequently stated that the sponsor had withdrawn after projected costs escalated significantly. She therefore requested clarity on whether a sponsor was still involved, as procurement concerns would remain relevant if no sponsorship arrangement existed.

She further questioned why South Africa appeared to be carrying the full financial burden for the Angola project. She argued that if the infrastructure or memorial would ultimately belong to and be maintained by Angola, there should reasonably be some form of shared financial responsibility between the two countries. While acknowledging the importance of international relations, she requested clarity on negotiations with the Angolan government and the absence of an apparent cost-sharing arrangement.

Ms Potgieter also raised questions regarding accountability for implementation of the Film Production Treaty with Canada. As the National Film and Video Foundation (NFVF) was directly responsible for implementation, she asked whether the NFVF would appear before the Committee alongside the Department to account for implementation of the agreement. She stated that the Committee required clarity on where accountability ultimately rested.

Lastly, she reiterated her request for a detailed list and financial values relating to the 30 projects implemented under the earlier agreement. She expressed concern that the agreement appeared to have been renewed or updated before the Department had readily available value-for-money assessments relating to previous projects. She stated that detailed financial assessments demonstrating the benefits derived by South Africa would ordinarily be expected before continuation or renewal of such agreements.

Mr Ndima welcomed Ms Potgieter’s proposal for an engagement with forensic archaeologists and stated that the Department would appreciate such interaction. He added that the MPTT would likely welcome the opportunity to host Members of Parliament and demonstrate the work involved in repatriation processes. He agreed that an oversight visit would provide Members with valuable first-hand insight into the complexity of the work.

Turning to the sponsorship issue, Mr Ndima clarified that the proposed sponsor had not been an Angolan entity but a South African businessperson operating in Angola. He explained that the Department’s intention had been to approach South African businesses already active in Angola, including mining companies and other enterprises, to assist with the refurbishment project. He stated that because South Africa was repatriating its own citizens, it had initially appeared appropriate to seek support from South African business interests operating in Angola.

Mr Ndima confirmed that there was currently no sponsor for the project because projected costs had escalated significantly. He stated that the Department no longer had any guarantee of external financial support and would therefore need to reconsider the project in its entirety. He acknowledged that Ms Potgieter had raised a valid concern regarding the absence of a cost-sharing arrangement with Angola. However, he explained that the original intention had been to treat the refurbishment as a symbolic gesture of appreciation towards Angola for hosting South African freedom fighters during the liberation struggle.

Mr Ndima also confirmed that the NFVF fell within the Committee’s oversight responsibilities.

The Chairperson asked whether the Department of Public Works and Infrastructure (DPWI) had been involved in the Angola project.

Mr Ndima responded that DPWI had not been involved because the project had initially been intended as a privately funded initiative supported by a sponsor rather than implemented directly through government.

The Chairperson then asked whether the refurbishment project would generate economic benefits for South Africa.

Mr Ndima replied that under the proposed sponsorship arrangement there could potentially have been some benefits for South Africa. However, he noted that the primary economic benefits would likely have accrued to Angola because local labour and services would have been utilised for the refurbishment work. He reiterated that the initiative had originally been intended as a symbolic gesture of appreciation towards Angola. He stressed once again that there was currently no sponsor and that the Department would therefore have to reconsider the project entirely, including whether a smaller monument might ultimately be more appropriate.

The Chairperson raised concerns regarding public perceptions that government should avoid unnecessary expenditure. He asked the Department to explain who had participated in negotiation teams and which authorities had been involved in the agreements and negotiations between South Africa and Angola.

Mr Ndima explained that agreements were generally developed by technical officials from both countries, including officials from the DSAC and their Angolan counterparts. He added that the Department of International Relations and Cooperation (DIRCO) was always involved in international agreements because no such arrangement could proceed without its participation. He stated that DIRCO reviewed agreement contents to ensure compliance with the Constitution, legislation, regulations and protocols governing international agreements. Negotiations therefore involved both technical specialists and international relations officials from both countries.

The Chairperson remarked that Members should note Mr Ndima’s point regarding cultural heritage and tourism. He stated that projects of this nature could potentially benefit Angola through tourism and heritage promotion and that this aspect also required consideration.

Ms Potgieter said that she wanted a firm commitment from the Department that the project would be reconsidered. She argued that because the memorial stemmed from South Africa’s historical support for Angola, it could equally be argued that Angola should express gratitude towards South Africa. She therefore believed that, in the absence of sponsorship, the project should be renegotiated entirely.

While acknowledging that the Committee could not directly instruct the Department, Ms Potgieter strongly urged the Department to revisit the matter. She stated that any benefits arising from the project should primarily accrue to South Africa and South Africans rather than solely benefiting Angola.

Mr Daniels referred to repeated references made during the meeting to struggle stalwarts and reacted strongly to comments which he believed had dismissed his earlier submissions. He accused Ms Potgieter of being dismissive of his lived experience, resulting in a confrontational exchange between Members.

Ms Potgieter objected to the remarks and stated that she had not referred to Mr Daniels’ background or struggle history. She explained that she had appreciated his sharing of personal experiences during previous meetings because it assisted Members in understanding one another better. She denied intending to insult him and stated that the Committee should remain focused on the agenda. The Chairperson intervened to restore order and thanked Ms Potgieter before asking Mr Daniels to proceed.

Mr Daniels then raised a substantive question regarding forensic identification processes. Referring to the earlier presentation that South Africa lacked the capacity to conduct certain DNA tests, he questioned whether all available local options had been fully explored before samples were sent to Argentina.

He referred to a previous missing child case involving Recolin Witbooi, where dry and difficult-to-identify remains had been discovered in a rural area of the Western Cape. He stated that while the South African Police Service (SAPS) had initially indicated that DNA testing would take several months, private testing arranged at a laboratory in the Western Cape had confirmed the identity within a few days. He argued that, where possible, utilising local forensic capacity could shorten timelines and assist families in reaching closure more quickly.

The Chairperson thanked Mr Daniels and described the information as useful. He then asked Mr Ndima whether he was aware of similar forensic institutions within South Africa.

Mr Ndima responded that the Department had experienced considerable frustration with the lengthy timeframes associated with forensic identification in complex cases involving older skeletal remains, including the Livingston 7 matter. He stated that the MPTT had also worked with SAPS but had been advised that the remains involved were extremely old and lacked tissue, making identification especially difficult.

He distinguished such cases from instances where remains were more intact and could more easily be reconstructed or identified. Mr Ndima stated that, in the Department’s experience, the specialised expertise required for very old skeletal remains was not yet fully available within South Africa. He added that SAPS was still developing this capacity, but that the Department could not indefinitely delay repatriation processes while awaiting further local development. He confirmed that the MPTT had therefore recommended the use of specialised external facilities where necessary.

The Chairperson stated that he intended to conclude discussion on the matter and requested that any further questions be submitted in writing. He added that the Committee still needed to address minutes and other administrative matters. Although he did not wish to open a broader debate on matters outside the agenda, he raised concerns regarding unpaid funding allocations to federations.

He stated that the Portfolio Committee required an urgent list of federations that had not received allocations for the 2025/26 financial year. He requested Ms Mmolotsane to assist in formulating the request so that it could be directed to the Department and relevant officials, including the Chief Financial Officer (CFO), who was present.

Ms Mmolotsane requested that the CFO of DSAC provide a list of all federations that had received allocations for the 2025/26 financial year. She further requested that the Committee Secretary write to all federations to confirm whether allocations had in fact been received. She explained that the Committee had previously been informed that all federations had received funding, only to later discover that organisations such as the South African Sports Confederation and Olympic Committee (SASCOC) and Cricket South Africa (CSA) had not received payments.

Ms Mmolotsane expressed concern that additional federations could potentially be affected and stated that the contradiction had been embarrassing for the Committee. She stressed the need for accountability and suggested that the responsible departmental official should account for the discrepancy. She further stated that the Committee could not continue receiving inaccurate information and later discovering inconsistencies. She added that she intended to open a formal case on the matter because of the seriousness of the discrepancy.

The Chairperson then allowed Mr Israel Mokgwamme, Chief Financial Officer (CFO), DSAC, to clarify the request.

Mr Mokgwamme asked whether the reference to “federations” related specifically to sports federations and sought clarity on the payment information being requested for 2025/26. Ms Mmolotsane confirmed that she was referring to sports federations and allocations for that financial year.

Mr Mokgwamme acknowledged the request.

Mr Motaung supported Ms Mmolotsane’s proposal and stated that the Committee should receive not only a list of federations that had been paid, but also the dates on which payments had been made. He requested that the Department provide proof of payment so that the Committee could compare departmental records with information provided by federations themselves. He stated that this would assist the Committee in verifying information and strengthening oversight.

Mr Motaung further suggested that federations should in future be required to confirm receipt of allocations before Committee meetings so that discrepancies could be identified earlier. Referring to previous discussions involving the South African Sports Confederation and Olympic Committee (SASCOC) and disputed amounts, he noted inconsistencies between information provided by the Department and claims made by federations. He added that clarity was also needed on whether allocations related to general federation support or funding for specific events. He stressed the importance of written proof in ensuring accountability and proper oversight.

The Chairperson thanked the Department for the engagement and expressed appreciation for the briefing. He acknowledged the concerns raised by Members and requested the Department to take them into consideration. He then thanked Mr Ndima and the departmental delegation for their attendance and contributions.

Adoption of Minutes

Minutes dated 21 April 2026

Minutes were adopted without any amendments.

Minutes dated 28 April 2026

A Member noted a spelling error relating to the use of “government” with but indicated that the overall content of the minutes was correct. No further amendments were raised.

The minutes were thereafter proposed and seconded, and the Committee adopted the minutes of 28 April 2026 with the minor correction noted

Minutes dated 07 May 2026, 08 May 2026

Minutes were duly adopted.

Committee matters

Ms Mmolotsane raised concerns regarding delays by federations and entities in submitting information requested by the Committee. She stated that information was frequently submitted several months after requests had been made, sometimes only being received in May for matters raised in March or earlier. She said that by the time the information was received, Members had often already moved on from the discussions and could no longer properly recall the context of the original requests.

Ms Mmolotsane stressed that the delays undermined the Committee's oversight work and made it difficult to track matters effectively. She stated that it was unreasonable for the Committee to set clear deadlines, only for responses to be submitted long after those deadlines had passed. She expressed frustration at the need for repeated follow-ups on outstanding information. She noted that although she frequently raised the matter through emails, she did not believe the problem lay with the Committee staff. Instead, she attributed the delays to the Department's failure to ensure compliance by federations and entities. She urged that stronger emphasis be placed on enforcing submission deadlines so that information was received within the timeframes determined by the Committee.

The Chairperson acknowledged the concerns raised by Ms Mmolotsane and noted that the matter had previously been discussed by the Committee. He expressed appreciation for the work of the professional support staff, stating that they had consistently followed up with departments and entities regarding outstanding information and compliance with Committee requests. He added that repeated reminder correspondence had been sent, but delays in submissions nevertheless continued.

He further stated that he had personally engaged with the official responsible for coordinating the requested information and understood that the process often depended on obtaining submissions from multiple sources before consolidated responses could be provided to the Committee. He recalled that the Committee had previously resolved to engage directly with the Minister and Dr Cynthia Khumalo, Director-General (DG), DSAC, in an effort to address ongoing delays and improve coordination.

He emphasised that while effective oversight remained important, the Committee also needed to remain fair and reasonable towards departments and federations by allowing adequate timeframes for responses, which generally ranged between seven and 14 days depending on the nature of the request. He added that the ongoing cycle of reminder emails and follow-ups had become burdensome and frustrating for both the Committee and officials involved in the process.

Closing remarks

The Chairperson stated that the Committee would further reflect on ways to improve information management and oversight processes during its forthcoming planning and training sessions. In closing, he thanked Members for their contributions during the meeting and expressed appreciation to the professional support staff and all participants for their work and engagement.

Meeting adjourned.
