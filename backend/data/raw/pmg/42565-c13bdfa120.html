Germany-SA Joint Declaration of Intent on Agricultural Development: meeting with German Minister of Agriculture

Agriculture

11 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Committee welcomed Germany’s Federal Minister of Agriculture, Food and Regional Identity Mr Alois Rainer together with his delegation. South Africa and Germany had strengthened their agricultural cooperation through the 10 March 2026 signing of a Joint Declaration of Intent on Agricultural Development aimed at enhancing collaboration in the control of Foot and Mouth Disease and promoting safe trade in animal products. The signing represented a significant diplomatic milestone, marking the first visit by a German Minister of Agriculture to South Africa in 13 years. It signalled renewed cooperation between the two countries in addressing key challenges related to biosecurity and international agricultural trade. The partnership focuses on vaccine research, diagnostic tools, and surveillance to combat ongoing FMD outbreaks.

In the discussion Members sought Germany’s guidance about the manner in which it dealt with FMD, food wastage and involving traditional leadership institutions in its agricultural sector. Members welcomed the signing of the Joint Declaration and looked forward to further collaboration with Germany and expanding its partnership to other areas.

Meeting report

The Chairperson gave a warm welcome to Germany’s Federal Minister of Agriculture, Food and Regional Identity, Mr Alois Rainer, together with his delegation and Members. Minister Rainer’s presence reflected the strong and growing partnership between the two countries.

The Committee was aware Minister Rainer met with South Africa’s Agriculture Minister Mr John Steenhuisen and signed the Joint Declaration of Intent on Agricultural Development aimed at enhancing collaboration in the control of Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) and promoting trade in animal products. She stated that this was an important step towards strengthening animal health management, safeguarding livestock industries, combating FMD and supporting agricultural trade between the two countries.

She highlighted how since assuming office Minister Rainer had emphasised strengthening the agricultural sector, supporting farmers, promoting innovation and sustainable farming practices, and ensuring that rural communities remained vibrant and economically viable. These priorities resonated strongly with the Committee’s own efforts in South Africa in developing a resilient and inclusive agricultural sector.

Germany remains an important partner to South Africa in agricultural cooperation, technology exchange and trade. As legislators tasked with oversight and policy development, the Committee valued opportunities such as this to exchange views and explore areas of deeper collaboration with Germany. The Committee appreciated the Minister was a Member of Parliament before and understood the importance of oversight over the executive. She hoped Minister Rainer could share some experiences and lessons from the past. The Committee was honoured by the visit and looked forward to constructive engagement.

Federal Minister of Agriculture, Food and Regional Identity

Minister Rainer thanked the Committee for taking the time today to engage and he was delighted to meet everyone. He explained he would have loved to visit South Africa on the occasion of the G20 but was unable to do so because Germany’s Parliament was in session that week and since Germany had a slim majority, it was always crucial to be there and to be present.

South Africa was truly a vital partner for Germany mostly because of its political stability and the numerous ties Germany has with South Africa in the fields of business and research. He noted two examples. Germany had a project in cooperation with the Southern African Confederation of Agricultural Unions, which addressed the introduction of climate resilient production methods. Moreover since 2025 Germany had been cooperating with the African German Centre for Sustainable and Resilient Food Systems and Applied Agricultural and Food Data Science (UKUDLA). He was certain that there was much more to discover and learn from South Africa and looked forward to this mutual exchange with Members.

Minister Rainer had a bilateral meeting with Minister Steenhuisen the previous day where Minister Rainer underlined a strong interest in increasing Germany and South Africa’s cooperation in terms of trade and exports. In this engagement he emphasised Germany advocated for trade as equal partners on an equal footing. He also had the opportunity to meet Minister Steenhuisen in as early as January this year on the occasion of the Green Week exhibition in Berlin and the Global Forum for Food and Agriculture. On that occasion we already discussed the FMD outbreak in South Africa.

Minister Rainer stated that part of his delegation included Prof Kuhn from the Friedrich Loeffler Institute (FLI) which is the Federal Research Institute for Animal Health, as well as Dr Ruhmroeder, who was Germany’s newly appointed Chief Veterinary Officer for Exports to provide expertise and input on this to Members. He emphasised the FLI was not only for Germany and its agricultural sector but also for South Africa’s extremely renowned institution UKUDLA which he regarded as one of the best that there is in the world.

As mentioned South Africa and Germany signed the Joint Declaration which aimed to increase cooperation and to fight and combat FMD. Thiis declaration was really meant as a starting point for a bilateral administrative partnership that was looking into animal health and in particular FMD. He emphasised Germany’s exports interests and said Germany would appreciate it if South Africa and Germany could bilaterally agree on the regionalisation concept for African swine fever. He added a large business delegation from Germany was also present in the country having other discussions. He highlighted German businesses had long been strong partners in and for South Africa.

Coming back to the Chairpersons’ opening statement, he said the German Parliament also had an Agriculture Committee which played an oversight role over government’s work. In talking about agriculture in Germany it was important to appreciate the agricultural sector, valuing what agriculture does which was providing the country’s daily bread. Secondly was valuing anyone and everyone who worked in agriculture whether a farm owner or worker. He emphasised everyone should be able to make a decent living and be able to survive on the wages they were paid.

In talks about strengthening the role of agriculture and the entire sector, making it more resilient, more sustainable, it was also important to look at markets and what markets were available. In Europe, Germany was in a relatively good situation, but of course, it required access to balanced markets, and that entails not only exports, but also imports. Certainly in Europe and in Germany, there were products they simply could not grow for themselves. Therefore they relied on trade in these products which some South Africans may be able to grow. He believed that likewise, South Africa might also be in need of certain products that Germany could provide. He thanked Members once more for the opportunity to come before them.

Discussion

Mr R Cebekhulu (IFP) asked what was Germany’s view on the production of genetically modified food for human consumption. Secondly, the Minister mentioned bringing a veterinarian in its delegation and about the treatment of FMD. In South Africa wild game buffaloes were referred to as the carriers of FMD. He asked what was the carrier of FMD in Germany.

Minister Rainer said in Germany and over Europe there were clear regulations and provisions on Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs) in food and one requirement was that it must be labelled accordingly whether a product was GMO free or not. He added Germany’s consumers really cared to know this information. There was a discussion at the moment which referred to Novel Genomic Techniques (NGTs) and the plan was NGT 2 must be labelled in the same way GMO foods simply for consumers to know what they were buying. NGT 1 on the other hand was a different category which he referred to as accelerated breeding. The decision was made at the European Union (EU) level – and the European Trilog was yet to conclude this discussion – however the idea was that the entities which fell within this category no longer had to be labelled. It was quite complex however this was the general distinction between the two.

Prof Christa Kühn, President of the Friedrich-Loeffler-Institut, addressed the question on FMD. The last outbreak of FMD in Germany was in January 2025 and the carrier was also a buffalo. However, the entry thereof on how it came to Germany was never fully clear. It was assumed it was imported by travellers bringing with them some food from countries where the disease was more prevalent. Germany had indication that the strain was closely related to strains impacting eastern Turkey or the Iranian area. Therefore the assumption was that some vacation travellers brought some food with them and then dropped it resulting in birds eating this – it then coming into the area where the buffaloes roamed. This was the assumption Germany had on FMD’s entry into the country.

She added interestingly the FMD outbreak before this was in 1988 in the German Democratic Republic area. This created a problem because the outbreak came from a vaccine-producing facility. Thus vaccine production was not without risks for the environment if it was not done properly. At that time, more than 30 years ago, the virus was released when the vaccine was produced. It was an unfortunate byproduct of vaccine production.

Ms L Arries (EFF) said South Africa was facing a huge challenge – every day more than 15 million people did not have access to food. Nearly 9 million tons of food was wasted every year, while there was 15 million people in the country that did not have access to food. She asked how Germany dealt with food wastage or excess food.

Ms Arries noted that the Joint Declaration was just signed and asked what concrete technical or financial support Germany would provide to strengthen South Africa's capacity to control FMD and if there are any timelines in this agreement for when would it be implemented. Following from this agreement what steps would Germany take to support and improve access markets for South African animal products, once the FMD has decreased. Lastly, would the coordination include the transfer of veterinarians to aid in preventing FMD in the future.

Mr Z Mthethwa (MKP) said his question related to traditional leadership institutions. He was aware Germany had a German Kaiser who was one of the kings in Germany. South Africa was in a peculiar situation where its kings had access to land but did not have access to capital, financing and technology. He added South Africa sought to balance Germany’s needs with products it could not produce. South Africa specialised in organic products which were not genetically modified and sought to keep such methods whether it be in animal production or animal farming or crop production – this was still done in an indigenous way.

He requested the bilateral agreement between the two countries include traditional leadership institutions to participate in such agreement recognising they had their own land which they too looked to cultivate and would need capital to do so which capital Germany had. He said the ask was for an exchange in technological transfer on agricultural products, packaging of those products and mechanisation in the farming community. Many a time South African farmers were not able to farm, not because they were not good at it but because modern day farming methods required an advanced technological way of doing things — all of which Germany had. South Africa was also interested in Germany’s agricultural shows which would expose traditional leadership institutions to the different markets available in the import/export industry.

Mr Mthethwa highlighted unemployment in South Africa and sought Germany’s intervention in possibly hosting an exchange problem for unemployed graduates in the agricultural sector to up-skill them in order for them to enter the agricultural space.

Mr S Sekoati (ANC) commended the decision taken by Minister Rainer to join South Africa in the fight against FMD. He welcomed steps taken to work together particularly in research on food production. This was incredibly important because whether in war times or peace times all countries sought to feed their people. He hoped the relationship between the two countries would be strengthened and partnership expanded to other areas particularly on modern farming methods to further expedite food production. Such partnership was the way forward especially considering the current global uncertainties.

The Chairperson said somewhere around 2024, if not early 2025, South Africa was shown to have exported a lot of agricultural produce to the EU at around 19%, with citrus being one of the leading exports. She sought the Minister’s view on South Africa exporting citrus particularly oranges and the issue of citrus black spot in the EU – considering South Africa exported citrus to various countries without complaints about black spot.

Response

Minister Rainer said Germany also had a food wastage issue with 12 million tons of wastage and if you relate it to the headcount, it would probably be almost the same. What was noteworthy was that 60% of this food waste occurred at the level of private households. Many of his predecessors as agriculture ministers had attempted to address this issue. Which was why they started the initiative Too Good for the Bin, which was set up in cooperation with retailers and industry associations. The idea was also to collect data. The current administration was further seeing to the project taking off. The project was showing effect, but also stagnating slightly.

Minister Rainer said it was important to invest in education and raising awareness about what food actually was, because many people did not really know how food was being produced. Social media was crucial in doing this. Germany was also looking at cooking shows on the occasion of the Green Week exhibition to teach people about food waste. There was currently a discussion about the Best Before data that is printed on products. That is because many people actually think that Best Before meant you have to throw it out right away – leading to food wastage – and of course that was not the case.

On traditional leadership he highlighted agriculture had always used its traditional methods while at the same time adapted to changes, adapting to new times. This was essentially part of the game. You must be able to adapt to what is new and you must be able to allow for progress to happen if you want to continue to produce using traditional methods. He highlighted there was a transfer of technology that was already happening.

His delegation included representatives from businesses that were involved in agricultural machinery and were in the process of accessing new markets. He emphasised there were plenty of markets that could be tapped into especially when looking at organic production. These could be used together. Conventional or traditional ways of producing and modern innovative ways did not always exclude each other. They do not cancel each other out, but of course agriculture must adapt to the times – it must change just like the economic system; the economy and climate was changing and therefore responses thereto must too.

On training, Minister Rainer said the German delegation had visited the UKUDLA project located at the University of the Western Cape, which has an excellent exchange program already up and running — together with another program between the University of Pretoria and the German University in Hohenheim. Thus there are plenty of opportunities for this exchange already – also looking at other partners, such as the Hanns Seidel Foundation.

Minister Rainer noted that South Africa was one of the most important producers across the globe for the export of citrus fruit to the EU. Further, Germany supported any and every effort to resolve this matter to be able to resume trade in citrus fruit.

Prof Kühn addressed the question on how the Joint Declaration came to life. The previous day they had met with colleagues including the Chief Veterinary Officer of South Africa, and together with the scientific experts, they had agreed to establish a working group at the veterinary level. The working group would be coming together as soon as possible – first to identify what was needed most for South Africa. What was clear at the moment was that South Africa required good diagnostics and Germany had a lot of expertise in this area. There would be the exchange of knowledge but also Germany would be sending veterinary experts to South Africa.

Closing Remarks

The Chairperson thanked the German Minister and his team, appreciating the responses to all Members’ questions. She welcomed the working groups which were going to be put in place. This would allow Members the opportunity to monitor the work that the working groups committed to doing and the related timelines. She looked forward to South Africa expanding is relations with Germany to other areas including the exports and imports market and welcomed other agreements between the two countries. She looked forward to sustaining its relationship on an equal footing.

Meeting adjourned
