DHA & BMA APP 2026/27; Marriage Bill: PLS response; with Minister and Deputy Minister

Home Affairs

24 April 2026
Chairperson: Mr M Chabane (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

Annual Performance Plan (APP) of Government Departments & Entities 2026/27

The Committee convened to consider the annual performance plans and budgets of the Department of Home Affairs (DHA) and the Border Management Authority (BMA), and to be briefed on legal issues arising from the Marriage Bill.

The Minister said the Department was accelerating its reform programme and building on the previous year’s foundation for digital transformation under the “Home Affairs at Home” approach. He said the plan was consistent with the DHA’s earlier policy direction and was focused on speeding up service improvements and reform delivery.

The DHA presented its annual performance plan (APP) and budget, stating that it aimed to fully transform services through digital systems, including online applications, facial recognition, electronic payments, and expanded digital identity services. This would improve access, efficiency, and inclusion, while supporting national development goals such as economic growth and job creation. It also highlighted plans to digitise large volumes of records, roll out smart identity document (ID) cards, automate birth registration, expand service points, and introduce immigration reforms such as an electronic travel system and e-passports. Referring to the budget framework, it indicated that funding was limited and required strict cost control and reprioritisation.

Committee Members welcomed progress on smart identity cards and rising issuance numbers, but stressed persistent gaps in birth registrations, school-based applications, and access for vulnerable learners. They raised concerns about system delays, complaint handling, and weak service delivery at missions abroad, as well as possible syndicates and corruption within Home Affairs’ operations. Questions were also asked about infrastructure, office capacity in rural areas, and if no new funding over the medium term would limit reforms. They challenged the adequacy of funding for the BMA, and sought clarity on digitisation progress, including whether records at foreign missions were being included, and also questioned coordination with other departments on enforcement, labour inspection, and border security. The Chairperson raised concerns on infrastructure issues, border security, audit findings, and preparation for the forthcoming local government elections.

The BMA presented its APP, outlining its goal of securing the country’s borders through digital systems, improved coordination, and stronger enforcement. It explained the entity’s alignment with government priorities, and highlighted risks such as funding and capacity constraints, commenting that it remained underfunded despite Treasury increases and transfers from departments. Most of its budget went to salaries, leaving limited funds for operations, infrastructure, and technology. It said that irregular expenditure came from lease agreements inherited during the BMA’s formation, and was not due to misconduct. It was correcting this through proper leases, renovations, and compliance processes, with no officials held liable, but with training being introduced.

Committee Members welcomed progress in border management, including reduced illegal crossings, increased traveller processing, and stronger anti-corruption enforcement, with several officials dismissed. However, they raised serious concerns about the funding shortfalls, weak staffing levels, delays in transferring border functions from the South African Police Service (SAPS), and unclear legal clarity on whether such transfers were mandatory. They also questioned performance indicators showing 100% success, arguing that undetected illegal activity was not being measured properly. Further concerns included corruption controls, lifestyle audits, cybersecurity readiness, uneven rollout of technology at ports, and reliance on uncertain funding sources, such as the Criminal Assets Recovery Account. The Chairperson noted unresolved issues around funding, SAPS integration, infrastructure, and audit matters, and confirmed the needed for further follow-up.

The Committee legal advisor presented the legal issues arising from the Marriage Bill within the constitutional framework of respect for dignity, equality and human rights. She explained that the Bill aimed to correct past exclusions in marriage laws, including recognition of previously unrecognised marriages, and responded to a Constitutional Court ruling that required Parliament to remedy the non-recognition of Muslim marriages. The Bill had been revived in the current Parliament after delays in the previous one. The key provisions dealt with marriageable age, consent requirements, recognition of different forms of marriage, and the regulation of polygamous marriages. She stressed that consent remained central to constitutional validity.

The DHA agreed with much of the legal presentation, but had raised concern about the royal family consent clause, arguing that it might affect customary leadership processes and required further explanation.

Committee Members raised concern that legal positions were not fully aligned, and that further clarity was needed. It was suggested that if problems remained, alternative wording options should be provided. A Member questioned the lack of final agreement, and raised the need for clearer departmental justification. The Chairperson agreed that further consultations were required, including with the Department of Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs, and requested a consolidated report with clear positions and possible amendment options.

Meeting report

Opening remarks

The Chairperson opened the meeting by greeting everyone and welcoming the Minister, Deputy Minister, Director-General (DG) and all Deputy Directors-General (DDGs). He noted the absence of the Border Management Authority (BMA), and said that either the Minister or the Deputy Minister would need to brief the Committee on the implications of their absence during the first presentation by the Department of Home Affairs (DHA), especially if issues arose that related to the work of the BMA. He also welcomed the Parliamentary Legal Services, the Chief State Law Advisers and all those in attendance.

He reminded the Committee Members that in the previous meeting they had struggled to finalise several items and had agreed to prioritise the annual performance plans (APPs) and the adoption of minutes required for affidavits linked to the extension of the Marriage Bill. Other items had been agreed to roll over into the fourth quarter as the Committee continued its work. As a result of that decision, the Committee had agreed to deal specifically with two items related to the APPs

The Chairperson recalled that the Committee had initially intended to receive a report from the BMA on the Easter operations. However, several concerns had arisen on the work of the BMA and other law enforcement agencies. He also referred to a matter raised by Adv Conny Moitse, DDG: Counter Corruption and Security Services, during a presentation by the Special Investigating Unit (SIU) concerning traffic register numbers (TRNs). Because of these issues, the Committee had agreed that it would be important to engage the Department of Transport (DoT) and the Cross-Border Road Transport Agency (C-BRTA). He explained that during the Easter weekend, buses carrying undocumented foreign nationals had been intercepted through the work of the Free State provincial government and its Department of Transport and Safety. These buses had passed through border processes where all documentation had been certified as correct, yet problems had been discovered later.

He said that the Committee had engaged with the Chairperson of the Portfolio Committee on Transport and had agreed to facilitate a joint meeting. This meeting would receive the Easter report from the BMA, a report from the C-BRTA on its collaboration with the BMA, and a report from DDG Moitse on the concerns identified around the TRN. The purpose was to gain a comprehensive understanding of the weaknesses in the transportation system that were linked to the work of border authorities. He expressed growing concern about buses transporting people from other countries into South Africa, and emphasised the need to examine the mechanisms in place and the question of accountability and punishment for those found to be operating outside the law, noting that this responsibility did not rest solely with the BMA.

He said this background was the reason the Committee believed it was necessary to have a joint meeting in order to receive a comprehensive response. Both Minister Dr Leon Schreiber and Deputy Minister Njabulo Nzuza from the DHA, as well as Deputy Minister Mkhuleko Hlengwa from the DoT, would be informed of the intention to hold this joint meeting and that correspondence had already begun. He clarified that this was also the reason the item on the Border Management Authority had been dropped from the current agenda, and that this would be addressed before the Committee adopted the APPs for the budget vote.

The Chairperson said that the following week the Committee would conduct oversight focusing on the modernisation hub, particularly the information technology area, to assess the progress made and the challenges faced by the Department. He had been alerted through WhatsApp messages that some individuals were mobilising to disrupt the Committee’s oversight visit at one of the Department’s entities or offices. He stressed that the Portfolio Committee had a constitutional responsibility to conduct oversight, and was not visiting the Department to “witch-hunt” anyone, solicit favours, or create conflict. Rather, the purpose was to ensure accountability and responsibility. He made it clear that the Committee would hold the Ministry and the Director-General accountable, and warned against any attempts by employees or others to disrupt the Committee’s work. He emphasised that the Constitution empowered the Committee to hold the Department to account on any matter.

After apologies had been received and minuted, the Chairperson invited the Minister to introduce the annual performance plan of the Department of Home Affairs.

DHA’s 2026/27 APP and 2026-2029 budget

Minister’s overview

Minister Schreiber said that from his perspective, the APP represented an acceleration of the reform journey already underway. He said that when comparing the current plan with that of the previous year, it became clear that the previous year had focused mainly on laying the foundation for the work of the seventh administration. This foundation had been necessary for the reform process referred to as “Home Affairs at Home,” and for the digital transformation that formed the centre of this journey. He said that when looking at the current APP, it was evident that the Department was now building on that foundation, and that there was a clear intensification of several reforms that had been discussed with the Committee during the previous year.

He emphasised that this was the correct approach, as the first year of an administration should set direction, establish a vision and lay the necessary conceptual and policy groundwork. In the second year, the expectation should be to accelerate in that same direction. He reassured the Committee that the Department was not presenting a plan that deviated from the policy direction previously adopted. Instead, the plan was coherent and showed that the Department was logically prioritising and pursuing the same path.

He said it would not be desirable to have APPs from one year to the next that were inconsistent or lacked a clear vision, and he believed this consistency was reflected in the current presentation. He commented that many of the initiatives in the plan would not be new to Committee Members, but he believed they would be able to assess them in relation to where the Department had started the previous year. He referred to the expansion of access to civic services and the work underway to scale up the Electronic Travel Authorisation (ETA) system as examples of initiatives that were continuing but now needed to be accelerated.

He said the key question facing the DHA was how to speed up the implementation of these reforms. He expressed confidence that the Department was moving in the right direction, and appreciated the role played by Committee Members, many of whom had sent parliamentary questions focused on how the Department could accelerate its work. He viewed this as an indication of cross-party support for the reforms required, and said he valued this support.

The important question now was how the Department could do more to increase the pace of the reform journey. He invited inputs from Committee Members, and said the Department would benefit from a discussion that focused on this issue. He concluded by saying that the APP was about building on the foundation already laid and accelerating in the same direction in which the Department and the Committee had been working together over the past 21 months.

DHA presentation

Mr Tommy Makhode, Director General, DHA, presented the Department’s APP for the 2026/27 financial year, together with an overview of the budget for the 2026/27 to 2028/29 period, explaining in detail that the purpose of the presentation was to formally table before the Committee the Department’s planned performance targets, strategic priorities, and financial allocations over the medium term.

He began by outlining the Department’s vision, mission, mandate, values, outcomes, and impact statement, framing these within the broader transformation agenda referred to as the “New Home Affairs: Home Affairs @ home.” He said this vision was anchored in the Department’s 2025 to 2030 strategic plan and its digital transformation strategy, which collectively formed the foundation for fundamentally changing how the Department operated and delivered services. He emphasised that the desired end-state was a fully digitally transformed DHA in which all civic, immigration, and refugee services would be delivered through decentralised electronic channels rather than being dependent on physical offices, thereby dramatically expanding inclusion, improving accessibility, and restoring dignity to all clients. He stressed that this vision was directly aligned with the apex priorities of the Government of National Unity (GNU) – namely, economic growth and job creation, poverty reduction, and the building of a capable and developmental state, and he argued that a modernised and digitally enabled Home Affairs would contribute meaningfully to all three priorities by acting as an economic enabler, improving access to enabling documents, and revolutionising government service delivery.

He said the Department’s digital transformation would involve a combination of adopting advanced digital tools and technologies, rethinking and redesigning business processes and service delivery models, changing the organisational culture and mindset to embrace innovation, improving customer experience through seamless digital channels, and leveraging data analytics to enhance planning and decision-making. He further indicated that beyond improving Home Affairs itself, the transformation had the potential to unlock wider value for government by providing a central digital platform that could support the digitalisation of additional public services across the state. Explaining the practical expression of this vision, he highlighted key service innovations such as the ability for clients to apply for documents online, the use of facial recognition technology for identity verification, the introduction of online payment systems, and offering clients flexible delivery methods of their documents, all of which were aimed at improving efficiency, convenience, and security.

Mr Makhode then situated the Department’s work within the broader national policy framework by explaining its contribution to the National Development Plan (NDP) 2030, particularly in addressing the triple challenges of poverty, inequality, and unemployment through enabling inclusive economic growth. He emphasised that the Department played a critical role in ensuring that all citizens were included in democracy and development by providing them with a legal identity and status that enabled access to rights, services, and economic opportunities, and said this had to be done in a manner that was efficient, effective, professional, and secure.

He also highlighted the Department’s role in facilitating the acquisition of critical skills needed for economic growth through immigration processes, working in alignment with the Department of Higher Education and Training (DHET) to build the country’s skills base. He noted the importance of integrated and coordinated border management, explaining that through the BMA the Department contributed to ensuring that South Africa’s borders were effectively protected, secured, and managed. He added that the Department supported regional development by working with Southern African Development Community (SADC) countries through the Department of International Relations and Cooperation (DIRCO) to establish secure and efficient migration systems, and stressed that the Department’s modernisation programme would reduce fraud, lower the cost of doing business, enable e-government, and ultimately attract greater investment into the country while positioning South Africa to benefit from the Fourth Industrial Revolution.

He proceeded to outline the DHA’s commitments to the 2024 to 2029 medium term development plan (MTDP), as well as the 2026 State of the Nation Address (SONA), providing a detailed account of the key deliverables that the Department had undertaken to implement. He said it would contribute to public and social employment by appointing unemployed young people to participate in the digitisation of more than 340 million paper-based civic records, which formed a central component of its modernisation programme. The Department would continue with its information and communication technology modernisation initiatives, focusing on strengthening network infrastructure, ensuring system stability, and advancing digital transformation projects. He indicated that the Department would continue implementing its repositioning programme to give effect to the White Paper on Home Affairs, and would accelerate the rollout of the smart identity document (ID) card to ensure that all eligible citizens and permanent residents possessed the more secure form of identification, while gradually discontinuing the green ID book. He also explained that the Department would automate the birth registration process for both citizens and foreign nationals at public and private health facilities, thereby improving efficiency and ensuring early registration.

Mr Makhode highlighted that the DHA would expand access to its services by increasing its footprint and improving coverage, including redesigning Home Affairs’ offices to enhance efficiency, utilising mobile offices and forming partnerships with both public and private sector institutions to reach more people. He said the Department aimed to obtain and maintain an unqualified audit opinion, demonstrating sound financial management and governance, and would implement the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) to safeguard citizens’ data in an increasingly digital environment. It would also support the objectives of the district development model (DDM) by ensuring access to enabling documents at the local level, would implement measures to combat gender-based violence and femicide (GBVF), and would promote inclusion by addressing the needs of women, youth, and persons with disabilities. He also emphasised the Department’s commitment to complying with international, regional, and national obligations.

Mr Makhode then provided a detailed exposition of the key elements underpinning the Department’s digital transformation agenda, identifying critical focus areas such as:

  • The development of a secure digital identity system;
  • Partnerships with the banking sector to expand service access;
  • Large-scale digitisation of records;
  • Visa reforms to support economic growth; and
  • The automation of service channels.

He also highlighted the planned introduction of an ETA system to modernise immigration processes, the rollout of e-passports, the enrolment of citizens into the smart ID card system while phasing out the green ID book, and the importance of digital literacy training and organisational change management to ensure that both staff and clients could effectively adapt to the new systems.

He referred to additional APP targets across various branches of the Department, including Information Services, the Director-General’s Office, Operations, Human Resource Management and Development, Civic Services, Immigration Services, and Finance and Supply Chain Management, indicating that these targets collectively supported the overall strategic objectives. He also referred to the five-year outcome indicators and targets contained in the 2025 to 2030 strategic plan, which were intended to measure progress and ensure accountability over time.

Budget overview

Mr Gordon Hollamby, Chief Financial Officer (CFO), DHA, presented a detailed overview of the Department’s budget. He said the Department’s allocation over the medium term amounted to R13.8 billion in 2026/27, R12.9 billion in 2027/28, and R13.3 billion in 2028/29, and that the baseline had increased by R1.3 billion in 2026/27, R774.9 million in 2027/28, and R643.2 million in 2028/29. He clarified that these changes were the result of baseline reallocations and reprioritisations, inflation-related adjustments, and additional allocations, including transfers to public entities.

He provided a detailed breakdown of these allocations, noting significant increases directed to the BMA to support the filling of vacancies and operational requirements following the transfer of functions from the Department. He also highlighted funding allocated for early retirement and voluntary exit programmes, as well as a substantial allocation of R492.4 million in 2026/27 for the Department’s digitalisation project under goods and services. He also mentioned additional allocations to the Political Representatives Fund over the medium term, and noted that the Independent Electoral Commission (IEC) had received an additional R1.1 billion for the 2026 local government elections, as announced in the Medium Term Budget Policy Statement (MTBPS).

Mr Hollamby emphasised that National Treasury had indicated that no additional funding would be made available over the medium-term expenditure framework period, and therefore the Department would have to manage all financial pressures within its existing baseline. He stressed that this required strict financial discipline, including reprioritisation of resources, cost-cutting measures, and careful management of expenditure to prevent overspending. He indicated that managers were required to ensure that personnel numbers remained within the compensation ceilings, and that all APP targets and operational requirements were implemented within the allocated budget, including absorbing inflationary increases and funding unfunded priorities through internal adjustments.

He outlined the high-level budget allocations across the Department’s four programmes -- Administration, Citizen Affairs, Immigration Affairs, and Institutional Support and Transfers -- and indicated that approval would be sought during the adjusted estimates of national expenditure (AENE) process for self-financing expenditure amounting to R2 billion. He also provided a detailed account of the transfer of funds to the BMA, specifying amounts transferred from Programme One (Administration) and Programme Three (Immigration Services) to cover information services, corporate services, accommodation costs at ports of entry, personnel expenses, travel costs, and the implementation of systems such as the Passenger Name Recognition system, all of which were associated with the shift of functions to the Authority.

Mr Makhode returned to focus on a detailed breakdown of the 2026/27 APP targets across the Department’s branches. These targets covered areas such as Information Services, the Director-General’s Office, Operations, Human Resource Management and Development, Civic Services, Immigration Services, and Finance and Supply Chain Management, and were carefully aligned with the Department’s strategic priorities and digital transformation agenda. He emphasised that achieving these targets would be critical for improving service delivery, enhancing operational efficiency, strengthening governance and accountability, and ensuring that the DHA fulfilled its mandate in contributing to national development objectives.

He concluded by formally recommending that the Portfolio Committee on Home Affairs support the Department’s APP for the 2026/27 financial year, reaffirming the Department’s commitment to implementing its plans within the available resources while advancing digital transformation, expanding access to services, and strengthening its role as a key enabler of socio-economic development in South Africa.

Discussion

Mr A Roos (DA) thanked the Ministry for what he described as a comprehensive presentation. He welcomed the significant increase in the number of identity documents produced over the past year, noting that the figure had increased from 2.8 million to 3.84 million. This meant that about 3 000 more people each day were receiving smart identity cards, which he believed were important for participation in the economy. This also reflected progress in reducing queues and system downtime. He welcomed the decision to split the smart identity card target, saying this showed a focus on ensuring that young people received their smart cards.

He then raised a question about the “Home Affairs on Wheels” project, which had visited 7 870 schools in the last financial year. He referred to a challenge he had previously highlighted, which had also become a Committee recommendation. He explained that when officials visited schools and found that a learner did not have a parent, or did not have the necessary documents to apply for an identity document, there needed to be a system to link that learner to a local Home Affairs office to assist with tracking and supporting the application. He referred to promised changes that were to have taken effect on 1 April, and asked if these changes had been implemented to improve the process of late registrations of birth. He stressed that these learners needed support because they were not at fault, and should not be left without assistance if they could not apply on the day of the school visit.

He also asked for an update on the rollout of services at bank branches, saying that the figures appeared promising and that he wanted to understand how the rollout was progressing and what the projections were for the rest of the year. However, he then expressed concern about service delivery at South African missions abroad. He commended Ms Veronica Loving, Assistant Director at the DHA, for her assistance in trying to resolve some of these issues, and acknowledged that the matter involved coordination between the DHA and the DIRCO. He referred to findings by the Auditor-General (AG) which had identified this as a risk, and had recommended that the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) between the two departments be reviewed. He asked for clarity on the progress made in this regard, as there were reports that some officials at foreign missions were not providing adequate service to South Africans.

He then asked Mr Hollamby what he considered to be the top two or three budget risks, apart from the BMA funding, that the Committee should be aware of so that it could pay attention to these matters. He further commended the internal immigration enforcement operations, saying that the number of operations had increased tenfold compared to previous years. He referred to international research showing that enforcement operations needed to be combined with closing access to employment opportunities for undocumented persons. He therefore asked if internal enforcement would be working together with the 10 000 labour inspectors announced during the SONA, and if additional resources would be required.

He concluded by congratulating the Ministry and executive leadership on the progress made and wished them well in implementing the APP.

Mr T Mogale (EFF) began by supporting the issue raised by Mr Roos on smart identity cards for learners aged 16 and above. He acknowledged that it was commendable that the Department had visited over 7 000 schools, but said this was still not sufficient because many young people were still struggling to obtain identity documents. He asked what additional systems the Department could introduce, beyond mobile trucks, to ensure that more young people were documented. He suggested that the Department could consider setting aside special days where schools could arrange to bring learners to DHA offices for assistance with documentation. He acknowledged that it was not practical for mobile trucks to visit every school in the country, but believed alternative arrangements could help. He further suggested that the focus should not only be on matric learners, but also on learners from Grade 10 or those aged 16 and above so that they could be documented earlier. He said that many of the challenges faced by these learners were linked to family circumstances, and that learners often sought assistance only when they were already in matric and urgently needed the document.

He then asked for clarity on the digitisation process, specifically what types of documents were being digitised. He referred to previous comments by the DG that foreign missions had large volumes of physical documents stored in offices, and asked if these were included in the digitisation process.

Finally, he referred to a meeting the Committee had held with the SIU and the Department of Public Works and Infrastructure (DPWI). He asked what immediate actions the Department had taken in response to the issues raised by the SIU, and what progress had been made in addressing concerns raised with the DPWI, particularly in KwaZulu-Natal, including the office in Pietermaritzburg.

Mr J Ngubane (MKP) said he wanted clarity on how the budget and the APP addressed crimes and syndicates within the Department of Home Affairs. He linked his concern to issues previously raised about the findings of the SIU, and said that in his view, these findings reflected only a small part of a much larger problem of corruption within the Department. He said there was a perception among members of the public that syndicates were still operating within Home Affairs, particularly around passports. There were reports of passports being renewed while people were still in the country, large numbers of passports being taken to the border to be stamped unlawfully, and then returned for use by individuals who were residing in South Africa unlawfully. He said the Committee wanted to hear how the Department’s plans, budget and focus would be directed at dealing with this ongoing corruption.

He raised serious concerns about customer service at Home Affairs offices. He said that many members of the public had lodged complaints that had remained unresolved for years, including simple matters such as correcting a misspelt surname. When people phoned the Department’s call centre numbers, they often received no assistance, and there was no proper system for tracking complaints. He described how people had lost confidence in raising issues with Home Affairs because of long delays and the lack of feedback. The Committee wanted to understand how the Department was improving the management of complaints and customer service, as many people were struggling with issues such as marriage records, identity documents for adults, and identity documents for matric learners.

Ms N Mtolo (ANC) asked whether the digital modernisation and transformation programme would be supported by physical infrastructure development, especially in rural and township areas, and wanted to know what infrastructure projects were planned. She referred to reports from provinces about vacancies, office space challenges and leasing problems, and asked if there were concrete plans and budget allocations in the current financial year to address these matters. She also raised the backlog in birth registrations, and asked what interventions were in place and when the Department expected to clear this backlog. Lastly, she asked what the practical implications were for service delivery, given that no additional funding would be provided over the MTEF period.

Ms M Modise-Mpya (ANC) emphasised the birth registration backlog. She noted that the Department appeared to be making good progress with smart identity card targets, but was not showing the same progress in addressing birth registration challenges. She suggested that the Department should apply similar effort and target-setting to birth registrations as it had done with smart identity cards, and asked for more detail on plans to deal with this problem.

She also raised concerns about funding for the BMA. She referred to the figures presented by the CFO, and said the amounts allocated did not appear to match the stated priorities of strengthening border security, infrastructure, technology and personnel. She expressed the view that the allocated amount was not sufficient, and asked for further explanation on how the Department intended to support the BMA in addressing its funding challenges. She also asked for clarity on the amount indicated for early retirements or voluntary exit programmes, wanting to know if this was a planned budget allocation or a projected estimate.

The Chairperson commented that many of the issues raised reflected previous Committee recommendations that were now appearing in the APP. He referred to the priorities outlined in the SONA, particularly the securing of the country’s borders. He said the Department needed to explain how this priority extended beyond the redevelopment of six ports of entry and included broader collaboration with the DPWI, especially on fencing and the use of technology along long stretches of the border. He pointed out that without proper fencing and technological support along the borders, the redevelopment of ports of entry alone would not prevent illegal entry into the country. He asked what the Department’s plans were, together with the DPWI, to address these infrastructure challenges, and referred to previous oversight visits to offices in Thohoyandou, the North West and Mokopane, where infrastructure delays had been observed.

He also said that there had been little indication that unused South African Post Office buildings were being considered for use by Home Affairs offices. He wanted clarity on how infrastructure planning was reflected in the APP and budget.

He reminded the Department of its commitment to prioritise the filling of critical vacancies in offices across the country, and asked if the budget made provision for this and for clear timelines. He also asked if the increased allocations to the BMA over the coming years would address concerns previously raised by the Committee on technology, staffing and infrastructure.

The Chairperson then referred to the issue of revenue collected at foreign missions, which had been raised by the Auditor-General. He said the Committee had requested clarity on how revenue collected through missions, often in collaboration with DIRCO, contributed to the Department’s revenue base. He also referred to the audit action plan, and asked if there was a record of recoveries or sanctions against officials linked to material irregularities identified by the Auditor-General.

Lastly, he referred to the preparations for the upcoming local government elections. He asked how the Department was collaborating with the Independent Electoral Commission, particularly in facilitating access to identity documents. He referred to challenges observed during oversight visits, where identity documents produced through mobile units were collected at Home Affairs offices rather than being returned to the schools where applications were made. He asked if there were plans to improve integration so that identity documents could be distributed more efficiently at the points where services had originally been provided.

DHA’s response

Mr Makhode began by responding to concerns about offices that were in poor condition or had been delayed for long periods. He reminded the Committee of the oversight visit to Atlantis that had taken place after the presentation by the DPWI. Following that visit, Minister Schreiber had written to Minister Macpherson to request permission, in line with section 18 of the Government Immovable Asset Management Act (GIAMA), for the DHA to undertake the work on certain buildings itself. This permission had now been granted, and the Department was proceeding with the work at the Atlantis office.

He reported that similar approval had been granted for the long-delayed office in Thohoyandou in Limpopo, which had remained incomplete for a long time, and approval had also been granted for work to proceed at the office in KwaDukuza, previously known as Stanger. The Department had submitted a list of about 15 offices for such approval and that some, including Howick and Ulundi in KwaZulu-Natal, were still under consideration. Once approval was received, the Department would appoint its own service providers to complete the work. He expressed appreciation for the support received from the DPWI in this regard, and said this progress followed directly from the Committee’s oversight and the Minister’s intervention.

He then addressed collaboration for the upcoming local government elections. He explained that Minister Schreiber was part of an Inter-Ministerial Committee on local government elections, together with the Minister of Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs (COGTA), Minister Velenkosini Hlabisa. At a technical level, he was working together with the DG of COGTA and the IEC, and that planning for the elections had started the previous year. The Department would align its work with that of the IEC, including extending operating hours over a designated weekend to allow South Africans to apply for the enabling documents required for voter registration.

On illegal migration and border security, he reminded the Committee that the Minister was also part of an inter-ministerial committee dealing with illegal migration. A draft Cabinet memorandum was being prepared which dealt with infrastructure at ports of entry, and broader collaboration between law enforcement authorities. This work also involved South African National Parks (SANParks) because of areas such as the Kruger National Park that were used for illegal crossings, as well as collaboration with provincial Departments of Roads and Transport in Limpopo to close certain roads identified by traditional leaders and farmers as contributing to illegal activities. He said this was a coordinated effort across departments, and that details would be shared once the Cabinet process had been completed.

He returned to the matter of utilising South African Post Office (SAPO) buildings for Home Affairs offices. He said the Department had approached SAPO as early as 2022, but had not received a favourable response. In East London, the Department would have preferred to use a nearby post office building instead of relocating to Hemingways, but this had not been possible. He added that the facilities in Silverton and Church Street, which the Committee had seen, were former SAPO buildings that the Department had leased and refurbished at its own cost. He noted that the Post Office’s business rescue situation made such arrangements difficult.

On identity document collections and the work with banks, he reported that 67 000 applications had been launched through the bank expansion programme, of which 35 375 identity documents had already been collected. This was encouraging progress following the recent launch of the programme at Orange Farm.

Dr Masiapato returned to focus specifically on irregular expenditure and explain it in the context of how the BMA had been established and how its operational environment had changed over time.

He said a key part of the issue arose from the way government property and infrastructure had historically been managed. Under Section 38 of the BMA mandate, land and infrastructure used at ports of entry were supposed to be transferred to the BMA so that it could function as the landlord at land border posts. This was still in process, and once completed, the BMA would fully own and manage land port infrastructure.

He contrasted this with other types of ports of entry. He said that airports were owned by the ACSA, and in those cases the BMA operated as a tenant. At sea ports, he said the TNPA acted as the landlord, while the BMA again operated as a tenant, using space that belonged either to state entities or private owners, depending on the location.

He said the irregular expenditure issue had arisen mainly during the transition of staff and services into the BMA. Before the BMA was fully operational, various government departments such as Health and Agriculture had staff working in buildings under separate rental agreements. These agreements were made with different landlords, including private property owners and entities outside TNPA-controlled spaces, particularly in places like Durban. He explained that when the BMA was created and had absorbed officials from these departments -- including immigration, agriculture, and health officials now working under the BMA -- the occupation of those buildings had continued under existing contracts that were not originally structured for the BMA as the new employer. As a result, invoices from landlords began to be directed to the BMA even though the original agreements had been made under different departmental arrangements. This transition had created accounting and contractual complications that were recorded as irregular expenditure in financial terms.

Dr Masiapato stressed that this situation had not been caused by misconduct by any BMA officials, but rather by the complexity of merging multiple departmental systems into a single authority while existing lease agreements were still in place. The BMA was now addressing the problem by consolidating staff from different departments into single, properly contracted office spaces. This would allow the BMA to enter into clear and unified lease agreements directly with property owners, instead of managing fragmented arrangements inherited from different departments. Once this consolidation process was completed, irregular expenditure linked to these transitional leases would be resolved. He added that the BMA was engaging with National Treasury to ensure that the historical expenditure linked to this transition was properly regularised and formally addressed.

Discussion

Mr Roos welcomed the presentation and also expressed appreciation for Dr Masiapato, describing him as professional, highly available and effective in communication. This had reflected positively on the BMA and had helped to strengthen confidence in its work. He also acknowledged the Minister for taking border security seriously, and noted that further progress had been reported in tackling corruption at border posts. He referred to the dismissal of seven more corrupt border officials, which brought the total number of dismissals to 63 since the formation of the Government of National Unity (GNU), which was a sign that enforcement was improving.

Mr Roos also observed that despite limited funding, there had been a decline in illegal border crossings while recorded traveller volumes had increased significantly. He referred to the Easter period, where there had been a 21% increase in travellers processed at the Lebombo border post, with similar increases reported at other ports. He interpreted this as a possible indication that more travellers were now using formal systems such as visas and border processing channels rather than crossing illegally, or that previously unrecorded movement was now being captured more effectively. He said this trend appeared positive overall.

However, he then raised a major concern on funding and institutional implementation. He reminded the Committee that the BMA had been formally established as a Schedule 3A public entity on 1 April 2023, and that key functions such as staff, budgets and assets from agencies like the South African Police Service (SAPS) were supposed to have been transferred by that stage. He said that three years later, there still appeared to be delays and uncertainty in this transfer process.

He said a recurring issue in the presentation was that staffing costs remained too high as a percentage of the budget, and that the BMA still lacked sufficient assets and funding that were supposed to have been transferred from other departments. He expressed concern that there did not seem to be clear progress in transferring border functions from SAPS to the BMA, and said this had not been discussed meaningfully in recent months.

Mr Roos questioned if the transfer of border functions from SAPS to the BMA was legally required or optional. He said there appeared to be uncertainty, as different interpretations had been suggested in previous discussions, including comments made in other political engagements. He asked if SAPS was legally obliged to transfer these functions, or if it was something that could be decided voluntarily. He said that without clarity on this legal question, it was difficult for the Committee to make informed decisions on how to proceed. He emphasised that if the transfer was legally required, then it should already have taken place after three years, and the lack of progress needed urgent attention. The Committee needed clear answers on whether the transfer would happen, would not happen, or how and when it would be completed. He stressed that the uncertainty was making it difficult for the BMA leadership, including Dr Masiapato, to fully succeed despite their efforts, and the Committee needed to support them by resolving this long-standing issue.

Mr Mogale first asked about lifestyle audits, referring to a previous Committee request that the BMA conduct them to strengthen accountability and prevent corruption. He recalled that funding constraints had previously been cited as a reason for delays, and asked if the process had now started or what alternative measures were being implemented.

He also asked about steps being taken to deal with corruption at ports of entry, acknowledging recent arrests of officials, but describing them as insufficient compared to the scale of the problem. He wanted clarity on what systems or controls had been introduced to prevent corruption beyond enforcement actions.

Mr Mogale then asked about the current staffing capacity of the BMA and how fully the organisation was resourced. He also requested information on MOUs between the BMA and key state institutions such as the SARS, SAPS and the South African National Defence Force (SANDF), as he wanted to understand how coordination was structured across enforcement agencies.

He also referred to funding concerns, stating that the BMA had requested approximately R3.4 billion from the Criminal Assets Recovery Account, but had received only about R50 million. He asked what impact this shortfall would have on planned investments in technology such as scanners and drones, which were intended to strengthen border control.

He also raised concerns about the complexity of the BMA’s reporting structure, noting that it reported to multiple departments, and asked if this created conflicting priorities or operational challenges.

Lastly, he asked about branding and signage at ports of entry, recalling that earlier discussions had included plans to improve the visibility and identity of the BMA. He asked if this process had also included consideration of renaming certain ports of entry as part of the broader branding exercise.

Mr Ngubane thanked the Commissioner and his team for their efforts, and acknowledged the work being done to improve border safety and security. However, he raised several concerns about the credibility and measurement of the BMA’s reported performance outcomes. He referred to indicators in the presentation that showed 100% achievement in areas such as illegal crossings intercepted, illegal goods confiscated, deportations carried out, and refusals of entry. He questioned how the BMA defined and verified this 100% achievement, and asked what independent evidence supported these claims.

He said his concern was based on the difference between detected and undetected activity at the borders. While the BMA may report success in what it had intercepted, there was still a large unknown number of illegal crossings and activities that were not being detected. He raised concern that this created a gap in understanding the real security situation, especially when communities reported serious crimes linked to individuals who entered or moved across borders undetected. He gave examples of criminals who were not traceable, and said this undermined public confidence when official figures suggested complete success. He said there needed to be a system that measured both known and unknown risks, possibly through improved surveillance or technology coverage across the border. He expressed concern that from the public’s perspective, the current reporting made it appear as though only a small number of illegal activities existed, while many others remained unseen. This created distrust and frustration among citizens.

Mr Ngubane also raised concerns about whether the BMA’s performance indicators reflected real impact or only procedural compliance. He questioned whether the reported achievements demonstrated actual reductions in illegal activity, or if they simply showed that administrative processes had been followed. It was important for the Committee to see evidence that border security was genuinely improving, rather than only meeting procedural targets.

He then turned to cybersecurity and said that cybercrime had been identified as a key risk in the presentation. He asked what level of cybersecurity maturity the BMA currently had, and on what benchmarks this assessment had been based. He requested clarity on how this maturity level was independently tested and if the BMA had experienced any significant cyber incidents that could be used to evaluate its readiness.

He also raised concerns about funding, particularly reliance on non-baseline sources such as the CARA and public-private partnerships. He asked what would happen to critical operations if such funding did not materialise or was delayed, and if any essential functions were currently dependent on uncertain funding sources.

He also questioned the prioritisation of ports of entry in relation to the EMCS 2.0 system, noting that only 19 ports had been prioritised for upgrades in the 2026/27 period. He asked what criteria had been used to select these ports, and what risks remained at other ports that were not included in the rollout plan.

He also raised concerns about governance, ethics and corruption. He referred to a reported increase in ethics compliance targets from 30 to 80, and asked what this measure represented, who assessed it, and how its rapid improvement could be considered realistic within a medium-term framework. He said corruption remained a known systemic risk at the borders, and asked how many officials had been disciplined, charged or dismissed for corruption, and what trend this showed. He also asked how whistle-blower protection was being practically implemented within the BMA.

Finally, he raised concerns about business continuity, and asked if the BMA regularly tested its readiness for major disruptions such as ICT system failures, border closures or pandemic-related emergencies. He asked when such tests had last been conducted and if they were practical simulations rather than theoretical plans, given the critical importance of border security.

The Chairperson reiterated that the BMA needed to clearly explain how it intended to use allocated funding, especially in addressing underfunding concerns and strengthening border infrastructure. He also raised again the issue of fencing and technological gaps along land borders, noting that despite upgrades at ports of entry, border lines remained vulnerable. He asked what engagements the BMA was having with other institutions, and if collaborations with external partners were sustainable and properly secured, including if security clearance requirements had been properly addressed in those partnerships.

BMA’s response

Dr Masiapato first responded to Mr Roos, acknowledging the appreciation expressed for the work of the BMA and confirming that there had been observable improvements in border management operations. He said that there had been a decrease in illegal border crossings and a rise in properly processed travellers, but stressed that a more detailed and evidence-based analysis would be provided when the BMA returned to report specifically on the Easter operational period. The upcoming report would include full statistical interpretation of trends and operational outcomes.

SAPS/BMA integration issue

Dr Masiapato then addressed what he described as the most important issue raised by Mr Roos, which was the long-standing question of SAPS border function integration into the BMA. He stated very clearly that at a technical level, discussions between the BMA Commissioner and the SAPS Commissioner had failed to resolve the matter. Because of this breakdown at the technical level, the issue had been escalated to the political level involving ministers. He emphasised that this was not a minor administrative matter but a serious structural and institutional challenge that required political resolution.

He clarified the legal position in detail, stating that the Border Management Authority Act, particularly Section 4(2), already provided that border law enforcement functions were legally assigned to the BMA from 1 April 2023. He stressed that this law had been passed by Parliament and signed into effect, meaning that the establishment of the BMA and its mandate were not in dispute. He explained that the confusion being raised was not about legality, but about institutional implementation and resistance to integration.

He explained that historically, different departments such as Home Affairs, Agriculture, Health and Environmental Affairs, had officials stationed at ports of entry. When the BMA was created, those officials had been transferred into the BMA system to create a single coordinated border management structure. The same principle was intended to apply to SAPS border police officers, who had previously performed border law enforcement duties under SAPS structures.

He pointed out that SAPS border police officers could not simply be dismissed or removed, but had to be integrated into the BMA because their function now formed part of the BMA’s legal mandate. He stressed that this was not a question of whether SAPS would cease to exist, but rather how border-related functions were reorganised across institutions. He clarified that SAPS would still retain its investigative and detective responsibilities, and that the BMA would handle border law enforcement only at ports of entry. He gave examples, explaining that if a crime such as murder or vehicle theft occurred at a port, SAPS detectives would still be called to investigate, while the BMA would handle the border control aspect.

He said the Office of the Chief State Law Adviser had already been involved in the matter, and had confirmed that there was no legal barrier to the integration. Legal experts had indicated that the issue was not legal but related to implementation and institutional agreement. He said the legal framework was clear and had been validated at the highest advisory level of government.

Dr Masiapato then shifted to the matter of lifestyle audits raised by Mr Mogale. He said the BMA had intended to conduct lifestyle audits for its senior leadership, including executive committee members, management and port commanders, as part of strengthening integrity systems. However, when the matter was referred to the SIU, it had indicated that it would cost approximately R9 million to conduct the audits. The BMA did not have the financial capacity to pay this amount due to its already constrained budget. Because of this funding challenge, lifestyle audits had not been implemented. Instead, the BMA had relied on mandatory vetting processes as provided for in Section 13(3) of the BMA Act, which was conducted by the State Security Agency (SSA) and did not require additional funding from the BMA. He clarified that officials dealing with sensitive or classified work were subjected to background checks, interviews, and in some cases polygraph tests conducted through the national intelligence system. He emphasised that while lifestyle audits had not taken place, vetting processes were fully operational and ongoing.

On corruption prevention, Dr Masiapato said the BMA had established a Border Management and Immigration Anti-Corruption Forum. This forum included the SIU, the Hawks, the National Prosecuting Authority’s (NPA’s) Investigating Directorate, and internal anti-corruption units. The forum worked proactively to identify risks and prevent corruption, rather than reacting only after incidents occurred.

He also described awareness programmes conducted at ports of entry, where enforcement agencies and even Correctional Services participated. Former officials who had been convicted of corruption were sometimes brought in to speak to staff about the consequences of corrupt behaviour, including dismissal and imprisonment. These interventions were designed to build ethical awareness and reduce corruption risks.

On staffing capacity, Dr Masiapato said the BMA was operating at only about 25% capacity. The organisation currently had just under 2 600 officials, while the approved structure required about 11 200, which meant the BMA had a shortage of approximately 8 500 posts. Most of the shortage was in operational “boots on the ground” positions, such as border guards and coastal guards, while a smaller portion related to specialist roles in immigration, agriculture, environmental and health biosecurity. He said the original funding request to establish the BMA had been around R2.9 billion, but the organisation had received significantly less at inception, which had created a structural funding gap that still existed. He added that if SAPS border officers had been integrated, capacity would have been significantly higher -- possibly around 60% to 65%.

He confirmed that implementation protocols had been signed with SAPS, SANDF and SARS as required by the BMA Act. These were not informal agreements but formal legal implementation protocols that governed how institutions cooperated at the border level. These protocols had been signed in 2023, including agreements with SAPS Commissioner Masemola and other heads of departments, and were reviewed regularly through steering committees.

Dr Masiapato said the BMA had applied for R3.2 billion in funding from the CARA, but had received only R50 million. He compared this with previous years, explaining that similar applications had also been reduced significantly by National Treasury. The limited funding meant that the BMA could not proceed with major procurements such as scanners and drones. Instead, the available funds would be used only for basic operational requirements such as firearms, ammunition and essential tools of trade.

He said that CARA funding and public-private partnerships were not used for core operations, but for specific projects, including port redevelopment and equipment support. He stressed that operational sustainability still depended mainly on baseline funding.

On governance and reporting structures, he clarified that the BMA did not report to five departments as sometimes assumed, but up to ten ministers as required by law. This multi-ministerial reporting system was structured and legal, and involved quarterly reporting and technical coordination. He said there was no operational confusion, because each department engaged at the technical or ministerial level, depending on the issue. He gave an example involving agricultural disease control, explaining how the BMA worked with the Department of Agriculture to track possible cross-border transmission of diseases such as foot and mouth disease (FMD). This demonstrated how interdepartmental coordination functioned in practice.

On border fencing and infrastructure, he said there was ongoing discussion about improving fencing, and that ministers were expected to visit northern border areas with the military to assess conditions. He indicated that additional funding might be secured for fencing improvements.

Branding at ports of entry was also underway, with some ports already displaying BMA signage, but that a full rollout had not yet been completed. He said some of the major commercial ports were excluded from branding for now because they were scheduled for redevelopment.

On performance targets, Dr Masiapato responded to Mr Ngubane’s concerns about the reported “100% achievement” figures. He acknowledged that this raised public concern, and explained that international border management practice often used such indicators, but admitted that measuring undetected illegal activity remained a serious methodological challenge. He said that while interceptions and deportations could be measured, it was much harder to measure activity that was not detected.

He gave a more detailed explanation, stating that illegal migration included both detected and undetected movement, and also included cases where individuals entered legally but overstayed. He said this complexity made absolute measurement difficult. He added that broader national intelligence coordination structures had been tasked with studying the real impact of border control on crime inside the country.

On cyber security, he confirmed that the BMA had not experienced any cyber security breaches since its establishment in April 2023, although he warned that vulnerabilities still existed and required stronger investment.

On business continuity, he explained that plans existed but had not yet been fully tested through large-scale simulation exercises due to inherited infrastructure weaknesses, including flood-prone ports.

He concluded by explaining that the R50 million CARA allocation was far too limited to support large technology investments, and would instead be used for basic operational needs. He said major technology upgrades such as scanners required significantly higher funding.

Ms Chonco then briefly added that National Treasury had clearly instructed that the R315 million allocation could be used only for critical posts and operational costs. She confirmed that it could not be used for infrastructure or technology investment, and that those requirements would need separate funding of about R500 million.

The Chairperson concluded by thanking the Commissioner and CFO, and indicated that further engagement would be required on funding priorities, SAPS integration, and unresolved audit-related concerns.

Minister’s concluding comments

Minister Schreiber thanked the Committee Members for their contributions, and said he would add a few additional points to the discussion.

He responded to a concern raised by Mr Mogale about possible changes to the names of ports of entry, and said such changes could not realistically be considered while the Department was still facing serious financial constraints. The Department first had to stabilise its finances before it could take on additional or non-essential projects of that nature.

He then focused on what he considered a more important part of the financial discussion, which had come up repeatedly during the meeting -- the need for a more stable and predictable funding model. He said the Department was exploring the rollout of the ETA system as a possible solution in this regard. The intention was not to treat the ETA as a temporary or irregular source of income, but rather to develop it into a reliable and sustainable revenue stream over time. He elaborated that the idea behind this approach was that foreign nationals who wished to visit South Africa would pay a reasonable fee when applying for a tourist visa through the improved digital system. This would be linked to a significantly improved service experience compared to the previous system. The revenue collected through this process could then be reinvested into strengthening border security, improving infrastructure, and supporting related state functions. He emphasised that this approach would be fair because it would not place the burden on South African taxpayers, but rather on users of the service -- specifically foreign travellers benefiting from entry into the country.

He also stressed that the fee structure would have to remain reasonable and not become a barrier to travel, but he believed that if properly designed it could become an important part of solving the long-term funding challenges facing both the Department and the BMA. He indicated that this was still a work in progress and that the Department could not implement it unilaterally, as it required a number of approvals and processes. However, there was clear potential in the idea, and the Department would report back to the Committee once the work had been finalised.

Minister Schreiber also responded more broadly to earlier questions about funding and institutional capacity, particularly those raised by Mr Roos. He said the Commissioner had already explained in detail the extent of the efforts being made to address funding pressures and operational constraints. He acknowledged that both the DHA and the BMA were actively working on multiple solutions, but he emphasised that cooperation across government structures was necessary to resolve these challenges effectively.

He added that while Treasury funding was appreciated and remained an important part of the budget, it was not sufficient on its own to address all needs. For that reason, the Department was now exploring additional and more innovative approaches to ensure long-term sustainability. He concluded by saying that this work would continue, with the aim of placing the BMA and border management functions on a stronger financial footing going forward.

The Chairperson thanked the Minister, the Deputy Minister, the Director-General, the Commissioner, and all senior officials and acting officials who had participated in the presentations. He said the Committee appreciated the detailed engagement and the extensive responses provided by both the BMA and the DHA. Both institutions had clearly outlined their challenges, particularly those related to funding and resources, and had also explained how they were trying to prioritise programmes within the limits of the resources that had been allocated.

Legal issues arising from the Marriage Bill [B43-2023]

Ms Zingisa Zenani, Parliamentary Legal Advisor, delivered the presentation by the Constitutional and Legal Services Office (CLSO) on the legal issues arising from the Marriage Bill [B43-2023] that had been introduced within the broader constitutional framework of South Africa.

She said South Africa was founded on constitutional values which included human dignity, the achievement of equality, and the advancement of human rights and freedoms. The Bill of Rights guaranteed equality, dignity, and freedom of religion, belief and opinion, and that although these rights could be limited, any limitation had to be reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom. The Marriage Bill sought to affirm these rights specifically within the context of marriages, and one of its primary objectives was to recognise all marriages, including those that had previously not been recognised under any law. She said the CLSO had considered the oral submissions received by the Committee, the public participation report, and the Department’s responses to those submissions, and that its legal opinion aimed to assist the Committee by highlighting the legal and constitutional issues raised by the Bill.

Ms Zenani provided background to the Bill by referring to the judgment of the Constitutional Court in the case of the Women’s Legal Centre Trust (WLCT) v President of the Republic of South Africa and Others. She explained that the Court had declared the Marriage Act, 1961, and the Divorce Act, 1979, unconstitutional because they were under-inclusive and resulted in various rights violations. The Court had found that the failure to recognise Muslim marriages solemnised in accordance with Sharia law, and which had not been registered as civil marriages, infringed the rights to equality, dignity, children’s rights and access to courts.

The Court had suspended the declaration of invalidity for 24 months to allow Parliament to remedy the defects, and had ordered that legislation be amended or enacted to recognise Muslim marriages as valid for all purposes. This period of suspension had later been extended to 27 June 2026. The Bill had originally been introduced by the Minister of Home Affairs in December 2023, but the sixth Parliament had been unable to process it due to time constraints before its term ended in May 2024. The Bill had then been revived by the seventh Parliament on 25 June 2024 and was being processed in terms of Rule 286 of the National Assembly Rules.

On the legal and constitutional issues, Ms Zenani addressed concerns on Clause 1 of the Bill, particularly the absence of a definition of a Muslim marriage, which some submissions had argued created a lack of synergy with the Divorce Amendment Act. She explained that the Constitutional Court in the WLCT matter had declared the common law definition of marriage unconstitutional to the extent that it excluded Muslim marriages. Clause 3(d) and (e) of the Bill provided for the recognition of subsisting monogamous and polygamous marriages that were previously not recognised, as well as those entered into after commencement of the Act that complied with its requirements. She expressed the view that Clause 1, read together with Clause 3, adequately covered all forms of marriage, including religious marriages such as those concluded according to Islamic tenets.

She then addressed the proposal from submissions that the minimum marriageable age be increased beyond 18 years. She stated that the CLSO aligned itself with the view that 18 years was constitutionally appropriate and consistent with section 28 of the Constitution, section 17 of the Children’s Act, and international instruments such as the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child and the Convention on the Elimination of all Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW).

On Clauses 5(1)(c) and 6(1)(c), which required prospective spouses to be of sound mind, Ms Zenani said that concerns had been raised that marriage officers were not medically trained to determine mental capacity and might be unfairly penalised. The Department had clarified that this requirement related to the mental capacity to understand and consent to the marriage, and that the test was objective and fact-specific.

On Clause 5(2), she addressed concerns that specific BaPedi customs such as ngwetši ya lapa (daughter-in-law of the family) should be expressly recognised. She clarified that the clause did not intend to recognise particular customs, but dealt with requirements for marriage, while Clause 3 set out the types of marriages recognised. She further indicated that concerns about verifying apostilled letters of non-impediment for foreign nationals under Clause 5(4) were operational matters for the Department to manage.

Ms Zenani devoted significant attention to Clause 6(2)(a), which required consent for polygamous marriages. She referred to the Constitutional Court’s judgment in Mayelane v Ngwenyama, where the Court had held that the consent of the first wife was necessary to protect her equality rights. She also referred to a recent confirmation of this principle by the Mpumalanga High Court in N.R.M v F.N and Others, where a subsequent marriage had been declared invalid due to a lack of consent. She stated that consent remained integral to the constitutionality of polygamous marriages.

She then addressed the “controversial” proviso in Clause 6(2)(a), which stated that in the case of royal families, consent must be obtained in accordance with the customs and traditions of that family. She reminded the Committee that the Bill had not been certified as constitutionally sound by the Office of the Chief State Law Adviser (OCSLA) due to this proviso. The CLSO had previously shared OCSLA’s view that this created unfair discrimination against women married into royal families, as it subjected them to a different consent regime. She referred to sections 9, 15, 36 and 211 of the Constitution, and emphasised that recognition of customary law had to remain consistent with constitutional rights. She cited the reasoning in Mayelane, King N.O. v De Jager, and Bhe and Others v Magistrate, Khayelitsha, to show that customary law must develop in line with the Constitution. In the CLSO’s view, the proviso imposed an unjustifiable limitation on the equality and dignity rights of women, and therefore rendered the Bill constitutionally vulnerable.

Ms Zenani then addressed Clause 6(3), which referred specifically to a “husband” and excluded same-sex partners from the provision. She noted the Department’s view that broadening the clause might introduce unintended consequences such as polyandry, which the Bill did not provide for. The CLSO’s view was that the clause must be understood within the context of polygamy, as defined in the Bill.

She also discussed Clause 11(4), where ex officio marriage officers were excluded from the opt-out clause. The CLSO agreed with the Department that allowing ex officio officers to opt out would result in discrimination, particularly against same-sex couples.

On Clause 11(5)(c), relating to prohibited relationships, the CLSO aligned with the Department that this prohibition was consistent with other legislation such as the Criminal Procedure Act.

On Clause 12 on objections to marriages, Ms Zenani stated that determining what constituted an authentic objection was an operational matter best addressed by the Department, but that objections should contain sufficient detail to allow an objective assessment of whether they were frivolous or valid.

On Clause 15 on proprietary consequences, she addressed suggestions that the default marital regime be changed to accrual, to prevent crimes associated with community of property. She referred to the Constitutional Court decisions in Ramuhovhi v President of the RSA and Sithole v Sithole, which confirmed that the default marital regime remained community of property.

In conclusion, Ms Zenani said that the Marriage Bill was firmly rooted in constitutional values of dignity, equality and human rights, and sought to remedy the constitutional defects identified in the WLCT judgment. She emphasised that section 7 of the Constitution affirmed the Bill of Rights as the cornerstone of democracy, and that the State was obliged to protect, promote and fulfil these rights. She indicated that the Bill aimed to balance these rights in a manner that would pass constitutional scrutiny, although the CLSO had identified the proviso in Clause 6 as a significant constitutional concern.

Discussion and responses

The Chairperson thanked the officials for the presentation and response on the Marriage Bill public hearing consultations. He then asked the Chief State Law Advisor if there were any further matters to raise after the discussions that had taken place between the legal teams of the Department and Parliamentary Legal Services.

Adv Suraya Williams, Principal State Law Advisor, OCSLA, responded that the Office had considered the matter and had already met with the Department of Home Affairs and Parliamentary Legal Services. After those engagements, there had been agreement between the Chief State Law Advisor’s office and Parliamentary Legal Services on the issues that had been discussed. They therefore had nothing further to add at that stage.

The Chairperson then asked if the DHA had anything to add.

Adv Moses Malakate, Director: Legislative Drafting, DHA, responded that the Department had taken note of the presentation from Parliamentary Legal Services, and agreed with much of what had been said. However, he raised a specific concern on Clause 6, Subclause 2(a), and said that the Department still believed it might be necessary to return to the Portfolio Committee to further explain the rationale for including a particular proviso in that clause. The Department’s view was that written consent should not be interpreted too narrowly, and that a broader understanding was needed. He said this was important because of the potential impact on customary practices within royal families, particularly in relation to the nomination and appointment of a successor within traditional leadership structures. He warned that requiring written consent in a strict way could have unintended consequences for those cultural processes. He indicated that the Department would be guided by the Committee on whether it would be allowed to make a further presentation on this issue.

The Chairperson asked if there had been consultation with COGTA and Traditional Affairs, as previously directed by the Committee.

Adv Malakate confirmed that coordination and liaison had taken place with both COGTA and Traditional Affairs. However, those consultations had focused mainly on the regulation of customary and cultural practices in general, and that they might still need a more specific response from Traditional Affairs on the proviso in question, as the letters received had not directly addressed it.

The Chairperson then sought clarity on whether there were still differences between the legal teams on the certification of the Bill. He asked if the department and the legal advisers were still not aligned.

Ms Zenani responded that she was not entirely certain where the process stood on final agreement. She said that although engagements had taken place between the legal teams, it had become clear that Parliamentary Legal Services and the Office of the Chief State Law Advisor still agreed with each other, but there had not yet been a clear understanding of the Department’s position on the specific proviso. She said the basis for the Department’s disagreement had not yet been clearly established, although she noted that this might become clearer if the Department was given an opportunity to present its full rationale for the proviso.

The Chairperson then summarised the situation, stating that it was clear from the discussions that although significant legal work had been done by Home Affairs, Parliamentary Legal Services, and the Chief State Law Advisor, differences still remained on the certification of the Marriage Bill. It appeared that the Department had requested more time to reflect and to continue consultations, particularly with COGTA and Traditional Affairs, before returning to the Committee with a clearer position.

He indicated that once those consultations had taken place, the Committee would need a consolidated presentation, including input from both COGTA and Traditional Affairs, so that all views would be formally placed on record and could be considered together. This would help ensure that the Committee reached a clear and informed position before any decision was taken, or before the matter was forwarded further in the legislative process. It was also important for the Committee to understand the reasoning behind the different legal interpretations before making a final decision.

Mr Roos supported the proposed way forward, but suggested that it would also be helpful if the Department and legal teams could not only explain whether the clause was constitutional or not, but also to provide possible options as to how it could be amended if it was found to be problematic. This would assist the Committee in making a more practical decision.

The Chairperson agreed with this suggestion, and said it should be included in the Committee’s approach going forward. He also clarified that COGTA and Traditional Affairs had not been formally invited to the current meeting, but would need to be included in the next engagement on the matter. He proposed that at the next session, stakeholders who had been consulted should also be present to present their views directly to the Committee. He also proposed that the Director-Generals of both COGTA and Traditional Affairs should certify the consultation process to ensure that there was formal accountability and clarity on the inputs received. This would prevent a situation where the executive departments were not fully represented in the process while decisions were being made based on their areas of responsibility.

Adv Malakate and Ms Zenani both indicated their agreement with this approach, stating that they would continue engaging with the relevant departments on the outstanding issues.

The Chairperson then concluded that Members of the Committee would also need to do their own reflection on the issues raised so that they could engage meaningfully once all reports and consultations had been completed. This would help the Committee to reach a clear and informed position when the matter returned for further discussion.

Committee matters

The Committee then moved to internal business, where it confirmed its oversight programme for the following week.

The meeting was adjourned.
