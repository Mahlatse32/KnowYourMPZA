SACE & Umalusi Annual Performance Plan 2026/27; with Minister and Deputy Minister

Basic Education

21 April 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Annual Performance Plans (APPs) of Government Departments and Entities

The Portfolio Committee on Basic Education met to receive briefings from the South African Council for Educators (SACE) and Umalusi on their 2026/27 Strategic and Annual Performance Plans. The meeting was attended by the Department of Basic Education, including the Minister and Deputy Minister.

The Minister emphasised that SACE and Umalusi occupied critical positions within the education sector and that their Annual Performance Plans should be treated as instruments of delivery, accountability and public confidence, rather than mere compliance exercises. The Minister highlighted the need to strengthen educator professionalism and ethics, improve teacher development, accelerate the handling of misconduct matters, and maintain the integrity and credibility of examinations and qualifications.

Umalusi outlined its strategic priorities, including the review of qualifications within the General and Further Education and Training Qualifications Sub-Framework, improving moderator training to minimise errors in question papers, strengthening quality assurance and marking verification processes, and contributing research towards mother tongue-based bilingual education. Umalusi reported that its 2026/27 budget amounted to R238 million, reflecting a nine percent increase from the previous financial year, with approximately 76% of the budget transferred from the Department of Basic Education. The institution also highlighted targets relating to ICT network performance, invoice payments within 30 days, and maintaining vacancy rates below eight percent. However, Umalusi raised concerns regarding human resource constraints, inadequate financial resources, increasing operational costs, limited monitoring capacity of examination centres, and challenges associated with regulating unregistered online schools. Umalusi further indicated that the rollout of Recognition of Prior Learning initiatives had been delayed due to budget constraints.

SACE highlighted its focus on strengthening the professionalisation of the teaching profession through awareness campaigns, educator development programmes, improved registration systems, and strengthened ethical standards. SACE projected total revenue of approximately R140.9 million for the 2026/27 financial year and highlighted plans to support educator professional development, improve certification turnaround times, and strengthen ethics and case management systems. The institution reported progress in integrating its systems with other government entities, improving cybersecurity measures, and addressing educator misconduct backlogs. SACE further indicated that it had investigated more than 1 200 backlog misconduct cases and was targeting the finalisation of 300 misconduct cases during the current reporting cycle. However, SACE acknowledged serious operational challenges, including increasing misconduct case backlogs, limited funding, fragmented systems, slow disciplinary processes, weak inter-institutional coordination, and ongoing concerns regarding abuse, harassment and violence within schools. SACE also indicated that budget limitations continued to affect core and mandatory functions of the institution.

Members raised serious concerns regarding the growing backlog of educator misconduct cases and delays in disciplinary processes within SACE. Concerns were also raised regarding weak enforcement of ethical standards, gender-based violence and sexual harassment in schools, cultural and religious discrimination, poor awareness of gender sensitivity issues, and the need for stronger training and professional development for educators and school management. Members further questioned SACE on weaknesses in its ICT systems, cybersecurity protections, educator registration processes, funding model, and ability to adequately monitor educators across the country.

Members also raised concerns regarding Umalusi’s limited monitoring of examination centres, delays in resolving examination irregularity investigations, and the withholding of learners’ National Senior Certificate results. Questions were raised regarding the regulation of online schools, Umalusi’s reliance on DBE transfers, travel and accommodation expenditure, staff retention, and the sustainability of its funding model. Members further emphasised the need for fair, transparent and efficient investigations into examination irregularities to avoid disadvantaging learners.

The Committee requested that the Department provide a comprehensive report on learners whose matric results had been withheld due to alleged examination irregularities and provide updates on the status of those investigations. The Committee also requested reports on specific incidents raised during the meeting involving educator misconduct, assault and abuse at schools. Further engagements were proposed on Recognition of Prior Learning and the implementation of the Three Stream Model certification process.

The Chairperson emphasised the importance of accountability, effective implementation, and ensuring that both institutions adequately fulfil their mandates in protecting learners, educators and the integrity of the education system.

Department of Basic Education

Opening Remarks by the Minister

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, welcomed the opportunity to come before the Committee and engage on the budget review and strategic plans of the two institutions. She said that both institutions occupied distinct but very vital spaces in the sector. SACE carried the responsibility of fostering a capable, ethical and professional teaching corporation through education, legislation, registration, professional development, and the maintenance of ethical and professional standards. On the other hand, Umalusi was the quality council for general and further education and training and played a critical role in safeguarding the credibility, integrity and quality of South Africa’s qualifications and assessments in the system.

As Members consider the plans for 2026/2027, it is important to do so with a clear focus. Such plans could not be treated as mere compliance exercises but read as instruments of delivery, accountability and public confidence. The plans would further show how these entities worked and intended to strengthen the sector in practical terms and how their work would contribute to better outcomes for South African learners, educators, and the rest of the country.

She highlighted that, for SACE, there were several issues which rightly demand close attention. These include the registration of fit-for-purpose educators, the strengthening of professional ethics, faster handling of misconduct matters, support for teacher development, and the continued professionalisation of early childhood development practitioners. SACE’s APP also placed clear emphasis on strengthening collaborative regulation and enforcement of ethical and professional standards, including digital systems and enhancing public confidence in the credibility of the teaching profession.

SACE had identified ethical standards and professional development as flagship initiatives. This was significant considering such a time when the country rightly expects teaching as a profession be respected, supported and held to the highest standards. Thus, an institution such as SACE was needed for both credible and effective carrying out of its mandate.

She recognised that Umalusi’s work often operated away from the public eye, but stressed that it was no less important because it underpinned the credibility and legitimacy of the education system. Public confidence in the value of school-leaving certificates depended on trust in the fairness of examinations, the integrity of marking and certification processes, and the standards applied to independent institutions. She noted that when Umalusi functioned effectively, it helped ensure that qualifications were trusted and that learners received recognition that was both credible and meaningful.

She highlighted to Members that the content provided was important and called on Members to guard against being too focused on inputs alone. Budgets mattered together with plans, systems and governance. However, in the end, the real test of whether these inputs produced results was in what they achieved. It was important to not only focus on what was planned and funded or reported, but what was actually achieved.

She expressed her gratitude to both entities for being present and assured everyone that she and the Deputy Minister, Dr Makgabo Reginah Mhaule, were both present in the meeting.

They are very careful oversight over what Council did, the sharpness of the questions which Council received and for their guidance. The Director-General of Basic Education in his absence, Mr Mweli, whose support for the work of Umalusi, and particularly the Council, had been a source of much strength to the sixth Council.

He invited Dr Rakometsi to begin presentations.

Dr Mafu S Rakometsi, CEO, Umalusi, began presentations by providing an overview of the institution, highlighting its responsibility for quality assurance of general and further education and training.

Ms Molly Mokoena, Senior Manager: Strategy and Governance, Umalusi, took Members through the institution’s key focus areas which include but are not limited to:

- Reviewing qualifications registered on the General and Further Education and Training Qualifications Sub-Framework;

- Enhancing moderators’ competencies through blended training approaches to support continuous professional development and minimise errors in question papers;

- Ensuring accurate and fair marking through the standardisation of marking guidelines and verification of marking processes; and

- Advocating the requirements for the accreditation of registered independent online schools.

She further highlighted Umalusi’s contribution to the Medium Term Development Plan (MTDP), one of which was its drafting of a ministerial advice based on the Mother Tongue-based Bilingual Education (MTbBE) Strategy, as engaged through Umalusi’s Research Forum. This advice covered Umalusi’s evaluation of the strengths and areas of improvement, with particular interest in the bearing that this strategy has for Umalusi as a quality council.

She further highlighted the programmes which form part of its APP, these include:

Programme one: Administration - To provide strategic leadership, management and administrative support services to the organisation;

Programme two: Qualifications and Research - To develop and manage an efficient and effective General and Further Education and Training Qualifications Sub-Framework (GFETQSF) within the NQF and to undertake strategic research in support of that goal; and

Programme three: Quality Assurance and Monitoring - To ensure that the providers of education and training have the capacity to deliver and assess qualifications registered on the GFETQSF and are doing so to the expected standards and quality.

She further highlighted some of the challenges Umalusi faced together with the steps taken to address these, i.e., human resource constraints, inadequate financial resources, increase in errors in question papers leading to marking concessions, etc.

Mr Hendrik van der Walt, CFO, Umalusi, provided an explanation on Umalusi’s budget. The budget for the 2026/27 financial year was R238 million, representing a nine-percent increase from the previous financial year (R218 million: 2025/26). The transfer from DBE (R182 million) accounts for 76.5% of the 2026/27 budget. The own revenue was expected to amount to R56 million in the 2026/27 financial year.

He further highlighted budget projections from 2023/2024 until 2028/2029, together with expenditure projections on each APP programme. He also presented the cost drivers which increased exponentially over the five-year period. These were IT expenses, travel, security services, repairs and maintenance.

South African Council for Educators (SACE) Annual Performance Plan 2026/27

Ms Ella Mokgalane, CEO, SACE, provided the Committee with an overview of the institution’s APP for the 2026/2027 financial year, in line with the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation’s (DPME’s) revised framework.

She highlighted planned activities were aligned to the available budget and focused on strengthening professional regulation, professionalisation of the teaching profession and public confidence in the teaching profession. The institution intended to embark on awareness campaigns and stakeholder engagements to ensure professionalisation of its teachers with strengthened professional standards and ethics.

She also provided a path/value chain for the professionalisation of the teaching profession and the importance of guiding teachers in being and growing as ethical, capable and developmental teachers. She further recognised that, while SACE had comprehensive legislative instruments, tools, and policies, it continued experiencing systemic operational challenges, including limited public awareness and trust, weak inter-institutional coordination, inconsistent enforcement of professional standards, resource constraints, and compromised quality assurance.

Ms Tuzana Sophethe, acting Head: Ethics and Professional Development, dealt with Part C of the presentation, highlighting amendments made to the Strategic plan and APP under programmes one, two and four.

APP Programmes include:

Administration: to ensure effective implementation of policy directives and Council priorities by providing strategic leadership, sound management, and comprehensive administrative support. This programme underpins the functional efficiency of SACE through the delivery of integrated support services that promote good governance, accountability, and organisational sustainability;

Professional Registration Induction and Certification: to register qualified educators and create sub-registers for special categories; maintain and update the educator database and enhance the quality of the registration of teachers by introducing standards;

Ethical Standards: to promote and maintain ethical standards in the teaching profession. It ensures that educators uphold the SACE Code of Professional Ethics through advocacy, monitoring, and enforcement processes. In doing so, the programme strengthens public trust in the profession and safeguards the integrity and credibility of educators in South Africa;

Professional Development Quality Management and Assurance: to provide comprehensive, high-quality professional development opportunities for educators and practitioners across all levels of the education sector. This includes designing, implementing, and evaluating structured training programmes, continuous professional development (CPD) initiatives, and capacity-building interventions that enhance pedagogical skills, leadership abilities, and subject-matter expertise; and

Research and Policy Coordination: to improve research coordination within SACE to strengthen its advisory role and service that is informed by policy, research, and consultative processes.

Mr Morris Mapindani, CFO, took Members through SACE’s budget review, highlighting its medium term projections. The total revenue for 2026/2027 was R140 964 million. He further provided a detailed budget for 2026/2027 financial year detailing the income received and operational expenditure expected. He said the 2026/2027 budget would be adjusted on the conclusion of the funding review. The Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF) projections were expected to improve accordingly. With the continuous implementation of membership compliance measures, SACE anticipated that additional revenue would be generated during the year and would be utilised to improve delivery levels.

Umalusi

Prof Ballim sought to respond to the question on quality assurance monitoring from Umalusi. He said the quality review process at Umalusi was in fact broader than simply monitoring. Umalusi also had an Assessment Standards Committee which received an extensive qualitative evaluation report from all examining authorities and made their decisions on the appropriateness, suitability and fairness of exams based on those reports.

Regarding outcomes from the research agenda, he said these were very much focused. The mandate of Umalusi was not only to provide quality assurance oversight. It was also to advise the Minister in the DBE on matters that Umalusi felt required attention, either at the request of the Minister or otherwise. He highlighted that a large part of Umalusi’s research was aimed at developing its understanding of the sector and, where necessary, providing advice to the Minister on where Umalusi believed development was needed.

Further, Umalusi had a Research Committee led by a Council member, he said the Research Committee did very competent work in areas that Umalusi believed needed attention. Of course, he recognised there were always more research questions that could be answered by Umalusi.

Regarding marks, he sought to reinforce the point that both the Minister and Dr Poliah made. He emphasised that withholding results was not the mandate of Umalusi. It did not have the capacity, ability or the mandate to do so. However, what Umalusi did keep an interest in was its capacity as quality oversight to ensure that the process had been followed. As indicated by Dr Poliah, DBE conducted the investigations. There would then withhold the results but at the end of the investigative process, they do need to refer to Umalusi for Umalusi to satisfy itself that the process was fair, reasonable and that the conclusions on whether or not to release results were properly arrived at.

Regarding questions relating to staff attrition, he sought to clarify that Umalusi did not have a high staff attrition rate. While staff would leave, such numbers were not unusual and significantly less than for an average private sector organisation. The problem here was in many ways Umalusi was a victim of its own success and in fact staff would leave to go to the DBE or the DHET. He sought to clarify that he was not implying DBE/DHET poached Umalusi staff but rather that this was a natural tendency with people who had specialised skills. Umalusi paid great attention to developing its staff and many reached various roles of promotion. He did not wish to embarrass Dr Poliah but he too was an example of someone who went from Umalusi to the DBE. He emphasised this was not a bad thing; it essentially was a pool of skills circulating in the education sector. While Umalusi would like to keep its staff it did not feel that skills were lost therefore its development efforts were not being wasted.

He further clarified that the practice of exit interviews was not unusual in Human Resources (HR) practice. It also provided Umalusi with an idea of what the issues were.

Regarding errors in marking guidelines, as explained by Dr Poliah, he wished to add that thankfully, almost all of those errors were discovered before exams were actually written, providing an opportunity for them to be corrected. The point he sought to make was that the system and the processes in place were sufficiently robust to pick up errors way before they become materialised. And as far as Umalusi was concerned this was the system that it would like to further develop and further defend, consolidate, and so on. On an ongoing basis, it looked for holes in the system which needed to be plugged and when errors occurred these were dealt with.

Regarding travel and accommodation costs, he said the problem was that these tasks could not be casualised. It could not merely find people in the area to assist on a contract basis. Umalusi required people who work in the office, who had developed their skills, who have developed the eyes for looking at things from a quality perspective. But more importantly, from an accountability point of view, Umalusi required people who were in the institution, who worked and considered themselves accountable to a higher authority, which is, of course, the organisation and its Council. Therefore, this required people with a specialist capacity. While Umalusi could find more efficient ways of doing things, the cost of travel was not unusual; such efforts would need to be done without compromising the confidence that Umalusi has in quality oversight.

He said that he recognised Mr Komane's concerns around hunger and the problem of agriculture in schools. The curriculum was the domain of the DBE. There was a programme in agricultural sciences in the NSC system. He understood Mr Komane was asking for more than this, but he said he disagreed with the argument made. He emphasised that he was convinced that democracy must be defended through institutional systems. And to ask schools to develop agricultural capacity in learners so that hunger and poverty issues may be addressed was to put the burden on the individual. It was a failure of a society. “Social institutions had failed when we find ourselves in a situation where children have to stand on the street and ask for R2 from people's car windows.” To provide private solutions to what were essentially public failures was incorrect. Yes, children may need to learn how to plant food, but they also need IT skills, mathematics, etc.

While Mr Komane may be right in saying that school may be a part of the solution, Prof Ballim said he was not convinced that school was the major or an important part of the solution. He felt this conversation was incredibly important for society to have. He handed over to the Umalusi team to deal with the rest of the questions.

Dr Rakometsi responded to the question on the number of online schools. He said that, according to the Independent Examinations Board (IEB) and the South African Comprehensive Assessment Institute (SACAI), there were 53 online schools. He highlighted that online schooling was not yet regulated and the only way Umalusi was able to find the numbers was through the IEB and SACAI because Umalusi had not accredited them. Umalusi had only issued directives to SACAI and the IEB, providing them with non-negotiables as placeholders until policy was implemented as some form of regulation.

He further confirmed Umalusi was not charging online schools because they were unregulated and Umalusi did not accredit them. Further, the DBE was also not registering them. Unfortunately Umalusi was not able to determine where unregistered and unaccredited schools were in the country because they were off its radar, and it did not have the capacity to police people who were operating illegally. Such issues must be reported to the SAPS to arrest people who are running schools illegally. Umalusi did not have the capacity to see where they were nor to monitor where they were operating illegally.

He highlighted investigations on irregularities were done by the DBE, SACAI or IEB. These were the assessment bodies, and it was up to them to come to Umalusi with a report having dealt with the irregularity reports through their irregularity committees. In the case of the DBE, it would start with the provincial irregularities committees, then they would go to the national irregularities committees. This would then be followed by the executive committee to make a recommendation on the results being withheld. In other words, Umalusi did not withhold the results on its own. It was the different assessment bodies which made recommendations that Umalusi withhold the results. He emphasised that Umalusi was not victimising anybody. As a statutory board, it could not victimise learners; it was established to serve and ensure the results they received were authentic, which could be defended in that way.

Regarding the criteria for accreditation, he said this was a very stringent process. Umalusi did not merely accredit schools; it followed a very stringent and rigorous criteria before a school was accredited. She further highlighted that, in the last 12 months, out of the money SACE received from adjusting the budget twice and the money that was coming from those owing SACE, it managed to come up with R11 million. However, the budget that was guaranteed was the R1.4 million approved by the Minister. She emphasised that, when comparing the two amounts, there was a serious disjuncture and disconnect between the figures. The R1.4 million would need to be used for almost 1 800 to 2 000 cases. The money was really a non-starter. It was money particularly allocated towards the unit that deals with the case load for travel, accommodation, and everything else the unit required. Further, there was no other funding or money that the unit was receiving outside of the particular money that had been allocated. She highlighted it was important to paint this picture for Members to understand the constraints they were under. Working within such constraints, SACE managed to deal with close to 600 plus cases within the financial year that just closed.

SACE took note of the cases raised by Members. Once Mr J Ndlebe was done with engagements, SACE would also need to get the educators’ and principals’ perspectives and not the SGB matter because this was not SACE’s area. She added that SACE could only deal with matters which were reported or reported on in the media. If they were not reported, it became a challenge for them to deal with.

On the questions raised by Ms Magagula, on why two research reports and not more, she clarified that this was due to budget constraints. Members would see from the APP that one of the programmes was only allocated R600 000 for the entire financial year yet this was a core function. It was these very constraints which required SACE to take robust action in ensuring it dealt with the issue of the collection of fees to make sure revenue was available within the organisation.

On the matter raised by Ms Van de Walt, she confirmed that SACE would follow up on the matter and provide the necessary information and follow up.

Mr Morris Mapindani, CFO, SACE, addressed the issue of non-compliance and SACE collecting money from unemployed graduates. He indicated that, from when the Council was established around 2006/2007, it was clear that unemployed graduates were not paying the membership fees. However, with the coming of the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), this became a challenge in that SACE was required to account for members and the membership fees. SACE was unable to account for all its members and could only account for those who were on the government payroll. Thus, SACE had to improve its operations and keep updated records of all its members in order to record its numbers as accurately as possible. He expressed difficulty in SACE being able to ascertain who was employed or unemployed. Therefore, it was unable to provide a figure on the monies collected from unemployed graduates.

Further, SACE worked with DHA and received updated records on members who had passed away and updated from government's payroll on those who had retired. When members failed to renew their membership fees, steps were also taken to communicate with members to do so in order for their membership to be in good standing. He highlighted that the membership fee was R 198 per year. And in instances where one failed to make such payment, a penalty fee of R150 for each year unpaid was required.

Regarding financial challenges affecting the delivery of its mandatory functions, as indicated by the CEO, the lack of sufficient financial support hinders SACE’s ability to execute its mandatory functions, especially when monies are needed to go to unavoidable contractual obligations, etc. He said Council had been engaging on this matter for the past three years with various stakeholders to finalise a sustainable funding model to address these constraints and ultimately get to a place where the organisation operated optimally. He hoped this would be finalised as soon as possible to get the ball rolling.

Regarding the vacancy rate, he indicated that there were four managerial positions that were vacant. These vacancies did affect delivery to some degree. He explained these positions could not be filled because of an ongoing job evaluation which also included a benchmarking exercise to ensure that, when the appointments were made, it was done in a well-informed manner. However, all other positions below such level were filled immediately.

Regarding the ICT improvements, he said that a number of improvements had been made with educators now being able to register online. There were also a number of other internal improvements made which directly affect educators. SACE had its own process in redeveloping its systems to adequately respond to its environment, to improve how systems operate and further develop some integration with other institutions. For example, SACE concluded the API integration with the DBE, whereby it shared the educator data in the same sector. It also worked with Umalusi in checking and verifying qualifications. Further, SACE concluded an agreement with DHA to share information on members who had passed and SAPS to verify offences. It was further working with tertiary institutions and the Department of Justice, as well as child protection services, to ensure on-the-spot verifications. These were just a few of the integration mechanisms it was currently finalising.

Concerning cybersecurity, SACE developed a firewall as part of its security and also saved all its data on a cloud environment managed outside of SACE to ensure that, should data be lost, it could be recovered. Further, SACE had a Microsoft authentication factor application, which it was using. It also had a virtual private network which controlled access.

Regarding the travel and accommodation budget, he explained that it was not necessarily the operating budget where the staff moved around. This was a consolidated figure for travelling, which included all meetings of Council. There was also a sub account within that, which dealt with the movement of personnel administration.

Ms Tuzana Sophethe, Acting Head of Ethics and Professional Development, SACE, addressed Ms Jordaan’s questions on the backlog of cases and the project that was there for two years. When SACE started the project, it had cases from 2019-2024 where it had about 2 621 backlog cases to deal with. Of the 2 621 cases, it investigated 1 231 cases, with 1 390 cases outstanding. It further received the following number of cases:

2022: 734 cases

2023: 576 cases

2024: 626 cases

2025/2026: at least 822 cases

SACE would provide the Committee with further details in its annual report. She recognised that there were problems, and the 300 target was questionable. Of the 822 cases received, it investigated about 70 of them, with 91 cases being dealt with. Further, in instances where educators were removed, this was communicated to the DBE. It further consulted with the nine provinces to ensure it was able to track closed cases and ensure such educators had been blocked from the system.

On the question raised on the increase of SMMEs, she said this was the first time the indicator was included within its APP. Therefore, it was cautious in not placing a high number. Its target was informed by the information it had within its system each year. It would be monitoring the figure and changes made if necessary going forward, but the information informing its decision was evidence-based.

On programme five, on the implementation of research, SACE was working closely with the performance information or planning as research, thus it was able to include and strengthen the performance indicators, especially within the mandatory programs within the Council. It was also important to ensure that this information was shared with other units such as professional registration and personal development, in order to close any gaps that the educators were showing. 

On key risks within the Council, she said SACE was aware thereof and thus trying to mitigate those accordingly. It further tracked these on a quarterly basis, ensuring they were dealt with. One of the issues identified was the problem of denied justice to learners and complainants. SACE was trying to resolve this problem through its case management system.

The Chairperson interjected and requested that SACE should wrap up. Members had to attend the plenary at 14:00, and some Members still had to travel thereto.

Ms Sophethe concluded responses by addressing the question regarding the 80% of internal and external audit. She said that the 2025 Strategic Plan and mid-term assessment showed that there were about 91 internal and external audits. SACE did about 78 of them which was around 86%. Therefore, this was how it got to that baseline of 80% to put it in the APP of 2026/2027.

Closing Remarks by the Chairperson

The Chairperson thanked everyone for their engagement and Members for their participation. She said the Committee hoped the two institutions would take into consideration the concerns that were raised by Members—especially where there were specific issues raised, such as specific names of schools, specific incidences that involve principals and teachers. The institutions, together with DBE, were expected to provide the Committee with a full report with feedback on how these matters were dealt with.

She was aware Members were stretched for time, and appreciated engagements once more. She responded regarding issues raised by Mr Louw and said she was still in session with the Office of the House Chair. She contacted the Office of the House Chair to request advice on how it would approach matters. There was a commitment that the legal team would come and brief her first and then brief Parliament, and then matters would be taken from there. The Committee would be provided with feedback on how the matter was being dealt with.

She said Members would meet next week at the oversight in Mpumalanga and hoped that Members would be available throughout the entire oversight and not simply for a day or two. She further highlighted that the meeting for next Thursday would need to be rescheduled due to the fact that it was the same day DHET was also sitting, and there were a number of Members of this Committee who were also Members of the Higher Education Committee. However, since it was only one item, it might need to be included in another agenda, but Members would be informed of the details after the meeting.

She expressed her gratitude for engagements today, and said Members would need to make their way to the House for the House sitting.

The meeting was adjourned.
