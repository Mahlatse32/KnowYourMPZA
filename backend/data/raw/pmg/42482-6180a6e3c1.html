Update on Foot-and-Mouth Disease outbreak; Department of Agriculture and Onderstepoort Biological Products Q1 & 2 Performance, with Minister

Agriculture

03 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Portfolio Committee on Agriculture met to receive briefings from the Department of Agriculture (DoA) on the outbreak of Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD), as well as the Department’s 2025/26 Quarter 1 and Quarter 2 performance and expenditure reports and progress on the implementation of its Audit Improvement Plan. The Committee also received a performance briefing from Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP) for the same reporting period.

The Department reported that the FMD outbreak had spread across all nine provinces, with the Free State, KwaZulu-Natal and Gauteng recording the highest number of outbreaks. Officials outlined a risk-based vaccination strategy and reported that vaccine doses had been procured and distributed to provinces, with further shipments expected. Members raised concerns about vaccine availability, enforcement of livestock movement controls, awareness among communal farmers, and the potential economic impact of the outbreak. The Department indicated that additional vaccines were being secured and that monitoring, awareness campaigns and coordination with provinces were being strengthened.

The Department also briefed the Committee on its mid-year performance, reporting that most of its targets had been achieved, although challenges remained in administration, audit outcomes and programme implementation. Members raised concerns about the qualified audit opinion, delays in invoice payments, cybersecurity risks and the performance of farmer support programmes. The Department reported that corrective measures were being implemented through its Audit Improvement Plan.

OBP reported financial and operational challenges during the first half of the financial year, including missed financial targets and declining revenue due to invoicing delays and production disruptions. However, the entity highlighted progress in farmer training, export market engagement and infrastructure upgrades aimed at improving vaccine production and availability. Members raised concerns about vaccine production capacity, compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice requirements and the entity’s financial sustainability. The Committee emphasised the importance of improved reporting, stronger financial management and continued updates on the vaccination rollout and audit improvement plans.

Meeting report

The Chairperson opened the meeting and outlined the agenda for the session, which included briefings and presentations from the Department and its entities.

She provided guidance on how the reports and presentations should be conducted, requesting that officials present their updates clearly to enable Members to engage effectively with the matters under discussion.

Minister’s opening remarks

Mr John Steenhuisen, The Minister of the Department of Agriculture (DoA), reported that a million high-potency vaccine doses for Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) had arrived from Argentina. He stated that by Wednesday the vaccines would begin to be administered in the field as part of the vaccination programme to control the outbreak.

He informed the Committee that he had visited KwaZulu-Natal on Friday to observe the vaccination rollout and had travelled to the Free State the previous day to monitor progress there. He expressed satisfaction with the progress made so far, noting that approximately 90 000 cattle had already been vaccinated in the Free State. He said the pace of vaccination there was encouraging and reflected the speed required for the rest of the country. The Minister added that he would continue visiting provinces to monitor the rollout and identify any blockages or operational challenges.

The Minister further reported that an additional 1.5 million vaccine doses from Dollvet in Türkiye were expected to arrive from Sunday and would be received by the Director-General. These doses would support the continuation and expansion of vaccination efforts across the remaining affected provinces.

He also informed the Committee that when the Cabinet recently discussed the FMD outbreak, it adopted a vaccination strategy as a key intervention. The Cabinet also emphasised the need for a regional approach to managing the disease. He noted that outbreaks had recently been reported in Zimbabwe, Lesotho and Eswatini. In response, the Cabinet resolved to establish an Inter-Ministerial Committee led by the Department of International Relations and Cooperation, together with the Department of Home Affairs, the Department of Agriculture and the Department of Defence, to engage neighbouring countries and develop a coordinated regional approach to addressing the outbreak.

Update on the foot and mouth disease presentation

Mr Mooketsa Ramasodi, Director-General, DoA, said he would in turn invite Dr Mike Modisane, Chief Director for Animal Production and Health, DoA, to present to the Committee.

Before commencing with the presentation, Dr Modisane said the Department would use the opportunity to update the Committee on the outbreak of FMD. He added that, following a request from the Committee, the Department would also provide an update on activities taking place in the provinces related to the proclamation of the disaster.

He noted that during the Department’s previous presentation, concerns had been raised about the allocation of vaccines to provinces, with some provinces indicating that they had received very limited quantities. He said the Minister had therefore advised the Department to develop a vaccine rollout plan. This plan would enable Members to better understand how vaccine doses would be allocated across provinces.

Dr Modisane then proceeded to present the Department’s update on the outbreak of FMD, provincial response activities, and the national vaccine rollout plan.

The presentation indicated that the FMD outbreak was severe and widespread, with cases reported across all nine provinces. A total of 935 outbreaks had been reported to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH). The most affected provinces were Free State with 277 reported outbreaks, KwaZulu-Natal with 202, and Gauteng with 195.

Dr Modisane noted that the actual number of outbreaks was likely significantly higher than those reported to WOAH because many suspected cases, identified through clinical signs, were still awaiting laboratory confirmation. Particular concern was raised about the spread of the disease in the Northern Cape, where an outbreak had been detected at a feedlot, and in the Western Cape, where the outbreak was expanding into the Malmesbury area.

The presentation outlined the control measures being implemented by provinces, although the scale of activities and the level of reporting varied.

The Western Cape reported vaccinating 13 913 animals and implementing daily roadblocks to monitor animal movement. The province had also secured R100 million in funding for the upcoming financial year to appoint additional veterinary technicians.

The Free State reported vaccinating 119 300 animals and operating 35 roving roadblocks. Weekly meetings of the Joint Operations Centre were also being held to coordinate response activities.

Other provinces provided less detailed reports. KwaZulu-Natal and North West indicated that awareness campaigns were ongoing and that meetings were being held with the South African Police Service (SAPS) to strengthen enforcement measures. However, limited information was provided on permits issued or funding allocations. Gauteng reported one roadblock and weekly Joint Operations Centre meetings.

Dr Modisane said that due to the widespread nature of the outbreak, the Department had adopted a risk-based vaccination strategy to guide the allocation of vaccines. The strategy was implemented at municipal level and relied on an epidemiological model that generated a heatmap identifying different levels of risk.

Three vaccination tiers were identified. Tier 1 consisted of Emergency Vaccination Zones, defined as areas within a 10 km radius of confirmed outbreaks. These zones were prioritised for immediate ring vaccination to halt the spread of the disease.

Tier 2 consisted of Primary Risk Areas, including provinces experiencing repeated outbreaks, such as parts of KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga and Limpopo, as well as areas with high cattle densities such as feedlots. The Department aimed to vaccinate 100% of the cattle population in these areas every six months to reduce transmission.

Tier 3 consisted of Secondary Risk Zones, which served as buffer areas adjacent to endemic regions. In these areas, strategic vaccination of high-risk herds was recommended.

The presentation estimated that South Africa had a national cattle population of more than 12.4 million animals. Of these, approximately 3.7 million cattle, representing about 30% of the national herd, were located in Primary Risk Areas.

The Department reported that 2.5 million vaccine doses had been received by the end of February 2026. These were supplied by Biogénesis Bagó and Dollvet, with one million doses sourced from Biogénesis Bagó and 1.5 million from Dollvet.

The vaccine allocation framework prioritised the risk-based strategy. Ten per cent of the doses, amounting to 250 000, had been reserved for emergency vaccination in the Western Cape, Northern Cape and Limpopo.

The remaining 2.25 million doses were distributed to provinces based on their proportion of cattle located in Tier 2 (Primary Risk) areas. KwaZulu-Natal, which had the highest number of cattle in this category at 43%, received the largest allocation of 960 473 doses.

Dr Modisane noted that the initial allocation would cover approximately 60% of the cattle population in Primary Risk Areas. Provinces would receive lists of priority municipal areas to guide distribution and ensure accurate record-keeping, including animal identification through tagging.

The Department recommended that the Portfolio Committee note the update on the current FMD outbreak, the control measures being implemented across provinces, and the details of the vaccine rollout plan for the doses already received.

Dr Modisane concluded the presentation by stating that provinces were expected to keep proper records of vaccination activities and provide geolocation data for commercial farms and other farming operations where vaccinations were being conducted. This would ensure that all animals vaccinated were accurately recorded and tracked.

He emphasised that the Department expected provinces to account for all vaccinated animals as part of strengthening monitoring and reporting systems in response to the outbreak of FMD.

Dr Modisane further concluded by requesting the Portfolio Committee to note the number of reported outbreaks, the interventions and support being provided by the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development, and the activities currently taking place across provinces to control the spread of the disease. He added that the Department would continue to update the Committee on developments, including progress on the vaccine rollout programme.

Discussion

The Chairperson said that in the presentation on vaccination statistics, the Department had not provided a clear comparison between the number of infected animals and the number of animals vaccinated in each province.

She said that, for example, the presentation did not indicate how many animals were infected in provinces such as the Western Cape, how many were expected to be vaccinated, and how many had already been vaccinated. She said such comparative data would assist the Committee in assessing progress in controlling the outbreak of FMD.

Mr C Smit (DA) sought clarity on aspects of the presentation. Referring to the colour coding used to classify primary, secondary and tertiary risk areas in the vaccination strategy, he said there appeared to be inconsistencies between the slides. He noted that the figures presented on page 20, which showed vaccination coverage in the tertiary category, did not seem to correspond with the colour coding reflected on the map on page 30. On the map, it appeared that the entire area had been classified as secondary risk. He asked the Department to clarify the relationship between the data presented and the colour-coded map.

Mr Smit also raised concerns about the provincial reports relating to permits for animal movement. He noted that while some provinces had reported on the number of animals vaccinated, roadblocks conducted, and related activities, several provinces had not provided information on these elements. He asked whether those provinces were expected to report on such matters and what the status of their reporting was. He emphasised that movement control and biosecurity measures were critical in managing the spread of FMD.

Lastly, he asked about the prospects of additional vaccine supplies beyond those already received. He requested clarity on whether further vaccines were expected and whether the situation in the Middle East might affect South Africa’s vaccine supply from Türkiye.

Dr N Nkabane (ANC) welcomed the presentation and said it was comprehensive and addressed many of the issues that the Committee had previously raised regarding the outbreak of FMD.

Referring to a study conducted between 2007 and 2016, she said expert opinion had indicated that significant factors contributing to FMD outbreaks included livestock population density, proximity to game reserves, and proximity to rivers where livestock may interact with wild animals. She asked the Department to clarify whether these same factors were responsible for the current outbreak or whether additional factors had been identified through the Department’s investigations in affected areas. She noted that the disease had occurred in the region before and therefore it was important to understand whether the drivers of the outbreak had changed.

Dr Nkabane also raised concerns about awareness levels among communal livestock farmers. She said there was a perception that some communal farmers might not fully understand that the movement of infected animals and animal products could contribute to the spread of the disease. In this regard, she asked what mechanisms the Department and the Ministry had put in place, in terms of the Animal Health Act, 2002, to ensure comprehensive information sharing, education and training for communal livestock farmers. She emphasised the need for improved event-based surveillance and prevention measures.

She added that awareness campaigns should take into account the realities faced by communal farmers. Some farmers might be illiterate or may not have access to online information platforms. As a result, face-to-face engagements and direct community interventions would be necessary to ensure that farmers understood the measures required to prevent the spread of the disease.

Dr Nkabane further commented on the Department’s decision to reserve 75 000 vaccine doses for emergency use. While she welcomed the intervention, she asked the Department to clarify whether it had sufficient storage capacity for these vaccines. She sought assurance that the Department had the appropriate facilities to maintain the vaccines at the required temperatures so that they would remain viable and effective when administered to animals. She requested a technical explanation on how the Department planned to manage vaccine storage and mitigate any potential risks associated with maintaining the cold chain.

She concluded by reiterating that she was satisfied with the overall presentation.

Ms L Arries (EFF) referred to the presentation and noted that the outbreak of FMD in the Western Cape had expanded towards the Malmesbury area, while the Northern Cape had reported an infection at a feedlot. She asked what specific containment measures had been implemented in response to these outbreaks.

She further asked whether any export abattoirs had been affected by the outbreak or whether any had been suspended as a result of the disease.

Ms Arries requested clarity on the number of highly suspected outbreaks that were still awaiting laboratory confirmation. She also noted that several provinces had not reported on certain activities in the presentation and asked whether the National Treasury had provided additional funding to provinces to respond to the outbreak. She asked which provinces, if any, remained unfunded.

Referring to issues previously raised by Mr Smit, she said the implementation of roadblocks was a key control measure. However, she had observed that some provinces with high infection rates had reported zero roadblocks. She asked the Department to clarify this matter.

She also referred to the functioning of Joint Operations Centres and provincial coordination meetings, noting that in the case of the North West the presentation had indicated “not reported” for certain coordination activities. She requested clarity on whether these structures were operational.

Ms Arries further noted that South Africa had approximately 12.5 million cattle, while only 2.5 million vaccine doses were currently available. She asked whether this quantity would be sufficient to interrupt transmission cycles.

She added that the presentation indicated that only 60% of cattle in the primary risk areas would be covered by the current vaccine allocation. She therefore asked what plans were in place to vaccinate the remaining 40% of animals and what enforcement measures would be taken against farmers who failed to comply with disease control measures.

Lastly, she asked the Department to indicate what the estimated economic losses resulting from the outbreak were to date.

Ms Ndalane also asked about the effectiveness of the vaccines being used to combat the outbreak of FMD. She asked whether the Department had plans in place for booster vaccinations if required.

Lastly, she asked what support mechanisms were available for farmers who had lost livestock as a result of the disease outbreak.

Mr A Trollip (ActionSA) thanked the Department for the report and raised several questions relating to the data presented and the implementation of vaccination measures to address the outbreak of FMD.

Referring to the vaccination figures presented, he said he had calculated that the total number of vaccinations administered to date amounted to 328 512 animals. In closing, she emphasised the importance of sound financial management, expressing the hope that the management of the FMD response would not result in the kinds of financial management challenges experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Department’s response

Dr Modisane responded to Members’ questions regarding the number of animals vaccinated, explaining that the information requested by the Committee was not included in the presentation because it had only recently been requested. The Department had begun collecting the data from provinces, but there were inconsistencies in the reporting periods. Some provinces reported the number of animals vaccinated from January, while others reported figures dating back to October, which made it difficult to present standardised information to the Committee.

He indicated that the Department was working to standardise reporting from provinces and had requested that all provinces submit vaccination data from October onwards. He also noted that it was important for the Department to distinguish between animals vaccinated with different vaccine types, including the Biogénesis vaccine and vaccines that would be procured from other suppliers, such as Dollvet from Türkiye. The Department would be getting additional vaccines from the Botswana Vaccine Institute (BVI). This would enable the Department to track which animals had received which vaccines.

Dr Modisane further acknowledged that the current vaccine supply was insufficient to conduct the mass vaccination programme required to control the outbreak. The Department was therefore in the process of securing additional vaccines.

Responding to questions about the colour-coding used in the outbreak maps, he explained that areas marked in darker red indicated concentrations of outbreaks, while lighter shades indicated areas where outbreaks had occurred but were less concentrated. The maps were also used to detect clusters of outbreaks across provincial boundaries, including areas involving North West and Gauteng.

He confirmed that according to the statistics presented, the Free State currently recorded more outbreaks (277) compared to KwaZulu-Natal (200). However, he cautioned that KwaZulu-Natal had experienced outbreaks since 2021, and many of those outbreaks had already been resolved or closed, which partly explained the differences reflected in the data.

Dr Modisane also informed the Committee that a new strain of the virus had been detected in October of the previous year, which had contributed to a new outbreak. He noted that the source of this outbreak was uncertain, as it had not originated from the Kruger National Park, which was historically a common source of FMD outbreaks. The last similar incident had occurred around 2008 in the northern parts of the country. The Department had also observed the spread of the disease to areas such as Gauteng, which had not been commonly associated with such outbreaks before.

In response to questions about the factors contributing to the spread of the disease, he stated that the Department did not yet have a definitive explanation. However, it was known that livestock movements played a significant role, including the practice of individuals purchasing cattle from communal areas, particularly those located near the Kruger National Park buffer zones, and transporting them to other areas.

Addressing questions about roadblocks and enforcement, Dr Modisane indicated that monitoring activities were undertaken in some provinces, although the information had not been fully reflected in the presentation, as some provinces had not anticipated that the Department would present those details at the meeting. He added that the Department would improve reporting on these enforcement activities going forward.

He further clarified the allocation of vaccines in relation to the classification of outbreak zones. The Department used a risk-based model that categorised areas into primary, secondary and tertiary zones. Approximately 80% of animals were located within primary zones, while secondary zones accounted for a smaller proportion, and tertiary zones represented the remaining share. These proportions were used to calculate and guide the distribution and allocation of vaccines across the affected areas.

Dr Modisane concluded by noting that the Department based its vaccine allocation decisions on these calculated distributions, which informed how many vaccines were directed to each zone.

Mr Smit interjected to clarify his earlier question to Dr Modisane. He referred to slide 27 of the presentation, which contained a colour-coded map indicating the different classifications of outbreak zones, including primary, secondary and tertiary zones.

Mr Smit noted that, according to the map, the Western Cape was classified as a tertiary zone, while Limpopo appeared to be classified as a secondary zone. However, when he compared this with the data presented on page 20 of the report, he observed that the figures reflected approximately 800 000 cattle in the tertiary category.

He therefore sought clarity on the relationship between the colour-coded map and the figures presented in the report, indicating that he was trying to understand how the zonal classifications corresponded with the number of animals recorded in each category.

Dr Modisane responded that the Member appeared to be referring to the allocation of vaccines in relation to the number of animals within the different zones. He noted that the figures reflected approximately 800 000 animals in the tertiary zone, about 200 000 in the secondary zone, and a smaller number within the primary zone, which guided the Department’s vaccine allocation.

Mr Smit indicated that he might not have been sufficiently clear in his question. He referred Members to the map on page 30 of the presentation, which showed Limpopo shaded in orange, indicating a secondary zone in terms of infection risk within the buffer areas. However, he noted that the figures presented on page 20 reflected 800 000 animals in the tertiary category and 200 000 in the secondary category, without a clear indication of how these figures corresponded with the map.

He therefore requested further clarity on the relationship between the map on page 30 and the figures presented on page 20, particularly in terms of how the zonal classifications corresponded with the number of animals recorded in each category.

Mr Madlala interjected to seek clarification regarding the vaccination figures presented by the Department. He asked whether small-scale farmers were included in the reported numbers of animals vaccinated.

He explained that his concern related to understanding the distribution of vaccinations between small-scale and commercial farmers. For example, if 11 000 animals had been vaccinated in a province, he wanted to know how many of those vaccinations were attributed to small-scale farmers and how many to commercial farmers.

Dr Modisane responded that the Department had not yet classified vaccination figures according to the type of farmers, such as small-scale or commercial farmers. However, he indicated that the majority of animals vaccinated in KwaZulu-Natal were located in communal areas. He added that with the latest developments in the outbreak, there would likely be a need for the Department to expand vaccination efforts further within communal areas. He noted that more detailed information could be requested from provinces to provide greater clarity on the distribution of vaccinations.

Mr Madlala maintained that in some areas of KwaZulu-Natal, small-scale farmers were not included in vaccination efforts and in certain cases were not even aware of the vaccination programme. He indicated that he was planning to visit the area the following day to further engage with farmers on the matter.

The Chairperson responded by cautioning against generalising the situation across the entire province. She noted that while there may be areas where farmers lacked information, it could not be assumed that all farmers in KwaZulu-Natal were unaware of the vaccination programme. She therefore requested that Mr Madlala specify the particular areas where these concerns were being reported so that the Department could respond appropriately.

The Minister rejected the claim that communal areas were not being included in the vaccination programme. He stated that all primary areas identified for vaccination were being covered, regardless of whether the farmers were commercial or communal. By way of example, he noted that over 30 000 cattle had already been vaccinated in KwaZulu-Natal, the majority of which belonged to communal farmers. The Minister emphasised that the Department had been clear from the outset that the vaccination rollout would include all categories of farmers within the identified primary areas.

The Chairperson noted that the Department had indicated that vaccinations had already taken place in communal areas, suggesting that the rollout might still be progressing to other areas referred to by Mr Madlala. She proposed that the Department should provide the Committee with a clearer breakdown of where vaccinations had already taken place and which areas were still to be covered.

The Chairperson also noted that additional vaccines had recently arrived, which created further opportunity to intensify the vaccination campaign, particularly in KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State, where infection numbers remained high. She therefore requested that when the Department returned to brief the Committee the following week, it should provide a detailed report indicating the specific areas where vaccinations had been conducted and where further vaccinations were planned.

She further observed that it appeared that commercial farmers were generally better protected against the disease, while many of the areas experiencing higher infection rates were communal farming areas. The Chairperson concluded by indicating that the Committee expected Dr Modisane to present a detailed progress report at the next meeting, which would allow Members to assess the progress of the vaccination programme.

Dr Modisane further informed the Committee that the Department was still awaiting additional funding to support aspects of the vaccination programme and the broader response to the outbreak. He noted that the Department did not have the authority to stockpile vaccines indefinitely, but it did maintain limited storage capacity for vaccines when necessary.

He explained that the Department was receiving assistance from the OBP in relation to vaccine storage and management. However, vaccines were not intended to be kept in storage for extended periods, as they were meant to be distributed and used when outbreaks occurred.

Minister’s response

Minister Steenhuisen noted that most of the issues raised had already been addressed to some extent by the Department. He used the opportunity to emphasise several important matters, particularly regarding the procurement, regulation and administration of FMD vaccines.

The Minister explained that certain agricultural lobby groups had initiated a court application seeking to allow the private importation and administration of vaccines without government control. He cautioned that such an approach could create serious risks for disease management and stressed that the Committee should understand the legal framework governing vaccine imports and use in South Africa.

He stated that any FMD vaccine imported into the country must be registered as a biological product in terms of relevant legislation, which requires the submission of a comprehensive dossier demonstrating the vaccine’s quality, safety and efficacy. In cases involving novel virus strains, written approval must be obtained from the Director of Animal Health and the Director of Genetic Resources. In addition, all imported vaccines require an import permit issued by the Director of Animal Health, regardless of whether they are imported by the state or private entities.

Minister Steenhuisen noted that the vaccines currently being imported into South Africa from suppliers such as Biogénesis, BVI and other international manufacturers were not yet formally registered in the country, which was common in emergency situations. In such cases, vaccines could be authorised through a Section 21 exemption issued by the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA) under the Medicines and Related Substances Act. This process allowed vaccines to be used temporarily during public health emergencies, similar to the approach used during the COVID-19 pandemic.

He emphasised that even when vaccines were registered or exempted, they could only be procured, distributed and administered for FMD control with written authorisation from the Director of Animal Health. This was necessary to ensure compatibility with the virus strains present in South Africa, as well as to maintain biosecurity standards, cold chain compliance and accurate reporting obligations.

The Minister further explained that the administration of FMD vaccines must comply with South African regulations and could only be performed by registered veterinarians or authorised animal health technicians. These requirements ensured that proper records were kept for each vaccination, enabling authorities to monitor vaccine use and track any potential complications or unintended consequences.

He compared these requirements with the regulatory controls applied during the COVID-19 vaccination programme, noting that vaccines could not simply be purchased and administered privately without oversight. Instead, authorised vaccinators in both the public and private sectors were permitted to administer vaccines under regulated conditions with strict record-keeping and tracking systems.

The Minister warned that allowing uncontrolled private importation of vaccines could lead to the introduction of new virus strains into the country, which would complicate efforts to manage the disease. He referred to a situation in KwaZulu-Natal where some farmers had attempted to import vaccines independently, which risked introducing strains not present in South Africa. He reiterated that any company, including private vaccine manufacturers or multinational pharmaceutical companies, could submit vaccines for approval through the Section 21 process, provided they supplied the necessary documentation and evidence of safety and compatibility with local virus strains. As an example, he referred to a private company that had successfully followed the process and received authorisation for its vaccine after submitting the required documentation and strain matching results.

Minister Steenhuisen emphasised that the current regulatory framework served as a safeguard to ensure that only safe and appropriate vaccines were used in the country. Allowing unrestricted importation and administration of vaccines, he warned, would make it far more difficult to control the spread of FMD and could result in new strains being introduced through uncontrolled vaccination practices.

He concluded by reiterating that the Department supported greater involvement of the private veterinary sector, noting that private veterinarians and animal health technicians could be authorised to administer vaccines once properly registered with the Department. He indicated that the Department had already authorised 64 private veterinarians in the Free State to assist with vaccination efforts. Minister’s closing remarks

Minister Steenhuisen thanked the Chairperson for the opportunity to comment on what he described as an important and sensitive matter. He acknowledged that the Sugar Master Plan and certain industrial matters fell under the mandate of the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (DTIC). However, he said it would be inappropriate for him, as Minister responsible for agriculture, to regard the matter as someone else's problem because of the significant impact on farmers.

He informed the Committee that he had already held two meetings with the South African Canegrowers Association, including representatives of small-scale growers. He noted that there was considerable frustration and concern within the sector. Although the entity involved was a corporate company, he explained that around 60% of its operations were closely linked to government-supported activities, including providing milling services to many small-scale sugarcane growers.

He warned that if the mills did not open in April, small-scale growers who were currently planting and preparing their crops would face serious difficulties because they would have no nearby facilities to process their cane and might be forced to transport it over much longer distances.

In his view, the situation had placed sugarcane farmers in the middle of a corporate dispute, where corporate strategies and interests risked destabilising the broader sugar industry. He confirmed that he had engaged with the Minister and Deputy Minister responsible for the relevant portfolio and that a joint effort was underway to resolve the matter. The aim was to ensure that mills would reopen in time for the new crushing season, particularly by 1 April, so that farmers would have access to milling facilities.

The Minister further noted that the situation highlighted the need for long-term reform within the sugar sector. He indicated that the industry would likely require restructuring and consolidation, as the existing model might no longer be sustainable. He added that developments within the sector could potentially create new opportunities for empowerment, including possibilities for greater participation by small-scale growers in milling operations. He welcomed the stance taken by ITAC and indicated that discussions were ongoing with stakeholders, including the sharing of innovative proposals to address the crisis.

In closing, the Chairperson noted that the Committee would continue to follow up on the matter with the relevant departments and institutions, particularly those responsible for the Sugar Master Plan and industrial interventions. She then formally adjourned the meeting.
