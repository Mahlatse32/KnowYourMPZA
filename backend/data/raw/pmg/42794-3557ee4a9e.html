Department of Correctional Services Annual Performance Plan 2026/27; with Minister

Correctional Services

21 April 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Ramolobeng (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Annual Performance Plans (APPs) of Government Departments and Entities

The Department of Correctional Services’ (DCS) 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan (APP) and budget briefing outlined its strategic priorities, financial constraints, and key operational challenges over the medium term. Its core priorities – enhancing security, reducing overcrowding, strengthening rehabilitation and reintegration programmes, and improving service delivery – are significantly constrained by limited funding, with approximately 70% of the budget allocated to personnel costs, leaving insufficient resources for infrastructure, rehabilitation programmes, and operational improvements.

A central issue throughout the discussion was persistent overcrowding, driven largely by high numbers of remand detainees. While DCS has implemented measures such as court referrals, interdepartmental collaboration, and infrastructure planning, overcrowding remains a systemic challenge with serious implications for safety, health, and rehabilitation outcomes. Members of Parliament raised extensive concerns about the effectiveness and credibility of the APP, arguing that it is overly focused on outputs and compliance rather than real outcomes. Key criticisms included: Weak measurement of rehabilitation success, particularly the lack of tracking of reoffending and post-release employment, unclear or poorly designed performance indicators, some of which may distort results or allow double counting, limited ambition in targets related to overcrowding, safety, and infrastructure development. Also noted was the slow progress in Information and communication technology (ICT) modernisation and ongoing reliance on manual systems, insufficient alignment between strategic goals, budget allocation, and measurable impact. Additional concerns included delays in infrastructure projects, declining participation in reintegration programmes, shortages of healthcare staff, and risks related to security, contraband, and inmate safety.

In response, DCS acknowledged many of these challenges, particularly budget constraints, staffing shortages, and infrastructure delays. It indicated efforts to improve performance through better training, interdepartmental collaboration, and gradual inclusion of more outcome-based indicators. However, it also noted that factors such as unemployment, recidivism, and overcrowding are influenced by broader socio-economic conditions beyond its direct control.

The Committee recognised some progress, including improvements in employment equity and certain operational areas, but stressed the need for: Stronger outcome-based planning and reporting, more ambitious and realistic targets. Improved infrastructure transparency and deliver greater accountability and follow through on commitments.

Meeting report

Department of Correctional Services Annual Performance Plan

The presentation was delivered in two parts: the programme performance component, presented by Mr Joseph Katenga Chief Deputy Commissioner: Strategic Management, followed by the financial report presented by Mr Lebogang Marumule, DCS CFO and Acting National Commissioner. Together, these provided a comprehensive overview of the Department’s Annual Performance Plan and Medium-Term Expenditure Framework for 2026/27 to 2028/29. The briefing outlined the DCS strategic priorities, performance targets, and financial constraints. It emphasised efforts to strengthen accountability through refined performance indicators, with a greater focus on measurable outcomes, while enhancing service delivery across incarceration, rehabilitation, care, and community reintegration.

Key priorities include enhancing security within correctional facilities, reducing incidents such as escapes and assaults, and addressing overcrowding through interdepartmental collaboration. DCS says it places strong emphasis on rehabilitation, education, and skills development, shifting towards measuring programme completion to improve reintegration outcomes and reduce reoffending. From a financial perspective, the budget shows only marginal growth over the medium term, accompanied by baseline reductions in outer years. Expenditure is largely driven by compensation of employees, which consumes approximately 70% of the budget, leaving limited resources for operational needs, infrastructure, and programme expansion. Incarceration remains the largest area of spending, followed by administration.

Key cost drivers include personnel, inmate care, property management, and infrastructure. Although there are modest increases in capital and infrastructure allocations, these are insufficient to address maintenance backlogs and facility pressures. Budget cuts and fiscal constraints continue to pose significant risks to service delivery. Overall, the Department’s plans align with broader government priorities such as job creation, poverty reduction, and building a capable and ethical state. However, persistent challenges including overcrowding, staffing pressures, infrastructure needs, and constrained funding require ongoing engagement with National Treasury and improved resource management to ensure the effective implementation of its mandate.

The Ministry was invited by the Chairperson to comment and Deputy Minister, Ms Lindiwe Ntshalintshali, indicated that she was satisfied with the presentation, noting that it accurately reflected the Department’s experiences and clearly illustrated the impact of budget cuts on its operations.

Minister of Correctional Services, Dr Pieter Groenewald, highlighted that a key challenge is that approximately 70% of the DCS budget is allocated to salaries, over which it has limited control, as salary increases are not determined within the Department. He noted that this significantly constrains funding available for operational requirements and he cautioned Members to take this into account during budget deliberations.

Discussion

Mr J Engelbrecht (DA) stated that the plan is overly complex and insufficiently strategic. While it contains numerous indicators, data points, and detailed technical definitions, it fails to address fundamental outcome-based questions, such as if inmates are safer, reoffending is decreasing, communities have greater trust in the system, and victims are being supported. The report focuses more on planned activities than on demonstrating real impact or meaningful change for inmates, parolees, victims, or the public. Targets were described as being unclear and indicators as potentially open to manipulation, with weak integration between programmes. Concerns were also raised about slow progress in infrastructure and ICT planning relative to the scale of existing challenges, as well as community initiatives that are broad but lack clear costing and measurable outcomes. The situational analysis, although extensive, was criticised for failing to translate broader economic and social trends into concrete operational decisions for DCS. Overall, the key concern was that there is a disconnect between the data presented and the actions proposed, with insufficient alignment between identified challenges and the Department’s strategic response.

Reference was made to the SWOT analysis, noting that while it identifies key weaknesses, such as delays in filling vacant posts, it does not outline clear strategies to address them, relying instead on incremental target increases. The Department focuses on measuring activities rather than outcomes. Using Programme Three as an example, he pointed out that while the Department tracks the number of offenders completing occupational skills programmes and those working on DCS farms, it does not assess if these interventions lead to reduced reoffending, employment after release, or income generation. Programme completion is treated as a success, even where it does not translate into meaningful outcomes. For instance, a parolee who completes a welding course but remains unemployed represents a failed outcome, yet this is not reflected in the APP. Similarly, although DCS reports on Grade 12 pass rates, it does not track if successful candidates proceed to tertiary education or gain employment.

Programme Five was criticised, arguing that the indicator on economic opportunities for parolees and probationers is poorly defined. He noted that it combines short-term training, stipends, and full employment, which conflates very different outcomes and results in misleading reporting. This approach allows for superficial “box-ticking” rather than meaningful measurement. A more accurate indicator would track if parolees remained in employment for at least six months or more after placement.

Of Programme Four, clinical indicators such as HIV viral load suppression and TB cure rates are valid outcome measures. However, the was concerns about the indicator for newly diagnosed hypertension patients initiated on treatment being set at 100%, arguing that once this target is achieved there is no further assessment of treatment quality, adherence, or complications. It was therefore described as a compliance focused, rather than outcome driven measure. Overcrowding was highlighted as a major and persistent challenge. Argument was made that the current target of 50% does not represent a meaningful reduction strategy but reflects acceptance of the existing situation. This level of overcrowding continues to render facilities unsafe and inhumane, and the plan does not set out a progressive pathway to significantly reduce overcrowding, suggesting limited ambition to materially improve conditions.

Mr Engelbrecht noted the lack of meaningful progress in key security related indicators. The rate of inmates injured due to reported assaults in DCS custody has only decreased by 0.010% over three years, which he described as a sign of negligence in a system affected by gang violence. Such a marginal reduction reflects a lack of ambition for transformative safety and prevention measures. Criticism was made about indicator 7.2.2.3 on unnatural deaths in custody, which remains fixed at 0.032%. The unchanged target suggests no real intention to improve outcomes, questioning why more ambitious reductions such as 0.03% are not being pursued.

More broadly, the report relies heavily on passive language such as “the Department will continue,” rather than reflecting active reform. The plan should demonstrate redesign, acceleration, and innovation, including bold initiatives such as large scale electronic monitoring for low risk detainees or community based sentencing to reduce overcrowding. There is a disconnect between identified strategic risks and performance indicators. For example, while facility maintenance failures are linked to issues such as weak national building capacity and inadequate budget allocation, the corresponding indicator only measures the number of infrastructure projects completed. It does not track service level agreement compliance, efficiency improvements, or reductions in maintenance backlogs. Similarly, of the risk of reoffending among parolees attributed partly to limited transport for monitoring the proposed solution is vehicle procurement. However, the related social reintegration indicators focus only on participation and compliance, without assessing if improved resources (vehicles) reduce reoffending rates.

Of indicator 7.3.3.11 on spiritual care services, an inmate is counted only once per financial year, regardless of if they receive a brief 15 minute interaction or ongoing counselling. This undermined the ability to distinguish between meaningful support and minimal engagement. For indicator 7.5.3.5 on economic opportunities for parolees and probationers, the same individual can be counted multiple times if they receive more than one opportunity within a financial year. This inflates performance figures without reflecting the actual number of individuals successfully placed into sustainable employment. On indicator 7.2.3.2 on inmate assaults, the methodology allows an inmate injured in separate incidents to be counted multiple times within a month. While acknowledging that the approach may be methodologically consistent, it results in distorted reporting, where a single inmate assaulted multiple times is recorded repeatedly. A supplementary indicator tracking the percentage of inmates who experience at least one assault would provide a more accurate measure of overall safety within correctional facilities.

The Department’s planning approach is fragmented, with programmes measured in isolation rather than as part of an integrated correctional system. He illustrated this by noting inconsistencies between Programme Two, which aims to reduce overcrowding to 50%, Programme Five, which seeks to maintain a 97% non-revocation rate for parolees. In Programme Three, which targets a 99% completion rate for correctional programmes, makes no indicator linking parole decisions to programme completion timelines, reflecting a lack of system wide coordination in how offender management is measured.

On the lack of alignment between strategic goals and measurable outcomes, while DCS promotes inclusive growth and job creation, it does not track post release employment outcomes. Similarly, although it claims to be building a capable and ethical state, it does not measure the effectiveness of anti-corruption training or reductions in disciplinary backlogs. Of infrastructure, Mr Engelbrecht noted that the projects listed (pages 159–164) are largely small-scale maintenance interventions, with very few major capacity expansion initiatives. Given overcrowding levels of approximately 59%,the planned addition of 750 beds is insufficient to meaningfully address the challenge.

On information technology (indicators 7.1.3.10–7.1.3.12), the DCS slow pace of digital transformation would take decades to achieve meaningful integration. Continued reliance on manual record keeping undermines data reliability, particularly on inmate information, and current targets do not reflect the urgency of modernising correctional systems. Annexure 17 lists approximately 300 community initiatives such as cleaning, gardening, and painting schools. While acknowledging their value, he argued that these activities lack clear impact measures in community trust, rehabilitation outcomes, or crime reduction. The absence of costing and output indicators makes it difficult to assess value for money, and raises ethical concerns about if parole labour is being used to compensate for insufficient departmental maintenance resources.

Finally, Mr Engelbrecht criticised the absence of a learning framework in the plan, noting that there is no clear mechanism to inform future planning cycles. There is also no section reflecting lessons learned from previous years, which he saw as a major gap in institutional learning and continuous improvement.

In summary, he argued that the APP must shift from a compliance document focused on activities to a strategic plan focused on real outcomes. The should have indicators that measure post-release employment, recidivism reduction, victim satisfaction, and family reunification, rather than mainly counting participation and outputs. Stress was placed for more ambitious targets on overcrowding and violence, closing loopholes that allow double counting, and improving integration across programmes to address systemic issues. Accelerating IT and infrastructure development was urgent introducing a learning system that uses performance data to adjust plans, and replacing passive language with clear, action-oriented commitments. Without these changes, the APP remains detailed but lacks real strategic impact and does not deliver meaningful transformation.

Ms K Kgobisa-Ncaba (DA) said that given the department’s stated crisis, it is misplaced to focus on tracking if sufficient youth or women are employed. The priority should be appointing the most suitable candidates, particularly considering limited resources. Referring to slide 8, she welcomed the shift away from simply counting the number of approved parole profiles but raised concerns about the new indicator measuring the percentage of cases decided by the parole board. She questioned if DCS is properly examining the reasons behind those decisions, noting that some applications may be rejected due to incomplete or incorrectly submitted documentation. Emphasis was placed on assessing the quality of parole reports to support sound decision-making and to avoid both inappropriate releases and unjust denials. Referring to slide 63, she questioned a significant increase in the “household” budget allocation, which rose from R643 000 in 2025/26 to around R1 billion in the current financial year. Request was made for a clear breakdown of what this funding is intended to cover.

Mr M Moela (ANC) noted that a Programme One project, that piloted in 2017, remains unresolved. He questioned if DCS has conducted a skills audit of personnel to assess if staff can implement programmes effectively once legal challenges are resolved. What preparatory steps are being taken to ensure readiness for implementation in 2026/27? What contingency plans exist should litigation remain unresolved?

On infrastructure projects, Mr Moela highlighted several long-standing delays, including facilities such as a broiler project at Grootvlei (started 2021), a bakery at Groenpunt (started 2022 with completion projected for 2027), security towers in Leeukop (started 2020), and a bakery at Qalakabusha (started 2022). Such delays appear systemic rather than exceptional. Request was made for clarity on the causes of delays, the financial cost of each delayed project, including cost overruns, and the broader cost of continued postponements. He asked if DCS would provide quarterly progress reports until project completion.

On self-sufficiency and sustainability programmes, Mr Moela noted inconsistent implementation across regions, arguing that this undermines rehabilitation and increases the risk of reoffending. A request on clarity was made to how regions are affected, how inconsistencies are impacting outcomes, and what measures are in place to ensure uniform implementation nationwide. A significant decline in the number of parolees and probationers participating in economic initiatives. He questioned the reasons for this reduction and how it aligns with DCS’s mandate to support social reintegration and reduce recidivism.

Mr M Sokopo (ANC) focused on youth employment, security, rehabilitation outcomes, agricultural programmes, and healthcare capacity.

He stated that youth empowerment is a non-negotiable priority for the ANC. While DCS achieved 43% youth employment in 2024/25, this reportedly declined by 13% in 2025/26. He asked for the cause of this decline and what corrective measures and targets are in place to restore and sustain the 40% benchmark. Secondly, he raised concerns about security operations, noting that current sub-programmes appear focused mainly on inmate safety. Is sufficient attention being given to the safety and security of correctional officials?

Thirdly, criticism was made to the skills development approach for focusing on outputs rather than outcomes. DCS should track post-release results such as employment rates and reductions in recidivism. He requested clarity on plans to strengthen outcome-based reporting and improve linkages between trained offenders and opportunities outside correctional facilities.

Fourthly, on agricultural and self-sufficiency programmes, what is the DCS mandate on ensuring these facilities operate effectively, humanely, and fulfil their intended rehabilitation and skills development purpose.

Finally, Mr Sokopo asked how DCS plans to address shortages and prevent adverse health outcomes for inmates due to shortages of healthcare professionals within correctional facilities..

Mr E Cloete (ANC) discussed programme sustainability, infrastructure funding, and the effectiveness of reintegration partnerships. Under Programme One, reference was made to the correctional learnership programme involving about 6 192 learners. Can DCS guarantee that the programme will not be interrupted due to funding constraints? How is the programme currently being monitored and evaluated, including if a structured quarterly review mechanism is in place. In the Incarceration Programme, he noted that R2.4 billion required for the operation of the Mangaung Correctional Centre will be reprioritised from facility sub-programme baselines, which are already under pressure. Clarity was asked on how DCS will manage infrastructure maintenance obligations across the correctional estate without compromising safety, humane conditions, and overall service delivery. Under Social Integration, he requested details on DCS concrete engagements with the private sector, civil society organisations, and municipalities to create economic opportunities for parolees. What measurable outcomes have resulted from these partnerships to date, and how DCS plans to scale successful initiatives?

Mr E Hendricks (PA) requested clarity on the key strategic shifts in the 2026/27 APP compared to 2025/26, and what factors informed these changes. How does DCS ensure alignment of the APP with the National Development Plan 2030, the Sustainable Development Goals, and the District Development Model? Under Administration, he questioned what concrete steps are being taken to achieve the 40% youth employment target in 2026/27, and how DCS plans to successfully recruit and employ 2 500 trained entry-level correctional officers within a single financial year, given previous implementation challenges?

Noting the concern about overcrowding, Mr Hendricks asked how DCS accounts for its contribution to this issue within the broader criminal justice system context. On Rehabilitation, he requested on how DCS measures if rehabilitation programmes effectively reduce reoffending. Why do completion targets for skills programmes remain lower than participation levels, and how are education and training such as TVET and Sector Education Training Authorities (SETA) programmes aligned with labour market needs? Under Care, he questioned if near universal targets for HIV, TB, hypertension, and diabetes treatment are sustainable in 2026/27.

On Reintegration, Mr Hendricks asked for an explanation on the decline in community participation, how restorative justice impacts are measured over the long term, and what mechanisms are in place to prevent reoffending, particularly given offenders’ socio-economic vulnerabilities upon release. He also questioned the allocation of more than 59% of the budget to incarceration, arguing that rehabilitation and reintegration needs remain under-resourced. Finally, under ICT and Cybersecurity, he asked what progress has been made in mitigating cyber risks, particularly in light of increasing reliance on digital systems.

Ms N Nolutshungu (EFF) had several concerns on the effectiveness, balance, and implementation of DCS APP, arguing that it remains largely compliance driven rather than outcome-focused.

The APP contains limited indicators that demonstrate system-wide outcomes such as reduced reoffending, improved institutional stability, or meaningful rehabilitation impact. The plan appears achievable on paper but not transformative in practice, and she asked which indicators genuinely reflect improvements in correctional outcomes rather than administrative compliance?

On overcrowding, she noted that the APP’s static target of 50% suggests acceptance of the current situation rather than a reduction strategy. The impacts of overcrowding included increased violence, gang influence, pressure on health and sanitation systems, and reduced rehabilitation effectiveness. What concrete measures are being implemented to reduce overcrowding? On security and contraband control, the current indicators focused on escapes, assaults, and deaths do not fully capture internal security risks. The absence of indicators on contraband such as drugs and cell phones is a significant gap, as these undermine institutional integrity and may involve internal collusion. Why are contraband related indicators not included and what systems are in place to detect and disrupt such networks?

On rehabilitation, Ms Nolutshungu pointed to a shift from measuring participation to completion of programmes, noting that completion rates are often lower than participation rates (such as TVET and occupational skills programmes). This reflects adjustment of targets to match performance rather than addressing underlying causes of non-completion. Concern was raised about declining participation in community reintegration initiatives and she asked what factors explain the gap between participation and completion, as well as the reduction in targets. Of the budget, she argued that the allocation prioritises incarceration over rehabilitation and reintegration, reinforcing containment rather than behavioural change. She questioned how this allocation aligns with the DCS rehabilitative mandate and what level of funding would be required to meaningfully reduce recidivism and strengthen rehabilitation outcomes.

Finally, she highlighted the slow implementation of the Integrated Inmate Management System, noting that only a single module is being rolled out while reliance on manual systems continues. This has serious implications for inmate tracking, parole decisions, case management, and accountability. Clarity was requested on the constraints delaying full implementation and a definitive timeline for system-wide rollout across all facilities.

Mr C Niehaus (EFF) argued that while the APP contains detailed targets and indicators, it is low on strategic insight and does not adequately address how transformation will be achieved. He acknowledged some positive elements, including anti-corruption targets, youth employment initiatives, self-sufficiency programmes (such as farms and workshops), and restorative justice efforts. However, he cautioned that the feasibility of these objectives remains uncertain.

Overcrowding in correctional facilities is the most persistent and critical challenge. This, combined with high recidivism, weak rehabilitation outcomes, low reintegration success, and declining infrastructure capacity, reflects a system operating under severe constraints while inmate populations continue to rise. Mr Niehaus identified a structural imbalance in budget allocation, with 79.3% allocated to administration and incarceration, leaving only 20.7% for rehabilitation, care, and reintegration. He argued this contradicts the White Paper’s emphasis on rehabilitation. Concern was raised about reprioritisation of funds away from infrastructure toward public private partnership (PPP) arrangements, warning that this may further weaken facility maintenance and development. He highlighted continued inefficiencies in core programmes and the financial strain caused by staffing costs, vacancies, and delays in filling posts. Performance data shows increasing inmate populations without corresponding expansion in bed space, worsening overcrowding and its associated risks, including violence, health pressures, sanitation failures, and human rights concerns.

On security, Mr Niehaus said only marginal improvements are shown in indicators such as escapes and assaults, while unnatural deaths remain static. There is insufficient focus on official safety, gang activity, contraband networks, and internal breaches, which continue to undermine institutional control. He also raised concerns about infrastructure and operational disruptions, including delays in farm and agricultural projects, SPCA-related animal welfare issues, land invasions in multiple provinces, and the impact on self-sufficiency programmes.

On rehabilitation, Mr Niehaus criticised the voluntary nature of programmes and low completion targets, arguing that this limits effectiveness. Highlight was made to the inconsistencies in implementation, weak linkage to labour market needs, and outdated systems that hinder meaningful reintegration outcomes. He expressed concern over delays in the Integrated Inmate Management System (IIMS), noting continued reliance on manual systems and limited digital progress despite long-standing legal and operational challenges. Concern was also raised about procurement risks, elite capture, and historical biases in correctional decision-making systems, outdated methods and inconsistent implementation.

Mr Niehaus raised a series of concise questions to DCS focused on its systemic challenges:

  • What measures are being implemented to address ongoing concerns about unnatural deaths, assaults, and staff-involved breaches, and if these amount to a human rights crisis within correctional facilities?
  • Why DCS has not more actively pursued the release of elderly prisoners, including state patients, where appropriate?
  • Why are rehabilitation interventions not made compulsory and more closely aligned with labour market needs?
  • What safeguards are in place to prevent elite capture, and how the proposed Amendment Bill will address historical biases in parole decision-making?
  • How does DCS intend to manage ageing facilities in light of funding reprioritisation and delayed projects.
  • What legal and operational responses are being implemented about land invasions and illegal occupation affecting correctional farms and how do these disruptions impact self-sufficiency and sustainability programmes?
  • How effective is correctional sentence planning currently?
  • How frequently are officials vetted and rotated and what measures address the increasing smuggling of contraband into correctional facilities?

Mr Niehaus recommended a shift towards more evidence based reporting, arguing that DCS should move beyond presenting only statistics and targets. There was a call for clearer outcome-focused reporting on overcrowding reduction, remand detainee decongestion, recidivism rates, and outcomes of farm and self-sufficiency programmes. He proposed that the Committee should require regular briefings on PPP handovers and the delays affecting the Integrated Inmate Management System (IIMS), particularly where arbitration processes are slowing implementation. He recommended that Parliament assess the impact of budget reprioritisation, especially of farm operations, land invasions, and delays in infrastructure development, as these are affecting operational stability and sustainability initiatives. In addition, proposed quarterly reporting requirements on key human rights and safety indicators, including unnatural deaths, assaults, official safety, mental health support, and psychological care services within correctional facilities. There should be an emphasis to better align departmental performance with Justice Crime Prevention Security (JCPS) cluster deliverables and broader social and economic drivers of crime. This includes establishing clear, measurable linkages between correctional skills development programmes and national economic transformation objectives.

Mr M Gasa (MKP) was concerned about the realism and coherence of DCS targets and implementation capacity. The credibility of near-perfect health targets in the context of a shortage of healthcare professionals and clinic infrastructure was questioned and these targets may not reflect operational reality. He asked if such targets represent genuine performance goals or are primarily designed to present an idealised picture on paper despite known staffing and resource constraints? DCS acknowledges the risk of reoffending and the role of post-release unemployment and stigma, but in practice focuses on limited interventions that reach only a small number of offenders, such as assisting about 650 individuals into employment, which he views as insufficient relative to need.

On workforce capacity and modernisation, he highlighted a 14.7% vacancy rate and the loss of senior staff through retirement, alongside a shortage of ICT professionals. He expressed concern that DCS is simultaneously attempting to implement advanced ICT while lacking the necessary human capacity to maintain them. He questioned who will operate and sustain new technological systems in a context of declining experienced personnel and limited technical expertise. There is a mismatch between ambitions for modernisation and existing institutional capacity.

Mr L Shangase (MKP) requested clarity on the number of additional beds DCS plans to provide in 2026/27, noting that in the previous year inmates were reportedly sleeping on the floor without mattresses. Emphasis was made for the need for improved capacity to address overcrowding and basic living conditions. Secondly, what budget allocation is being set aside to procure BOSS (Body Orifice Security Scanner) chairs, noting that these chairs appear to improve working conditions for DCS staff, particularly during the intake and processing of new inmates.

Ms A Ramolobeng (ANC) noted the significant 37% reduction in the target for parolees and probationers participating in economic programmes (reduced to 13,113). She questioned the rationale for this decline and asked how DCS justifies this with its mandate to support reintegration and reduce recidivism. Secondly, what strategies are in place to prevent, detect, and address unauthorised, irregular, and fruitless expenditure, and how regularly are these controls reviewed for effectiveness?

Ms Ramolobeng said reference was made to a previous commitment made during a KwaZulu-Natal oversight visit on the prioritisation of vehicle allocation in KZN. Has this commitment been implemented in the current budget or, if not, why has it not been included? There had been discussions in a prior meeting on unnatural deaths and the inmate amnesty/release process, where DCS reportedly committed to equipment upgrades. She asked if these upgrades have been implemented in the current financial year, and if not, requested reasons for the delay, stressing the need for accountability on prior commitments. Lastly, she noted the committee recommendation for BOSS chairs and asked for an update on procurement progress, if quotations have been obtained, and if these items are reflected and prioritised in the current APP budget allocation.

Ministry response

Minister of Correctional Services, Dr Pieter Groenewald, acknowledged that there are different frameworks used to review key performance areas but emphasised that reoffending remains the most critical indicator of rehabilitation success. However, DCS cannot guarantee that a released offender will not reoffend, as this is influenced by broader social and economic conditions beyond its control. On post-release employment, DCS aims to facilitate job opportunities for parolees but cannot guarantee employment due to South Africa’s high unemployment rate. This is a national socio-economic challenge rather than a correctional services-specific issue.

On compulsory training and education programmes, the Minister explained that participation is subject to eligibility requirements and qualifications, meaning not all offenders can access all courses. Selection criteria therefore apply.

On budget monitoring, in year expenditure tracking systems are in place and allow for adjustments where necessary. Certain matters raised by the Committee, such as ICT discussions, would be addressed in dedicated future meetings. He confirmed that discussions have taken place with the SPCA and collaboration is ongoing to address animal welfare concerns in agricultural facilities.

The Minister said that overcrowding remains a serious challenge and current figures are not ideal. Sentenced offenders and remand detainee populations exceed available bed space, resulting in inmates at times sleeping on mattresses on the floor. However, measures are being implemented and the issue is actively being addressed through ongoing planning processes. DCS is considering incorporating additional outcome-based indicators into the APP to better reflect rehabilitation impact, alongside output measures.

On infrastructure delays, the Minister expressed frustration over slow progress on projects, citing delays caused by external departments such as Public Works, which have affected facilities including agricultural and production projects. On self-sufficiency programmes, successful implementation requires adequate funding and support to ensure sustainable agricultural production and operational capacity.

Finally, on employment equity and recruitment, the Minister stated that it is legally bound to implement existing legislation and targets, even where there may be disagreement. Any changes would require legislative amendment. He indicated support for recommendations aimed at improving operational effectiveness.

Deputy Minister, Ms Lindiwe Ntshalintshali, thanked Members for their comments and recommendations, noting that they are valued and will be taken into consideration. Concerns on the structure of the APP will be addressed through more detailed unpacking of specific targets in future reporting, such as the expansion of ICT related programmes, while the current presentation provides only a broad overview.

She said that DCS is operating under financial constraints, which are largely beyond its control. Vacancies in healthcare services are being addressed through ongoing recruitment processes, with posts currently advertised and filling still in progress.

On youth employment, she reaffirmed the Department's commitment to youth empowerment, noting a slight increase in targets as reflected in the APP on slide 34. Youth recruitment is necessary due to an ageing workforce. On concerns about incomplete programmes, these will be assessed at the end of the financial year rather than quarterly, as part of its standard review cycle.

Rehabilitation and reintegration are interconnected processes, with rehabilitation forming the foundation for successful reintegration into communities. She highlighted the role of community-based programmes and aftercare services, noting that some successful reintegration cases have been publicly shared. Of education and training outcomes, DCS stated that university-level education is difficult to include as a performance target, as it depends on individual funding by offenders and is therefore not suitable as a formal Key Performance Indicator (KPI).

On overcrowding, the Deputy Minister acknowledged that current bed capacity is insufficient. While efforts are ongoing to address overcrowding in collaboration with the justice sector, alternative measures have not yet yielded the desired results. Despite this, DCS remains committed to addressing the issue. Finally, she expressed confidence in its ability to implement the APP and confirmed that progress will be reported through quarterly performance updates, where it will account for implementation progress and outcomes.

Department response

DCS Acting CFO, Mr Lebogang Marumule, confirmed that the APP is aligned with the Medium-Term Development Plan, as reflected in slides 18 and 19. On overcrowding, DCS is engaging with the budget and infrastructure authorities to secure funding for the construction of two additional prisons as part of its long term response to capacity constraints.

Mr Marumule explained that the budget is directly linked to programme implementation, ensuring that APP targets are financially supported and achievable. Risks identified within the APP are referred to a risk committee, which assesses their impact and oversees mitigation measures. The household budget line item relates to individuals exiting the correctional system. Of youth employment, youth targets are reflected in slide five and are further incorporated under designated groups in slide six. On the reprioritisation of R2.8 billion, these funds were redirected to manage PPP obligations. On the allocation of 59% of the budget to incarceration, this does not imply reduced focus on rehabilitation, as rehabilitation funding is embedded within programme-specific allocations and performance targets.

On project delays, Mr Marumule acknowledged implementation challenges and indicated that it is considering reducing reliance on implementing agencies in favour of directly appointing qualified service providers to improve delivery. On unauthorised, irregular, and fruitless expenditure, it stated that policies and monitoring systems are in place, which have contributed to a reduction in such expenditure. Prevention measures include staff training and accountability mechanisms. In respect of vehicle procurement in KZN, DCS confirmed that approximately 50 vehicles have been procured. On the procurement of “boss chairs,” it indicated that it is reviewing pricing concerns, as current quotations are considered too high.

Ms Anne Molepo, DSC Chief Deputy Commissioner: Incarceration and Corrections, replied about the percentage of offender profile reports decided upon by the Correctional Supervision and Parole Boards (CSPBs). The concern relates primarily to the quality of offender profile reports and the rate of cases being referred for further profiling.

DCS conducted an audit of Case Management Committees (CMCs) to understand the reasons behind the high number of cases requiring further profiling. The findings revealed that a significant number of CMC members had not been adequately trained, largely due to changes in committee composition and limited refresher training. This resulted in inconsistencies in the quality of reports submitted. To address this, DCS implemented a comprehensive training programme for CMCs. The training included all relevant regional stakeholders, such as CSPB chairpersons, social workers, and psychologists, as gaps were also identified in the availability and completion of supporting reports from these professionals. This intervention is expected to reduce the number of cases referred for further profiling in future cycles, including for 2024/25 as report quality should improve following training. In addition, DCS is reviewing the structural arrangements of CMCs, particularly in smaller centres where permanent committees are not always in place. In such cases, officials are required to travel between centres to provide support. Similar capacity constraints were noted for social workers and psychologists, with arrangements made for professionals from other management areas to assist where shortages exist.

On the Correctional Sentence Plan (CSP) effectiveness, Ms Molepo explained that for offenders serving sentences longer than 24 months, a structured case review system is in place. This system monitors implementation of the CSP at three, six and 12 month intervals to assess progress. The chairperson of the CSP oversight process is responsible for ensuring that all identified needs in the sentence plan are addressed. Once interventions have been completed, offenders are reassessed and reports are compiled accordingly. These mechanisms are intended to ensure ongoing monitoring and effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions.

On rehabilitation programme delivery, DCS enters into memorandums of understanding (MOU) with universities and non-government organisations (NGOs) to deliver services. These programmes are subject to quality assurance processes to ensure that only accredited and suitable service providers are engaged. However, funding constraints were identified as a challenge, particularly in sustaining health-related partnerships such as TB and HIV programmes. On spiritual care services, she acknowledged that there was previously double counting of offenders, which led to inflated figures. This has since been corrected, and offenders will now be counted once per individual participation, while distinguishing between individual and group interventions.

Psychological service targets are set based on available resources and capacity and therefore cannot exceed current service delivery constraints. Healthcare targets, particularly the 100% target for diagnosis and treatment initiation must remain at 100%, as diagnosed inmates cannot be left untreated. It therefore considers full coverage in this area a non-negotiable service requirement.

Mr Evans Maponya, DCS Chief Deputy Commissioner: Government Information Technology Office (GITO), explained that implementation of the IIMS was initially measured by the number of sites where installation had taken place, which proved to be a slow process. As a result, it has now adopted a more centralised implementation approach, which is being progressed through a settlement agreement between DCS and the service provider to enable faster rollout. On cybersecurity risk management, 12 cyber security initiatives have been implemented. These include the deployment of an anti-ransomware solution aimed at strengthening DCS cyber resilience. On capacity building in IT skills, DCS as procured enterprise software licences to allow staff to access and complete training courses at their convenience, supporting continuous professional development. He confirmed that commitments made in the previous year on Oracle licensing have been fulfilled. In addition, e- recruitment services have been procured, and a system for electronic monitoring has been sent to SITA to be advertised.

Ms Cynthia Ramulifho, DCS Chief Deputy Commissioner: Remand Detention, referred to the number of elderly detainees, including about 371 individuals over the age of 65 who remain in custody while awaiting trial. In some cases, recommendations are made for their release based on health assessments conducted by medical professionals. However, these recommendations are not always accepted by the courts, limiting the impact on reducing overcrowding. DCS continues to implement various strategies to manage overcrowding. These include the classification and reclassification of inmates, as well as the allocation of accommodation based on available bed space and facility capacity. Overcrowding remains particularly severe among remand detainees, who form a large portion of the inmate population. To address this, legal mechanisms such as Sections 49G and 63 of the Criminal Procedure Act are being used to refer cases back to court for review. These measures aim to facilitate the release of detainees, especially those with low bail amounts such as below R1000 to ease pressure on correctional facilities. Dedicated committees at national and provincial levels are working to improve efficiency in this process and ensure regular monitoring and reporting. Interdepartmental collaboration plays a critical role in addressing overcrowding. DCS is working closely with departments such as the Department of Health which has been providing treatment at facilities for state patients.

Overcrowding would be significantly reduced if unsentenced remand detainees were excluded from the correctional system; however, alternative holding facilities are not currently available. The establishment of new correctional facilities, alongside the application of provisions such as Section 62 of the CPA, may help alleviate overcrowding over time.

On children in custody, there are currently 44 sentenced child offenders and 84 child remand detainees and Ms Ramulifho indicated that cooperation with the Department of Social Development is contributing to managing these numbers. She noted ongoing engagement with the Department of Home Affairs to address foreign nationals awaiting deportation in correctional facilities, with the aim of reducing pressure on prison capacity.

Mr Gustav Wilson, DCS Chief Deputy Commissioner: Community Corrections, acknowledged concerns on short term job opportunities for offenders, noting that this remains a challenge within the correctional system. The Social Reintegration Programme is being used to monitor and improve performance across different regions, with an increased focus on strengthening collaboration with stakeholders such as non profit organisations (NPOs) and higher education institutions. This shift is aimed at developing evidence-based interventions and improving community acceptance to support reduced re-offending. DCS is moving towards more collaborative and data driven strategies, including partnerships intended to strengthen reintegration outcomes and long-term offender support.

He noted that the legislative changes in the Correctional Services Amendment Act have strengthened a victim-centred approach, ensuring that victims are more meaningfully included in restorative processes alongside parolees. In support of this, Community Correctional Forums are being established to enhance collaboration between DCS and communities. On community initiatives, he emphasised the importance of assessing costs and ensuring that such programmes are not merely symbolic but deliver measurable impact in rehabilitation and reintegration outcomes.

On skills development, DCS has entered into MOUs with private sector partners to provide training opportunities for probationers and community members with the aim of supporting both rehabilitation and victim empowerment. There is support for the use of innovative monitoring approaches, including the potential use of technology such as drones, to improve oversight and operational efficiency.

Mr Wilson agreed that the APP implementation should not operate in silos, but rather through improved coordination and communication across programmes and departments. He highlighted efforts to expand private sector and community partnerships to create more sustainable, long term economic opportunities for offenders. Although some DCS performance targets have been reduced based on the outcome assessment by the Auditor General, this does not imply reduced ambition as DCS will continue to work to even overachieve. Finally, he clarified that the figure of 650 refers to the number of economic opportunities to be explored, rather than the number of offenders directly benefiting. Multiple individuals may benefit from the set 650 target.

DCS Deputy Commissioner: Human Resource Development, Ms Linda Bond, spoke to the delay in the filling of vacant posts, explaining that it is due to the high volume of applications they receive combined with a largely manual recruitment system. To address this, it confirmed that an e-recruitment system will be implemented in the 2026/27 financial year, which is expected to improve recruitment efficiency in the short to medium term. It was also noted that DCS has adopted a project management approach by conducting weekly meetings with regional and management area HR functionary to ensure tracking of critical human resource processes, including large scale recruitment and staffing projects.

Of transformation targets, DCS is required to meet employment equity objectives. The employment of youth is considered essential due to an ageing workforce. On the decline in youth employment, this was largely due to the absence of a learnership intake in 2024/25, which had a knock-on effect in subsequent reporting periods. However, for 2026/27, learnership programmes have been reintroduced, which is expected to support the appointment of entry-level officials and assist in achieving the target of 2,500 entry level correctional officials.

Ms Bond dispelled concerns that the safety of DCS officials is not looked after. It does indeed monitor the safety and wellbeing of officials through the Risk Management Committee, which tracks both physical injuries on duty and emotional wellbeing. She also confirmed that efforts are ongoing to fill vacant posts, and that funding for leadership and recruitment priorities will be secured as they are part of the APP implementation.

Dr Zodwa Mosoma, DCS Acting Deputy Commissioner: Incarcerations and Corrections, stated that, due to financial constraints, DCS has prioritised five projects including the upgrade of staff living conditions in Mthatha, Eastern Cape, the upgrade of Burgersdorp in the Eastern Cape which will provide 500 bed spaces, upgrade of the Groenpunt bakery, St Albans security fence and repairing and maintaining eight sites under integrated security systems. On unnatural deaths, the National Commissioner appointed a team to investigate the causes of unnatural deaths. The findings indicated that that suicide remains the leading category. This is linked to staff shortages as there needs to be regular and close monitoring of those inmates.

On contraband control, every Monday senior managers or executive management have to focus on operations and security and part of that is the monitoring of contraband. Other interventions include searches and unannounced searches to detect prohibited items. Officials found to be involved in bringing contraband into facilities are subjected to formal investigations, disciplinary processes and criminal charges where required. In addition, integrated security systems which include cameras are other interventions implemented to strengthen efforts to curb contraband smuggling.

Mr Joseph Katenga, DCS Chief Deputy Commissioner: Strategic Management, stated that the APP is primarily an output focused planning tool rather than an outcome or impact focused framework. It mainly measures short-term outputs linked to activities implemented within the financial year, rather than assessing if these activities lead to long-term outcomes or broader impacts. While there is reference to impact statements and longer-term objectives over a five-year period, these are part of the broader logic framework. As a result, the APP does not adequately show if department actions are producing sustained improvements or meaningful change but rather focuses on if the planned activities have been completed.

Mr Marumule clarified that the funding of rehabilitation programmes are specifically toward production workshops and educational programmes for offenders, which form part of its rehabilitation and skills development interventions.

Also noted was that there is a Social Reintegration Officer function in place to provide assistance to individuals who have exited incarceration. This role has the primary responsibility of engaging the private sector and other stakeholders to facilitate the creation of employment opportunities for former offenders, thereby supporting their reintegration into society. The Minister further affirmed this comment.

Committee Recommendations

The Chairperson expressed general satisfaction with several areas of DCS performance, particularly the employment equity plans, noting progress toward achieving a 50% female representation target. It was recommended that this should not plateau but rather the target even increase towards 60% representation.

The Committee welcomed progress in the procurement of vehicles in KZN, while emphasising the importance of ensuring that commitments made by DCS are fully implemented. Positive acknowledgement was also given to the increase in rehabilitation targets and the rise in youth employment targets from 40% to 42% in 2026/27.

However, concerns were raised on the decline in participation of parolees and probationers in community initiatives. The Committee stressed that this should not reflect a regression in targets and it urged DCS to re-evaluate its approach to ensure sustained or improved participation levels, particularly given the importance of reintegration outcomes. The Committee also questioned the consistency of performance in preventing unnatural deaths and injuries in custody, noting that while progress has been made in reducing injuries, similar progress is not evident in reducing unnatural deaths.

Concerns were also raised about the lack of specificity in infrastructure reporting and the Committee requested clearer identification of projects to enable effective oversight. Members also raised a concern that low target setting may create the perception of over performance and requested clarity if current targets genuinely reflect desired impact or merely baseline performance levels. A report was requested on the intended level of impact embedded in current target setting practices.

Of the Zonderwater incident, the Committee requested a report on disciplinary and consequence management actions taken against an official who allegedly provided misleading information during the Committee oversight visit.

The Department confirmed that internal investigations and consequences had been initiated, and the Minister requested that a formal report be submitted to him.

The Chairperson indicated that follow up would be conducted to ensure the report is received. He noted that the details of the oversight visit programme scheduled for 28 April to 1 May will be communicated to Members.

The Chairperson indicated that these points constituted the Committee’s recommendations and requests arising from the 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan (APP) deliberations. The meeting was thereafter concluded.
