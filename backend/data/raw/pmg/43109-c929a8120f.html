Department of Mineral Resources and Petroleum Annual Performance Plan 2026/27 (with Minister)

NCOP Agriculture, Land Reform and Mineral Resources

26 May 2026
Chairperson: Mr M Modise (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

Annual Performance Plans (APPs) of Government Departments and Entities

The Select Committee met to consider the Department of Mineral Resources and Petroleum’s (DMRP’s) annual performance plan (APP), budget vote matters, reports arising from previous meetings, and outstanding Committee business.

The Department said the mining sector faced several challenges, including declining production levels and insufficient greenfield exploration. These challenges were compounded by constraints outside the mining sector itself, particularly energy and coordination constraints. Although electricity availability had improved significantly, the high cost of electricity remained a critical concern because South Africa’s deep-level mines required extensive energy use for cooling and ventilation systems. This placed pressure on the commercial viability of many operations. Despite the challenges, the sector presented substantial opportunities, particularly because of rising global demand for critical minerals. South Africa possesses significant deposits of critical minerals, including approximately 95% of the world's platinum group metals, 75% of chrome reserves, and 30% of manganese reserves. These minerals were increasingly important globally, and South Africa therefore had an opportunity to expand beneficiation and grow the economy through value addition.

The Department described petroleum as a critical enabler of industrialisation and economic activity, supporting transport, mining, agriculture, and coordination, and was therefore vital to economic growth. It also generated substantial government revenue through fuel levies collected by the National Treasury. However, it also faced major challenges, particularly South Africa’s heavy reliance on imported crude oil and refined petroleum products. Recent geopolitical tensions involving Iran, Israel and the United States, demonstrated the country’s vulnerability to fuel shortages and price shocks. The Department was also concerned about ageing infrastructure and the closure of several refineries over time, which had further increased the country’s dependence on imported refined products. Nevertheless, it highlighted opportunities within the petroleum sector, particularly oil and gas exploration potential, referring to recent discoveries in the Orange Basin on the Namibian side, and indicating there was a strong possibility that similar reserves existed within South African waters.

The Committee also reflected on issues arising from the community engagement concerning mining-related disputes in the North West Province, particularly matters relating to relocation, mining operations, and community grievances associated with the Sogima issue – a black-owned mine whose blasting rights had been revoked in 2018 shortly after obtaining mining rights. The Chairperson noted that officials were already on site, and indicated that the Committee would facilitate further engagements involving the DMPR, the Department of Human Settlements, affected communities and relevant stakeholders to obtain clarity on the differing accounts presented by community representatives, the mining company, and government officials. The Minister outlined the Department’s stance regarding compliance and permit revocations in the mining sector, stressing that enforcement actions were not based on race or discrimination, but rather on whether companies complied with legal and regulatory requirements. He emphasised that any company or individual found to violate the law would be dealt with accordingly, irrespective of race or ownership profile. He provided examples related to blasting regulations and mining compliance requirements.

The Chairperson raised concerns regarding late invitations extended to Committee Members for departmental events, and requested that future invitations be circulated well in advance to allow Members sufficient time to accommodate such engagements in their schedules. The Minister apologised for the late invitations, and said that additional engagements with the Committee were forthcoming, and undertook to improve coordination with Members in the future.

Following the departure of the Minister and departmental delegation, the Committee proceeded with internal business, including:

  • The consideration and adoption of previous meeting minutes;
  • The consideration and adoption of the Budget Vote Report of the Department of Agriculture; and
  • The consideration and adoption of the Rural Development Budget Vote Report.

The reports were adopted without amendment.

Meeting report

Introductory remarks

The Chairperson said the meeting agenda comprised a briefing by the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources (DMPR) on its annual performance plan (APP) and Budget Vote 34, which translated its five-year strategic framework into actionable short-term goals for the current budget year. The Committee would hear about the Department’s strategic alignment, performance tracking mechanisms, transparency and accountability measures, and budget integration plans for the 2026/27 budget year. Members would also hear about the political leadership that would be provided by the Ministry to ensure that the Department achieved the targets set for the budget year.

He welcomed the secondment of Ms L Mhlongo (MKP, KwaZulu-Natal) to the Committee, replacing Ms S Mokoena-Zondi, who had since been elected as Chief Whip of the Mkhonto weSizwe Party in the National Assembly. He said the Committee trusted that she would actively participate in oversight matters relating to all five departments overseen by the Committee, in the same manner as her predecessor.

The Chairperson then invited Minister Gwede Mantashe to lead the DMPR delegation and make introductory remarks.

Minister’s introductory comments

Minister Mantashe introduced the departmental delegation accompanying him, including the Deputy Minister, the Director-General (DG), and executive directors from various branches within the Department.

Before handing over to the DG, Minister Mantashe emphasised that the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources should not be viewed merely as a regulatory department, but rather as an economic department. He said the Department’s vision and mission focused on economic growth, job creation, and inclusive economic development. He stressed that although the Department was often perceived primarily as a regulator, its mandate extended significantly beyond regulation, and included a direct developmental and economic role.

He said the DG would provide a comprehensive account of the Department’s work and institutional structure. He explained that the Department operated through several regional and divisional structures, and oversaw several entities and enterprises that were performing crucial functions within the sector.

He said the Department’s economic performance was above average, adding that before the most recent audit cycle, the DMPR and its entities had achieved twelve unqualified audits. However, one entity, African Exploration Mining and Finance Corporation (AEMFC), had received a disclaimer audit opinion. The Department was not satisfied with that outcome and would work to address the matter.

The Minister then formally handed over to the DG to lead the Department’s presentation.

DMPR on its 2026/27 APP and Budget Vote 84

Mr Jacob Mbele, Director-General, DMPR, said the Department now operated through four programmes following the split of the former Department of Mineral Resources and Energy (DMRE).

Programme One dealt with administration, and included corporate services and economic management. Mr Mbele said the position of Deputy Director-General (DDG) for Corporate Services had become vacant after the incumbent had moved to one of the entities, and the Department was at an advanced stage of finalising the appointment of a replacement candidate, together with the Minister.

Programme Two focused on mineral and petroleum regulation. Compliance monitoring and enforcement functions were located under this programme because, after the restructuring and separation of the former department, there were DDGs who had become additional to the approved establishment. Since the Department had not yet finalised a fit-for-purpose structure, some compliance and enforcement responsibilities were allocated to those DDGs.

Programme Three focused on mine health and safety, while Programme Four dealt with mining, minerals, and petroleum policy development.

Mr Mbele said the Department was in the process of reviewing and redesigning its organisational structure. The work was already at an advanced stage, and the Department hoped to implement a revised structure soon that would enable it to respond more effectively to its mandate.

He reiterated the Minister’s earlier remarks that the Department was fundamentally economic and not merely a regulatory authority. Its vision and mission focused on economic growth, job creation, and inclusive economic development. He added that the Department worked closely with its entities, which supported it in implementing and delivering on its mandate across both the mining and petroleum sectors.

Providing context to the APP, Mr Mbele emphasised that it had not been developed in isolation. It was informed by the sectors within which the Department operated, as well as its five-year strategic plan developed at the beginning of the Seventh Administration. That strategy was aligned with the government’s medium-term development plan (MTDP).

He said the mining sector remained a major contributor to the South African economy despite its reduced contribution to the gross domestic product (GDP) over time. He explained that the decline in mining’s proportional GDP contribution was partly due to growth in other sectors of the economy. Nonetheless, the mining sector remained a significant source of state revenue through taxes and royalties, and continued to be a major employer, supporting almost half a million direct jobs. He added that the figure excluded indirect employment, which would make the total number significantly higher.

Mr Mbele said the mining sector faced several challenges, including declining production levels and insufficient greenfield exploration. These challenges were compounded by constraints outside the mining sector itself, particularly energy and coordination constraints. Although electricity availability had improved significantly, the high cost of electricity remained a critical concern because South Africa’s deep-level mines required extensive energy use for cooling and ventilation systems. This placed pressure on the commercial viability of many operations.

Despite the challenges, the sector presented substantial opportunities, particularly because of rising global demand for critical minerals. South Africa possesses significant deposits of critical minerals, including approximately 95% of the world's platinum group metals (PGMs), 75% of chrome reserves, and 30% of manganese reserves. These minerals were increasingly important globally, and South Africa therefore had an opportunity to expand beneficiation and grow the economy through value addition. The Department’s objective was to position the country to take advantage of these strategic opportunities.

Turning to the petroleum sector, Mr Mbele described petroleum as a critical enabler of industrialisation and economic activity. The sector supported transport, mining, agriculture, and coordination, and was therefore vital to economic growth. It also generated substantial government revenue through fuel levies collected by National Treasury.

However, the petroleum sector also faced major challenges, particularly South Africa’s heavy reliance on imported crude oil and refined petroleum products. Recent geopolitical tensions involving Iran, Israel and the United States, demonstrated the country’s vulnerability to fuel shortages and price shocks. The Department was also concerned about ageing infrastructure and the closure of several refineries over time, which had further increased dependence on imported refined products.

Nevertheless, Mr Mbele highlighted opportunities within the petroleum sector, particularly oil and gas exploration potential. He referred to recent discoveries in the Orange Basin on the Namibian side, and said there was a strong possibility that similar reserves existed within South African waters. The Department believed the country should exploit these opportunities.

He then outlined the broader strategic context informing the APP. The DMPR’s planning process considered political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. Politically, the Department had to respond to geopolitical tensions and their implications for the mining and petroleum sectors. Economically, it recognised that South Africa was a price-taker in global commodity markets, and therefore vulnerable to volatile commodity prices. Socially, the Department understood that mining and petroleum were major employment sectors and therefore had to be managed in a way that promoted job creation and minimised job losses.

Technological developments were also important, particularly in mine health and safety, where modern technologies made it possible to mine in areas previously regarded as unsafe. Environmental sustainability remained a key priority, while all departmental actions had to comply with the legal framework governing the sectors.

Mr Mbele said the Department had reviewed its five-year strategy at the end of the previous budget year because the operating environment was constantly changing. The review had identified five key strategic priorities requiring continued focus. These were promoting investment in the mining and petroleum sectors, accelerating transformation, ensuring environmental sustainability, promoting regional integration, and strengthening institutional capacity and governance. The APP targets had been developed around these priorities.

Under the priority of promoting investment in the mining and petroleum sectors, the Department had developed 12 targets aimed at creating a coherent and enabling policy environment, promoting investment in petroleum infrastructure, supporting beneficiation, and streamlining licensing processes.

Under an accelerating transformation, the Department had established 18 targets focused on driving transformation within the sectors. Three targets related to environmental sustainability, while targets linked to regional integration focused on servicing international agreements, bilateral arrangements, and forums such as Bi-National Commissions (BNCs). These targets would be monitored through annual operational plans rather than directly in the APP. Eight targets under institutional capacity and governance aimed to build a capable, high-performing, and digitally enabled organisation.

Mr Mbele then outlined specific APP commitments.

Under policy and framework development, the Department committed to submitting the revised Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act (MPRDA) Bill to Parliament during the current budget year. He reminded Members that the Department had already conducted public consultations and continued engaging business, communities and traditional leaders, on the proposed amendments.

The Department also planned to finalise amendments to the Diamonds Amendment Bill, while another major policy commitment involved developing a strategic stock policy for the petroleum sector. The work was already at the Cabinet process stage, and would provide safeguards for national energy security by ensuring sufficient fuel reserves during supply disruptions.

On infrastructure investment, he said the Department committed to finalising the review of the fuel price formula within the budget year. It would also continue conducting independent audits to ensure transparency and reliability in the fuel pricing system. It would also develop a Petroleum Sector Development Plan to guide infrastructure investment and market access in the sector up to 2040.

Regarding industrialisation and beneficiation, Mr Mbele said the Department would continue implementing the Junior Mining Exploration Fund, and monitor funded projects to ensure progress. It would also oversee implementation of the critical minerals strategy approved by Cabinet, and provide quarterly reports on progress against its pillars.

The Department was also committed to quarterly reporting on research into shale gas development being conducted by the Petroleum Agency South Africa (PASA), including work on the regulations required to lift the moratorium on shale gas drilling applications.

On streamlining mining licensing processes, the Department was committed to rolling out the new online cadastral licensing system across all provinces by the end of the budget year. Implementation in the Western Cape had progressed well, and that the Department had now begun moving into other provinces.

The DMPR would also process at least 2 500 prospecting rights, mining rights, and mining permit applications during the year. He stressed that this was a minimum target rather than a maximum. Similarly, the Department undertook to process at least 50 petroleum applications.

On socio-economic development, the Department would continue its financial and technical support for artisanal and small-scale mining. It was also committed to implementing gender-responsive budgeting as part of government-wide initiatives led by the Department of Women, with the aim of ensuring that procurement processes intentionally support gender transformation.

Mr Mbele outlined the Department’s compliance and enforcement work, which included monitoring social and labour plans, conducting legal compliance inspections, mine economic inspections, environmental inspections, and petroleum inspections. He said that the targets represented minimum commitments based on historical performance and available resources.

Regarding my health and safety, the Department had established several targets aimed at advancing its goal of achieving zero harm in mines.

On environmental sustainability, the Department planned to rehabilitate three asbestos mines and continue to seal and secure unsafe mine shafts. It would also continue collaborating with the Council for Geoscience (CGS) and Mintek on programmes addressing acid mine drainage (AMD).

He said the remaining APP targets focused on improving internal controls, strengthening governance, addressing corruption and ethics issues, improving operational efficiency, and ensuring effective oversight of state-owned entities.

He then handed over to the Chief Financial Officer, Ms Yvonne Chetty, to present the Department’s budget allocation.

DMPR’s budget

Ms Yvone Chetty, Chief Financial Officer (CFO), DMPR, said the Department’s budget had been developed according to National Treasury guidelines. Departments were required to fund new priorities from existing baselines through reprioritisation, while compensation of employees (CoE) had to remain within the approved expenditure ceiling. She added that departments had also been encouraged to reduce salary costs, and had therefore implemented early retirement measures. All employment decisions also had to consider affordability over the medium term.

She reported that the Department’s total budget allocation for the 2026/27 budget year amounted to R2.856 billion.

  • Programme One: Administration, received R630 million;
  • Programme Two: Mineral and Petroleum Regulation, received R799.1 million;
  • Programme Three: Mine Health and Safety Inspectorate, received R276.6 million; and
  • Programme Four: Mining, Minerals and Petroleum Policy Development, received R1.150 billion, which was the largest allocation.

The budget was primarily divided into transfers and subsidies amounting to R1.212 billion, with CoE amounting to R947.5 million, and goods and services amounting to R679 million. The outer-year budgets for 2027/28 and 2028/29 remained indicative at this stage.

Ms Chetty noted that the Department’s budget had decreased slightly, from R2.860 billion in the previous budget year to R2.856 billion in 2026/27, representing a reduction of R3.7 million. CoE had increased by R41.9 million, while goods and services had increased by R19.4 million. However, these increases were offset by a reduction of R65.5 million in transfers and subsidies.

She explained that Programme Two’s budget included transfers to the South African Diamond and Precious Metals Regulator (SADPMR), PASA, international membership fees, and support for small-scale miners. Programme Four, which accounted for 40% of the budget, included transfers to Mintek and the Council for Geoscience.

The cadastral licensing system received an allocation of R16 million for the 2026/27 budget year. During the previous budget year, expenditure on the project amounted to R20.13 million.

Ms Chetty detailed operational funding under goods and services, which included accommodation leases, travel costs, and departmental events. Specific earmarked allocations included shale gas projects, mine rehabilitation research projects, AMD work, rehabilitation of mines through Mintek, and derelict and ownerless mines projects.

Transfer payments included allocations to entities such as the Mining Qualifications Authority (MQA), the SADPMR, the Mine Health and Safety Council, the Council for Geoscience, Mintek, PASA, and the Industrial Development Corporation (IDC). The IDC received funding for small-scale mining projects and the Junior Miners Exploration Fund. PASA also received operational funding, while international membership fees were paid to organisations such as the African Petroleum Producers Association and the African Diamond Producers Association.

Regarding staffing, she reported that the Department had 1 095 filled posts, 48 vacancies, and 11 early retirements at the time the budget was prepared. The total approved establishment stood at 1 154 posts. Although the Department faced a shortfall of R56 million in compensation funding, the gap would be managed through vacancy-related savings. She added that the ongoing organisational redesign had not yet been costed into the current budget.

Minister's comment

Minister Mantashe expressed concern that the state was not investing sufficiently in productive sectors of the economy. He said government continued spending heavily on social expenditure without adequately ensuring sustainable revenue generation. He argued that the Department’s limited budget allocation reflected this broader policy challenge. The Department continued raising the issue, but the situation persisted.

Discussion

Mr N Pienaar (DA, Limpopo) opened the discussion by identifying what he regarded as two significant issues affecting the sector. He argued that South Africa’s increasing dependence on imported fuel products was a consequence of decisions taken by the government. He said that in 2019, South Africa had produced approximately 70% of its own diesel requirements, but that this had declined to around 30% at present. This decline had followed the 2020 decision requiring local refineries to comply with European Union (EU) fuel standards, which significantly increased refinery upgrade costs. He asserted that the financial burden had forced many refineries to cease refining operations and instead function as storage facilities. He further contended that the government had failed to provide guarantees or support mechanisms to assist refineries with the transition. He therefore maintained that South Africa’s vulnerability to fuel imports and global instability was self-created through departmental policy decisions.

Mr Pienaar also raised concerns regarding the proposed Mineral Resources Development Bill, stating that it had not been identified by the Department as one of its key challenges, despite the widespread criticism directed at the Bill by industry stakeholders. He noted that the Minerals Council of South Africa, which represented approximately 90% of mining producers and contributed significantly to GDP, had warned that the Bill could stifle investment and economic growth. He argued that the government repeatedly introduced policies that harmed the sector, and then later expressed concern about unemployment and economic decline resulting from those same interventions.

Mr M Montwedi (EFF, North West) raised several issues relating to transformation, mining regulation, licensing backlogs, exploration investment, environmental rehabilitation, and fuel policy. He referred to an oversight visit conducted on 23 March to Sogima Mining near Brits in Madibeng, describing it as a 100% black-owned mining company, whose blasting rights had been revoked in 2018 shortly after obtaining mining rights. He questioned why neighbouring white-owned mines had allegedly continued operations without similar action being taken against them, and asked whether the Department was effectively undermining black-owned mines. He requested that a full report be provided to the Committee on the matter.

Mr Montwedi questioned how the Department intended to ensure that mining companies fulfilled their social and labour plan (SLP) commitments, particularly after reviewing reports indicating that some mining houses had failed to implement obligations dating back to 2013. He argued that South Africa’s share of global exploration spending remained below 1%, while exploration investment had declined further, and questioned why the Department’s interventions appeared limited to the Junior Mining Exploration Fund instead of a broader exploration strategy.

He also raised concerns about the extent of the mining licensing backlog, stating that while the Department targeted the finalisation of 2 470 applications, it had not disclosed the total number of outstanding applications or their age profile. On derelict and ownerless mines, he criticised the Department’s target of rehabilitating only three sites annually, despite the existence of thousands of abandoned mines that continued to attract illegal mining activity by zama-zamas.

Mr Montwedi questioned whether the Department possessed adequate personnel and financial resources to meet its target of conducting 8 000 inspections, given that staffing and budget shortages had been identified repeatedly as major constraints. He also queried the proposed review of the fuel price formula, asking what measurable reductions in fuel prices were expected, and why those outcomes had not been quantified in the APP.

Regarding strategic fuel reserves, he argued that the Committee required policy-level answers rather than implementation responses from the Central Energy Fund (CEF). He asked what policy interventions the Department intended to introduce regarding strategic petroleum reserves, particularly considering the unresolved matter concerning the illegal sale of South Africa’s previous strategic oil stock. He also questioned how a new strategic petroleum stock policy could be approved before accountability for the earlier losses had been addressed.

Finally, he raised concerns about gender-responsive planning, noting that while reports were envisaged, no enforcement mechanisms or sanctions against non-compliant mining companies had been outlined.

Mr H van den Berg (FF+, Northern Cape) raised concerns about the security and resilience of the Department’s digital systems. Referring to recent reports about vulnerabilities within police information systems, he questioned whether the Department could face similar risks in relation to its own information management infrastructure.

He sought clarity on the status of the Mineral Resources Development Bill, and asked how the Department intended to address concerns from industry stakeholders regarding regulatory uncertainty, administrative inefficiencies, and investment deterrents. He further questioned whether the Department intended to accelerate offshore oil and gas exploration, despite concerns regarding ownership structures and foreign participation. He also requested further information regarding alternative shale gas extraction methods previously referenced by the Minister, particularly methods that allegedly avoided environmental and groundwater damage.

Lastly, he requested more detailed information regarding the Department’s information technology (IT) targets and transformation plans.

Mr B Farmer (PA, Western Cape) focused primarily on the effectiveness of the Department’s performance indicators and governance systems. He argued that many of the APP indicators relied heavily on technically auditable and administratively measurable targets, rather than demonstrating actual developmental impact. He questioned whether these indicators adequately reflected improvements in sector regulation, mining community development, transformation outcomes, investment certainty, or reductions in illegal mining.

He warned that the APP risked becoming administratively active without being strategically effective. Perceptions among mining communities, investors, and industry stakeholders indicated low confidence in the Department’s effectiveness. Referring to the 2025 Fraser Institute Annual Survey of Mining Companies, he noted that South Africa ranked poorly in terms of investment attractiveness and policy perception compared with other mining districts. He therefore asked what measures the Department intended to implement to improve investor confidence and overall competitiveness.

He argued that despite extensive reporting and monitoring systems, there remained insufficient evidence demonstrating improvements in regulatory effectiveness, environmental governance, transformation, or sustainable development outcomes. He warned that APP targets could technically be achieved while broader governance failures and weak sector confidence persisted.

Ms S Boshoff (DA, Mpumalanga) asked whether the Department had assessed the impact of rising fuel prices and unemployment on mining operations and employment levels. She requested that such a report be provided to the Committee, if it existed.

She questioned the effectiveness of the Junior Mining Exploration Fund, asking how many projects supported through the fund had progressed into viable mining activities, job creation, or new mineral discoveries. She requested clarity regarding the amount allocated to the fund, how much had been disbursed, and what oversight mechanisms existed to ensure accountability and prevent politically connected individuals from disproportionately benefiting.

She also requested a detailed report on the mining licensing backlog, including the number of outstanding applications and projected timelines for finalisation. Regarding mine health and safety oversight, she questioned how much of the Department’s allocation was directed specifically towards inspections and enforcement, and whether the inspectorate possessed sufficient capacity to perform effective oversight.

Ms S Sithole (ANC, North West) questioned whether the Department’s eight stated objectives under Priority One were realistically achievable. She requested details regarding the Department’s monitoring and evaluation tools used to assess implementation and progress.

She also questioned whether the Department could still describe itself as a global leader in the mining and petrochemical sectors, considering global geopolitical instability, including conflicts involving the United States, Iran and Ukraine. She asked whether the Department had considered revising its vision, mission, and impact statements in response to changing global conditions, and whether it remained confident that it was making the desired impact.

Mr P Mabilo (ANC, Northern Cape) began by commending the Minister for what he described as a direct leadership approach, particularly during the recent disaster at the Ikapa Mine in Kimberley, where workers had lost their lives. He praised the Minister’s engagement with affected communities and workers.

He then raised concerns regarding the global demand for critical minerals, and South Africa’s positioning within that environment. He questioned the extent to which non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and environmental groups had delayed shale gas exploration in the Karoo through litigation and advocacy campaigns. He argued that such interventions had prevented South Africa from fully exploring the economic and employment potential of shale gas development. He asked how the government intended to balance environmental sustainability with economic development and industrial growth, while also responding to what he described as opportunistic tendencies by foreign-funded NGOs. Referring to developments in neighbouring Namibia, he argued that South Africa risked falling behind in exploration activities.

He questioned how the Department intended to convert the challenges facing ageing and underutilised refineries into investment opportunities. He also raised concerns regarding implementation timelines associated with clean fuels and energy transition commitments linked to international agreements such as the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement.

On beneficiation, he argued that the government needed to move beyond policy discussions and begin implementing beneficiation projects in mineral-rich provinces such as the Northern Cape. He linked beneficiation directly to industrialisation and economic development objectives. He also referred to the Kimberley Process Certification Scheme, and questioned how the government intended to strengthen systems to combat illicit mineral trade and illegal mining activities. He asked whether lessons from the Kimberley Process could be applied to other minerals which were increasingly being targeted by illegal mining syndicates.

He concluded by raising concerns regarding inclusivity and transformation within the sector, particularly regarding opportunities for persons living with disabilities. He questioned how historically disadvantaged individuals, women, youth, and persons with disabilities were benefiting from the procurement of goods and services opportunities within the mining sector.

The Chairperson addressed concerns raised regarding Sogima Mining and the Libode Stone Quarry Mine, noting that the Committee would hold dedicated engagements on those matters after finalising reports arising from oversight visits and stakeholder meetings involving local government representatives and affected communities. He informed the Minister and Department that future meetings would ensure that the DMPR had an opportunity to respond directly to concerns raised about its conduct and decisions.

DMPR’s response

Mr Tseliso Maqubela, Deputy Director-General (DDG): Mineral and Petroleum Regulation, responded to concerns regarding the closure of refineries in Durban, clarifying that the closures were not linked to the implementation of Clean Fuels 2 policy requirements. He explained that the first refinery to close was the Engen refinery, where shareholder Petronas had already decided to divest from the facility, before the refinery suffered a catastrophic fire that destroyed much of its crude oil processing capability. The closure of the BP and Shell-owned SAPREF refinery formed part of a broader global restructuring strategy by the two companies, noting that Shell had also closed refineries in countries such as New Zealand and Australia as part of portfolio rationalisation. He stressed that the decisions were commercial shareholder decisions rather than policy-induced closures.

Mr Maqubela said that other South African refineries, including the Astron refinery in Cape Town and the Sasolburg refinery, were currently being upgraded to comply with the 2027 clean fuels regulations. He indicated that the government, through the Minister, had resolved that the SAPREF refinery be acquired by the CEF, after which refurbishment processes would commence. However, he noted that the refinery had also suffered significant flood damage during the KwaZulu-Natal floods, meaning major rehabilitation work would be required before operations could resume.

Mr Mbele addressed the concerns regarding policy uncertainty and the review of the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act (MPRDA). He said policy reviews were a necessary and continuous process because the operating environment was constantly changing. The Department recognised the importance of ensuring policy alignment, not only internally but also with other departments such as Environmental Affairs and Water Affairs, to reduce delays in licensing and investment approvals. The Department was therefore reviewing several policies within the current APP to ensure they remained responsive to prevailing conditions and investment requirements.

Responding to issues relating to the Sogima matter and allegations regarding departmental enforcement actions, Mr Mbele said that all prohibitions and directives issued by the Department were grounded in prevailing legislation. Where directives were issued against mining operations, these directives clearly stipulated the legal basis and reasons for the action taken. He added that the law also provided operators with the right to appeal departmental decisions where they were dissatisfied.

Mr David Msiza, Chief Inspector of Mines, supplemented the response by stating that the Department had received numerous complaints from surrounding communities and neighbouring mines regarding blasting activities and associated safety concerns. Communities had raised issues relating to structural damage and public safety caused by blasting operations. He said the Department followed due legal processes in addressing such matters, and had also introduced guidelines to assist the mining sector in safeguarding workers and communities. These guidelines required mining companies to conduct inspections of properties within a 500-metre radius before blasting activities occurred, undertake proper drill-and-blast designs, monitor blasting impacts, conduct risk assessments, and consult affected communities before operations commenced.

DDG Maqubela also responded to questions relating to social and labour plans, stating that while there were occasional compliance challenges, the SLP programme remained one of the Department’s more successful initiatives. Mining companies attempted to comply with their obligations. However, delays often arose due to difficulties such as obtaining investor funding, waiting for sign-off approvals from local government, shifting community priorities regarding projects, and disruptions caused by construction mafia activities. He noted that where non-compliance occurred, the Department issued directives compelling mining companies to rectify deficiencies. It also undertook regular inspections across all regions to monitor compliance with SLP commitments.

On the issue of mining licence backlogs, Mr Maqubela acknowledged that the Department continued to face challenges due to the high volume of applications received annually, which exceeded 3 000 new applications each year. He said the Department was constantly processing applications while simultaneously receiving new ones, making it difficult to eliminate backlogs. He commented that many applications should never enter the system because they were related to areas where rights or permits had already been issued. To address this, the Department had invested in a new mining licensing system that would automatically prevent applications from being submitted over already allocated rights. He undertook to provide the Committee with a more comprehensive report on the age and status of outstanding licences.

Regarding ownerless and derelict mines, Mr Maqubela said that the Department’s ability to rehabilitate abandoned mines was constrained primarily by budget limitations. He suggested that additional budget allocations would enable the Department to rehabilitate significantly more derelict mining sites across the country.

Addressing concerns regarding fuel price reform and rising fuel prices, he said it would be premature to quantify consumer relief measures before the completion of an extensive study currently underway. Crude oil prices remained the largest determinant of fuel prices globally, and international conflicts had significantly disrupted freight and shipping routes, particularly around the Strait of Hormuz. These disruptions had created global vessel shortages and increased freight costs, thereby placing additional pressure on fuel prices.

Mr Maqubela clarified that the government had successfully recovered ownership of all strategic fuel stocks following a court ruling that had declared the previous disposal process unlawful. The Minister had instructed the Department to ensure that ownership of the stocks reverted fully to the state and confirmed that the government retained ownership of all strategic reserves. He assured Members that South Africa’s fuel supply remained secure despite international instability. The Department remained in constant engagement with oil companies to ensure continued fuel availability over the coming months. While certainty regarding future fuel prices could not be guaranteed due to international market conditions, the Department was actively working to ensure continuity of supply.

Chief Inspector Msiza responded to questions regarding inspectorate capacity and targets, and explained that departmental targets were determined according to the budget allocated by the National Treasury. Inspection targets were therefore calibrated to available resources and staffing levels. He stressed that mine health and safety remained a critical priority for the Department. He reported that the previous year had been the safest on record in terms of fatalities, injuries, occupational diseases, HIV, and tuberculosis rates within the mining industry. South Africa now compares favourably with leading mining areas such as Canada and Australia, particularly in the coal and iron ore sectors.

Mr Msiza reiterated that the Department remained committed to achieving its goal of “Zero Harm,” and continued to work closely with organised labour, business, and other stakeholders to improve health and safety outcomes. He also announced that the Department would host the Mine Health and Safety Tripartite Summit later in the year to assess industry performance and identify additional interventions.

Mr Mbele also addressed concerns regarding the scale of government support for the Junior Mining Exploration Fund. He explained that the fund had initially received R400 million, comprising R200 million from the fiscus and a matching R200 million contribution from the Industrial Development Corporation (IDC). Although some Members viewed the intervention as relatively small, it had already functioned as a catalytic mechanism that had attracted additional investment commitments from institutions such as Anglo American and the Public Investment Corporation (PIC). Several private investors and financial institutions were also expressing interest in contributing to the fund.

He clarified that South Africa’s exploration expenditure statistics were often underestimated because available data reflected listed companies only. Many smaller unlisted mining companies were conducting exploration activities that were not adequately captured in national statistics. To address this gap, the Department had initiated a survey requiring licence holders to submit detailed information on exploration spending.

On the status of the MPRDA Amendment Bill, Mr Mbele informed the Committee that the legislation was currently before the State Law Advisors for constitutional scrutiny. Once that process was complete, the Bill would return to Cabinet before eventually being tabled in Parliament for consideration.

Regarding offshore oil and gas exploration, he said the Petroleum Agency South Africa had intensified engagement with exploration companies, leading to an increase in exploration applications. He added that the Minister and PASA had also undertaken community engagement initiatives along coastal regions to raise awareness regarding the economic potential of offshore oil and gas activities. Further, government was exploring the establishment of specialised court arrangements or dedicated judicial processes to expedite appeals and litigation relating to oil and gas developments.

Mr Mbele also responded to questions relating to transformation within the mining sector. He said that transformation was not limited to ownership patterns, but also included ensuring broader participation in mining, improving worker safety, and expanding opportunities for historically disadvantaged individuals through initiatives such as small-scale mining programmes and the Junior Mining Exploration Fund.

Minister’s response

Minister Mantashe reinforced many of the Department’s responses, and stressed the strategic importance of maintaining domestic refining capacity. He said that South Africa’s remaining operational refineries currently supplied approximately 40% of the country’s fuel needs, and warned against relying entirely on private-sector commercial decisions for national energy security. He commented that private companies prioritised profitability, which explained why firms such as Engen, Shell, and BP had exited refining operations. However, he argued that periods of geopolitical instability demonstrated the importance of maintaining domestic refining capability.

Minister Mantashe disputed claims that 90% of the Minerals Council opposed revisions to mining legislation, arguing that such assertions did not reflect the Department’s regular engagements with the sector. He emphasised that many industry stakeholders did not share those views. He said departmental teams were actively working in affected communities such as Mdlankomo regarding quarry-related disputes. He stressed that the Department treated all mining companies equally, regardless of race or ownership. Companies violating the law would face penalties, irrespective of whether they were black- or white-owned entities.

The Minister also clarified that municipalities should focus on integrated development plans (IDPs) rather than attempting to administer social and labour plans, noting that some municipalities incorrectly assumed SLPs replaced municipal development planning responsibilities.

On the Junior Mining Exploration Fund, he said that all eight funded projects were progressing positively. He highlighted projects in areas such as Aggeneys, Giyani and Barberton, where exploration had already identified promising deposits including gold, antimony, and rare earth minerals. He indicated that several projects were moving towards eventual mining development.

Addressing mine safety, Minister Mantashe confirmed that the mining industry had recorded its lowest number of fatalities in history, with 41 fatalities recorded during the previous year, compared with 42 in the preceding year. He noted particularly strong safety performances in the chrome and manganese sectors, although gold and platinum mining remained areas of concern. He also raised concern about the increasing number of road accidents involving mineworkers travelling to and from work, noting that these incidents represented an emerging safety challenge requiring departmental attention.

The Minister concluded by emphasising that matters involving fuel levies, tax measures, and financial interventions required coordination with National Treasury, as such decisions could not be taken independently by the Department. He also cautioned against framing investment discussions solely around foreign investment, arguing that domestic black-owned mining companies were increasingly making substantial contributions to the mining economy, particularly in the coal and manganese sectors.

Chairperson’s remarks

The Chairperson thanked the Minister and departmental delegation for their engagement with the Committee, and urged Members not to treat the engagement as a “do or die” exercise, but rather as the beginning of an ongoing oversight process. He said Members should continuously monitor progress against the plans and targets presented by the Department throughout the budget year, rather than waiting until year-end to assess performance. He encouraged Members to keep track of departmental commitments so that future engagements could focus on progress made on each undertaking.

He also referred to the issues raised concerning Mdlankomo and Sogima, noting that the Committee had now received confirmation that departmental officials were already on site, engaging with the affected communities. He said that the Committee would await the Department’s report and would also engage directly with the communities that had initially approached Parliament with complaints regarding the Department’s alleged failure to respond adequately to concerns raised over an extended period. He committed the Committee to creating a dedicated platform in the North West province where all relevant stakeholders would be brought together to engage comprehensively on the matter.

He said the proposed engagement would include the DMPR, the Department of Human Settlements (DHS), affected communities, ward councillors, portfolio committees, and other relevant stakeholders. He noted that earlier reports received by the Committee during visits to the area had presented differing accounts from those raised by communities, particularly concerning allegations around relocation, occupation of mining areas, and tensions between communities and the mining house. He therefore stressed the importance of allowing the Department and all affected parties an opportunity to present their perspectives fully before conclusions were drawn.

The Chairperson also raised a procedural concern with Minister Mantashe regarding invitations sent to Committee Members for departmental activities. He explained that Members had recently expressed dissatisfaction after receiving invitations at noticeably short notice to a full-day departmental engagement organised by the Ministry. Some Members had wished to attend, but were unable to do so because the invitation was only received either late on the preceding day, or on the morning of the event itself, despite Members already having other parliamentary and constituency commitments. He respectfully requested that the Ministry improve communication and provide invitations well in advance where Committee support or participation was required. He pointed out that other departments, including the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE), routinely issued invitations weeks ahead of events, even where venues and finer details were still subject to confirmation. He stressed that Committee Members wanted to support the Ministry and participate meaningfully in departmental programmes, but adequate notice was necessary to enable proper planning.

Clarifications and responses

Following the Chairperson’s remarks, Mr Farmer requested an opportunity to raise a matter relating to the Sogima issue. He said that while the Chairperson had cautioned Members against engaging too extensively on the matter during the meeting, he wished to request that when the proposed engagement on Sogima was eventually convened, the Department should bring documentary proof or evidence of any alleged legal transgressions committed by the company, and if revocation of permits or enforcement actions had been justified based on breaches of the law.

In response, the Chairperson assured Mr Farmer that the Committee would ensure that all relevant information in its possession was forwarded to the Department ahead of the proposed engagement. He stressed that the Committee did not intend to “catch the Department off guard,” and wanted all parties to be properly prepared to engage constructively and transparently on the matter.

Minister Mantashe then responded by first apologising for the late invitation issued to Committee Members regarding the earlier departmental engagement. He acknowledged that the invitation process had not been overseen appropriately, and assured Members that another engagement involving the Select Committee would be arranged properly in the future. He said the previous event should not have been treated as a substitute for a formal engagement with the Committee, and reiterated his apology for the inconvenience caused.

He also clarified his earlier remarks regarding Sogima and allegations of discrimination. He said that his comments had been made in response to suggestions that Sogima was allegedly being unfairly targeted because it was a black-owned company. The Minister stressed that the Department did not discriminate based on race or ownership when enforcing mining legislation -- enforcement actions were based solely on whether a company complied with applicable laws, regulations, and operational requirements. Using blasting regulations as an example, he said that if any mining company conducted blasting activities within prohibited distances or violated operational safety standards, enforcement action would follow regardless of the company’s racial ownership profile. He emphasised that “it is your blasting that counts,” not the colour or ownership of the company involved.

The Minister concluded by thanking the Chairperson and Committee, before being formally excused from the meeting.

Committee matters

The Chairperson indicated that invitations for future engagements with the Department would be circulated approximately three weeks in advance to allow sufficient time for the Department to prepare its presentations, and for Members to make the necessary arrangements to attend.

The Committee then proceeded to consider the minutes of the previous meeting.

The Chairperson asked whether any Member who had participated in the meeting had identified inaccuracies or issues within the minutes. It was noted that Ms M Ndlangisa-Nodada (ANC, Eastern Cape), who had chaired the previous meeting, was not present.

The Chairperson said that he had personally attended the meeting, despite having to divide his attention between multiple commitments on that day, and that he had not identified any issues with the minutes. He then asked Members whether the Committee could proceed with the adoption of the minutes.

Ms Sithole formally moved for the adoption of the minutes as presented. The Chairperson seconded the motion, noting that he had attended the meeting and was satisfied that the minutes constituted a true reflection of the proceedings. Mr Farmer also indicated support for the adoption.

The minutes were subsequently adopted without amendment as a true reflection of the proceedings.
