SONA Impact on sector; School Infrastructure backlogs, Maintenance and related matters; with Minister

Basic Education

03 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Committee met to be briefed on the implications of the 2026 State of the Nation Address (SONA) on the basic education sector, as well as school infrastructure backlogs.

The analysis of the 2026 State of the Nation Address (SONA) indicated that basic education remains a central component of the government’s development agenda, particularly in supporting economic growth, social inclusion and improved state capacity. Sector priorities emphasise expanding access to and improving the quality of Early Childhood Development (ECD), strengthening foundational literacy and numeracy, improving system efficiency and inclusivity, and aligning infrastructure and resources with learning outcomes. The address reaffirmed commitments to universal access to education, the expansion of ECD through the mass registration of centres and the implementation of compulsory Grade R, and continued efforts to improve reading outcomes and mother-tongue-based bilingual education. Progress in the sector was reflected in high levels of school participation, increased completion rates across schooling phases, and improved equity outcomes for historically disadvantaged learners. However, challenges remain in learning quality, particularly literacy and numeracy, and in learner retention in the senior grades. Programmes such as school nutrition, non-fee schools, and the Presidential Employment Stimulus also continue to support access, learner wellbeing and employment opportunities within the sector.

Infrastructure planning and delivery remain critical to supporting these reforms, but are constrained by funding pressures, growing enrolment, migration patterns and ageing infrastructure. The education system faces a substantial infrastructure backlog estimated at approximately R227 billion, including both capital and maintenance needs, while annual allocations remain significantly below the level required to meet national development targets. Although progress has been made in areas such as sanitation improvements under the SAFE programme and ongoing delivery of classrooms, water, electricity and fencing, backlogs persist, with about 13% of schools classified as being in poor or very poor condition. Overcrowding continues in certain provinces due to population shifts, and climate-related disasters have affected thousands of schools in recent years. The Department has prioritised preventative maintenance, improved infrastructure planning and strengthened data systems, while exploring alternative funding mechanisms and partnerships to address the shortfall and support long-term infrastructure development, including the expansion of ECD facilities and infrastructure for learners with special education needs.

Members raised concerns about the Department’s accountability, governance, and engagement with the Committee. Concerns were expressed about the Department’s late apology for missing a previous meeting, with the view that it created the impression that the Committee’s oversight role was being undermined. Members also reiterated concerns about alleged interference from the Minister’s office in a recruitment process, stating that previous responses had not adequately addressed the substance of the allegations, particularly claims that a CV was submitted after the closing date for applications. Several Members supported the proposal for an in-house Committee meeting to investigate the matter further and requested that the Minister attend in person to clarify the allegations. Members emphasised the importance of transparency and compliance with lawful recruitment processes. The Chairperson indicated that the Committee had a responsibility to investigate such allegations and that a dedicated session would be scheduled to examine the matter.

Members also raised a wide range of policy and implementation concerns relating to literacy outcomes, infrastructure delivery, and Early Childhood Development (ECD). Concerns were raised about poor reading outcomes reflected in national and international assessments, and questions were asked about plans for early grade standardised testing, participation in PIRLS, curriculum time allocated to literacy, and nutrition support in ECD centres. Several Members questioned the accuracy of infrastructure data. They raised concerns about progress in eradicating pit latrines, the use of infrastructure grants, delays in construction projects, and the condition of schools observed during oversight visits. Members also raised issues relating to infrastructure backlogs, storm damage to schools, contractor performance, and the need for stronger monitoring of provincial spending. Additional questions related to the expansion of Grade R, support for ECD centres operating on private land, teacher shortages, learner transport, and access to schooling, with Members emphasising that improved infrastructure, maintenance, and reliable data were essential to improving learning conditions and ensuring learner safety and dignity.

Meeting report

Opening remarks

The Chairperson said the Committee understood the issue of dilapidated school buildings. The reason for inviting the Department of Public Works and Infrastructure (DPWI) was clear: in many provinces, it is the provinces that implement school infrastructure projects, whether building new schools, adding classrooms, repairing dilapidated buildings, or conducting minor and major maintenance. In these processes, the Department of Basic Education (DBE) often works with the DPWI and other implementing agents to deliver on infrastructure projects and address the backlog.

In many instances, the Auditor-General has highlighted challenges in projects implemented through the DPWI using different agents. As a result, the Committee wanted insights from the DPWI and these implementing agents to understand frustrations and delays especially cases where projects were started but remain incomplete, or even abandoned by contractors.

Unfortunately, the DPWI requested to be excused from the meeting. That was concerning, and the Committee would raise it with the House Chairperson to escalate the matter with the Leader of Government Business, the Deputy President, regarding the accountability of these departments to Parliament.

During its oversight visits, the Committee observed that while some school infrastructure was in good condition, many areas faced severe challenges. It was therefore important to understand the reasons behind those delays, the funding backlogs, and the Department’s ability to deliver infrastructure commitments.

For example, during engagements with the DBE in different provinces, they indicated that delays were often due to the DPWI. In one case, a Member of an Executive Council (MEC) had committed a year ago that a project had been handed over to the DPWI, but when the Committee visited, there was no progress and no report on the site. This illustrates the importance of having the DPWI engage with the Committee.

She proposed that after these engagements, the Committee re-invite the Department, but this time with better preparation as a Portfolio Committee. Previously, the Committee requested a comprehensive list of outstanding school infrastructure projects, including start dates, delays, and backlogs broken down by school, province, and district. That information, which the Committee received the previous year, should now be updated and submitted before re-inviting the Department. That would allow the Committee to hold the Department accountable for specific projects and backlogs.

Similarly, National Treasury needed to be engaged. In some provinces, like KwaZulu-Natal, officials reported insufficient funding to address infrastructure backlogs due to budget cuts. The Committee needed to hear Treasury’s perspective on how government planned to provide quality education in adequate school facilities and how government planned to address ongoing and future infrastructure needs, especially in provinces with increasing learner populations.

The intent of the oversight session was therefore to engage fully with those departments and, if necessary, escalate concerns to both the House Chairperson and the Deputy President regarding non-attendance and accountability.

That said, the Committee had the DBE present, and it had prepared a presentation for the Committee.

Minister’s opening remarks

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, said the Department welcomed the opportunity to appear before the Committee, as the Department considered that its constitutional obligation.

She said that the first presentation related to the 2026 State of the Nation Address (SONA). In the 2026 SONA, the President reaffirmed that education is central to economic recovery, social cohesion, and long-term national stability. For the DBE, this meant that it needed to strengthen foundational literacy and numeracy, protect learner support grants, stabilise provincial finances, and ensure that infrastructure delivery supported the implementation of compulsory Grade R. She said that the Department would take the Committee through the commitments made and the progress the Department had made to implement those commitments.

The Minister said that the second presentation would focus on school infrastructure. It was important to note that school infrastructure delivery was a concurrent function implemented by provincial education departments. The DBE was involved in setting norms and standards, allocating and monitoring conditional grants, providing oversight and support, and intervening through intergovernmental mechanisms where necessary, especially where provinces were underspending on infrastructure despite significant need.

She said that she also wanted to highlight the transition from the School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant (SIBG) to the Education Infrastructure Grant (EIG). Previously, under SIBG, certain projects were centrally managed by the DBE. The EIG, however, was allocated to provinces. Those two grants had now been merged, leaving the provinces with greater responsibility for planning, procurement, and implementation. While that provided flexibility, it also increased accountability, and rigorous monitoring and evaluation by the national department was now more critical.

The Department’s role included ensuring accurate expenditure tracking, improved project performance on sites, early identification and management of delays, and intervention when implementing agents underperformed. One example was the Sanitation Appropriate for Education (SAFE) initiative, which targeted schools with inappropriate sanitation facilities that posed immediate risks. The programme was now substantially complete, with the majority of verified sites addressed. It was important to distinguish SAFE backlog projects from the broader infrastructure backlog. The SAFE projects were identified in 2018, and once completed, further audits would be conducted to identify remaining infrastructure needs.

Current infrastructure challenges were driven by several factors, including implementing agent performance, procurement delays, planning weaknesses, maintenance backlog, fiscal constraints, and capacity differences across provinces. Funding allocations alone did not resolve those challenges. With the consolidation of Early Childhood Development (ECD) and the implementation of compulsory Grade R, infrastructure planning needs to be realistic and phased to ensure expansion aligned with: classroom readiness, practitioner deployment, Learner Teacher Support Material (LTSM) availability, sanitation, and compliance with standards.

In conclusion, the Minister said that the work before the Department was complex but manageable. The Department needed to clarify responsibilities, strengthen oversight, support provinces, tighten monitoring and evaluation, protect learner dignity and safety, and align infrastructure planning with foundational learning reforms. The Department would now present a detailed analysis of the SONA and its implications for the sector, followed by the infrastructure presentation. The Department looked forward to a constructive discussion with all Members.

Analysis, Implications and Impact of the President’s 2026 SONA

Dr Stephen Taylor, Director: Research Coordination, Monitoring and Evaluation, DBE, said the presentation provided an analysis of the recent SONA address by the President, focusing on its implications for basic education.

Background and Strategic Priorities

The Minister had identified five strategic priorities for the sector, namely enhancing access to and quality of Early Childhood Development (ECD), strengthening foundational literacy and numeracy, improving quality and efficiency across the system, expanding inclusivity, and aligning infrastructure and resources to support learning outcomes. The Action Plan to 2029 includes 27 goals: Goals 1–13 focus on improving learning outcomes, such as Grade 3 literacy and Grade 12 performance. Goals 14–27 focus on systemic improvements, including district management, class sizes, and resource allocation.

These plans are aligned with long-term frameworks like the National Development Plan, Medium Term Development Plan (previously MTSF), and international commitments. They inform both national and provincial annual performance plans, which are monitored quarterly and reported periodically. The SONA functions as an annual reaffirmation of priorities within this broader framework.

Theory of Change

The Department has mapped a theory of change that aligns with government’s three strategic priorities economic growth and job creation, social welfare and inclusivity, and improved state capacity. Basic education activities feed into these priorities, particularly through ECD and early grade literacy and numeracy, foundational learning improvements, and pathways into higher education and skills development.

Progress in the Sector

Progress is assessed across six dimensions: access, redress, equity, quality, efficiency, and inclusivity.

Access

South Africa has high levels of schooling participation from ages 6 to 24, including strong early learning participation among 5- to 6-year-olds. Participation in early childhood education (0–4 years) has also increased significantly.

Attainment

Grade 7 completion: ~97% (2023)

Grade 9 completion: >90% (2023)

Grade 12 completion: >60% (2023)

International comparisons show South Africa’s secondary completion rates now align with countries like Brazil, China, and Turkey.

Quality

Quality remains a challenge, measured by learning outcomes in international (TIMSS, PIRLS, SACMEQ) and national assessments. Grade 9 numeracy proficiency increased from 12% in 2002 to 45% in 2023. National Senior Certificate (NSC) (matric) passes have increased from ~40 000 in 1970 to over 650,000 currently, with significant growth in bachelor-level passes since 2018.

Efficiency

Grade repetition remains high in Grades 10 and 11. Large class sizes persist, highlighting the need for adequate educators and resources. Over-aged learners are still present in later grades, though the situation has improved over the last 25 years. Gender disparities exist, with girls consistently outperforming boys.

Redress and Equity

Most improvements in access, completion, and quality have been driven by black and coloured learners in Quintile 1–3 schools. Bachelor-level pass increases are predominantly from these schools, representing a “silent revolution” in educational equity. Historical improvement: Only ~10% of black South Africans born in the 1950s–1960s completed matric; now ~60% do.

Inclusivity

Programmes such as school nutrition (feeding >10 million learners daily), non-fee schools, and special education initiatives support vulnerable learners.

Key Highlights from the 2026 SONA

∙ Emphasis on universal access to quality education and the role of education in economic growth.

∙ Expansion of ECD through mass registration of facilities and compulsory Grade R.

∙ Focus on literacy, numeracy, and mother-tongue-based bilingual education.

∙ NSE results: 2025 class achieved 88% pass rate, the highest historically.

∙ Learner retention and STEM: Need to address high dropout rates and expand participation in maths and science.

∙ Skills development, overhaul of skills training, including dual education models and improved governance of CTAs.

Presidential Employment Stimulus

Basic education contributed nearly 1.5 million job opportunities, the highest among sectors. Comparison to previous SONAs (2021–2025):

∙ 2021: Focus on pandemic recovery and ECD;

∙ 2022: Emphasis on learning recovery and leadership;

∙ 2023: ECD support, infrastructure, vocational training, and matric results;

∙ 2024: Continued focus on matric, presidential employment, and system challenges;

∙ 2025: Emphasis on ECD, reading quality, infrastructure, and introduction of time-based funding;

∙ 2026: Continued focus on matric results, foundational literacy/numeracy, Mother-tongue-based Bilingual Education (MTBDE), dropout, STEM access, and skills development.

Reporting on Previous SONA Commitments

The DBE tracked progress against past SONA commitments through monthly reporting and meetings.

∙ Examples included: development of eCare system, registration of additional ECD centres and facilities, training, and learning materials.

∙ Reading: Early Grade Reading Program rollout, classroom libraries, teacher training, and assessment of African language benchmarks.

∙ Inclusive education: Task teams for out-of-school learners with disabilities.

∙ Three-stream model: Electrical, mechanical, civil technology schools; coding and robotics programmes, teacher training, lesson plans, and connectivity support.

∙ Teacher support: National Teaching Awards and professional development programmes.

∙ Infrastructure: Regular progress reports on school construction and sanitation projects.

Infrastructure Planning, Delivery and Monitoring in the Sector

Mr Ramasedi Mafoko, Acting Chief Director: Infrastructure Planning and Delivery, DBE, briefed the Portfolio Committee on the status of school infrastructure delivery, funding pressures, maintenance, disaster response, and Early Childhood Development (ECD) infrastructure. He confirmed that implementing agents and provincial heads of infrastructure from all nine provinces were present to support the engagement.

Policy Context and Programme Overview

Infrastructure planning was guided by the National Development Plan (NDP) and sector priorities. He said that the DBE currently implemented two main infrastructure funding streams: Provincial Infrastructure Programme (primary programme going forward), SIBG, which funds the ASIDI (Accelerated School Infrastructure Delivery Initiative) and the SAFE. He said that the SAFE programme would conclude on 31 March of the current financial year. From 2026/27 onwards, infrastructure delivery would primarily occur through the provincial infrastructure programme.

Fiscal Context and System Pressures

He highlighted that the education infrastructure system operated within an expanding education system, a constrained fiscal environment, increasing enrolments and migration pressures. He said that although allocations had increased nominally, unmet infrastructure demand remained substantial. Key pressures included approximately 3 000 schools still built from inappropriate materials, overcrowding caused by internal migration (affecting all provinces, not only the Western Cape), historical under-maintenance, vandalism and crime, climate-related damage and disasters.

Infrastructure Data Systems

Infrastructure data was drawn from the Education Facilities Management System (EFMS), which was integrated into the National Education Management Information System (NEMIS). The Education Management Information System (EMIS) was the primary source for school data. He said that provinces supplement that data to ensure completeness.

Status of Infrastructure Provision

SAFE Programme: 3 372 total SAFE projects, 3 320 completed, 44 projects remaining (to be completed by year-end). Mr Mafoko noted that there was one overcrowding project in the Northern Cape nearing completion. He added that an audit would be conducted after programme completion to identify any remaining sanitation backlogs.

Basic Services

On water. Mr Mafoko said that all schools have some form of water provision (municipal supply, boreholes, rainwater harvesting). On electricity, he said that schools use grid connections, solar systems, and generators. Many schools now have multiple supply sources.

Libraries and Laboratories: 57% of schools have libraries, 32% have laboratories, 49% have computer facilities. Mr Mafoko said that these figures excluded classroom libraries, cluster libraries, mobile libraries, and multi-purpose rooms used as laboratories or computer centres.

Classrooms and Overcrowding

Nationally, the average learner-to-classroom ratio was 33.6 learners per classroom. He said that on paper, it suggested no systemic overcrowding. However, overcrowding persisted due to geographic mismatch between available classrooms and learner migration patterns. For example, Gauteng would require over 400 additional 24-classroom schools and the Western Cape would require over 300 such schools.

Infrastructure Backlog (Financial Magnitude)

The DBE developed a Capital Investment Planner to quantify infrastructure backlogs. He said that the estimated requirement was R129 billion for the capital backlog, R98 billion for the maintenance backlog, and the total was R227 billion. He said that to meet NDP 2030 targets required annually R57 billion. The current annual allocation for the EIG was approximately R16.3 billion.

He noted that the current MTEF allocation total was R49.9 billion. That meant that current funding did not cover even one year of required investment. After accounting for committed projects (about R10 billion annually), only around R6 billion remained nationally to address backlogs.

Condition of School Infrastructure

Condition ratings:

∙ Very poor: 2% (506 schools);

∙ Poor: 11% (2 544 schools);

∙ Fair: 26% (5 998 schools);

∙ Good: 49% (11 153 schools);

∙ Excellent: 11% (2 588 schools).

He said that approximately 13% of schools are in poor or very poor condition and must be prioritised in planning. Condition assessments were ongoing processes and varied by province. Provinces were being required to update assessments regularly.

Infrastructure Delivery Since 2013

Over the past decade, significant investments have been made in new and replacement schools, water supply, sanitation, electricity, classrooms and high-security fencing. Mr Mafoko noted that while backlogs remained, substantial infrastructure had been delivered nationwide.

Education Infrastructure Grant (EIG) Performance

The current financial year expenditure (as at late February) was 87% spent nationally. He said that most provinces were performing well. He added that the Eastern Cape and Free State had fully exhausted their allocations. Provinces were on track to spend 100% of the EIG by year-end. He noted that future allocations for 2026/27 were R16.2 billion. For 2027/28 it was R16.5 billion and the 2028/29 allocation was R17.1 billion.

Capacity in the Built Environment

There were shortages in critical technical skills such as electrical engineering, mechanical engineering and quantity surveying. Mr Mafoko said that government competed with other departments and the private sector for those scarce skills.

Maintenance Strategy

Maintenance was a priority reform area. Mr Mafoko said that at least 60% of EIG allocations needed to be directed to preventative and corrective maintenance. Provinces submitted Infrastructure Asset Management Plans (IAMPs). A sector-wide maintenance plan had been developed. Costing norms and training manuals had been issued. He said the long-term goal was to shift investment from new builds toward maintenance and rehabilitation.

Disaster Impact

Over the past five years, 3 491 schools were affected by storms and disasters. Almost all provinces were affected annually. Provinces needed to reprioritise EIG funds to respond to disasters, which disrupted planned projects. The Disaster Management Act placed responsibility for rehabilitation on the sector department.

Early Childhood Development (ECD) Infrastructure

ECD infrastructure was funded through the ECD Maintenance Grant. The grant supported: health and safety upgrades for conditionally registered centres, minor maintenance and upgrades and construction of new centres where required. Progress on current year targets included: 23 new centres, 256 centres maintained and 800 health and safety packs delivered. To date, four new centres were completed, 149 centres were maintained and 421 health and safety packs were delivered. He noted that with the budget of R164 million (current year), there was 63% expenditure to date. He expected full expenditure by year-end. Future allocations would increase to R268 million (the following year), to R286 million for 2026/207, and increase to just under R300 million in 2028/2029.

LSEN Infrastructure

Infrastructure provision for Learners with Special Education Needs (LSEN) included new builds, maintenance, upgrades, and additions across provinces.

10-Point Infrastructure Strategy

The DBE had implemented a 10-point plan to accelerate delivery, including strengthening EFMS data, framework agreements to fast-track procurement, standardised prototype school designs, cost norms, revised capacitation framework, project pipeline strengthening, diversified implementation strategies, alternative funding models (Public-private partnerships [PPPs], blended finance, Infrastructure South Africa collaboration), and post-occupancy evaluations to improve future design.

Key Risks

Risks included insufficient funding, cost overruns, political and governance risks, construction delays, environmental approvals and rezoning delays and crime and vandalism. Mitigation measures were in place at project, programme, and portfolio levels.

Mr Mafoko concluded that infrastructure backlogs remained substantial. Funding levels were insufficient to meet NDP 2030 targets. Maintenance needs to be prioritised. Provinces needed to ensure credible project pipelines. Alternative financing mechanisms needed to be explored. Full expenditure of conditional grants was non-negotiable. He recommended that the Portfolio Committee note and discuss the infrastructure progress report.

Discussion

Mr S Louw (ANC) noted that the Department’s absence from the previous meeting had caused concern, particularly as apologies were submitted at short notice. He expressed the view that this created the impression that the Committee’s oversight role was being undermined.

He stated that when the Committee sought to exercise its oversight function, Members should not be “lectured” or treated as though they did not understand their responsibilities. He emphasised that the Committee was fully aware of its mandate and the legal framework governing its work.

Mr Louw referred to an issue he had raised in a previous meeting concerning alleged interference from the Minister’s office in relation to a particular appointment. He said that instead of receiving a direct and substantive response, he received what he described as a legal lecture and a reframed administrative explanation. In his view, the response did not adequately address the concerns raised.

He expressed frustration that the allegations of interference were not directly engaged, the letter received did not respond to issues such as the submission of a curriculum vitae (CV), and there was no clear explanation regarding the alleged involvement of the Minister’s office. He noted that there was no indication of a willingness to investigate the matter and indicated that the response focused on administrative processes rather than accountability.

Mr Louw argued that if interference did occur, it would raise serious concerns about transparency and accountability as required by Section 195 of the Constitution, as well as compliance with the Public Service Act of 1994 and lawful recruitment processes.

He stressed that this was the second time the matter had been raised and that he felt his concerns were not being taken seriously. He maintained that the Committee must not be undermined and must fulfil its oversight and accountability responsibilities without being directed or instructed by the Department.

In light of those concerns, Mr Louw proposed that the Committee establish a special in-house session to deal specifically with the matter. He indicated that he wished for the issues of legality and alleged interference to be properly examined and, if necessary, investigated by the appropriate authority.

He concluded by stating that he would submit his infrastructure-related questions in writing and reiterated that the Committee must not be placed in a position where it appeared to be disregarded or inadequately engaged by the Executive.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) differed with her colleague’s remarks regarding the apologies, while also acknowledging the importance of the Department’s participation the previous week in engagements on the country’s literacy crisis, particularly at the uMlambo Foundation Reading Panel Conference.

She noted that the outcomes of recent literacy studies should be of serious concern, specifically: the 2021 Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) results, which found that 81% of Grade 4 learners in South Africa could not read for meaning in any language. The more recent Funda Uphumelele National Survey, which found that 15% of Grade 3 learners could not read a single word in their third year of schooling, even in their mother tongue.

On literacy and assessment, Ms Jordaan posed several questions in response to these findings. On Early Grade Standardised Testing, she asked if the Department had considered introducing early-grade standardised testing to better inform interventions? Was there a national reading plan with binding targets and reporting systems?

On time allocation for literacy, she said that in light of recent literacy outcomes, did the Department believe sufficient time was currently allocated to literacy activities? She asked if there was a need to review the curriculum time devoted to reading.

On Participation in PIRLS, she asked if the Department intended to participate in the next cycle of the PIRLS .If not, what informed that decision? What alternative independent and rigorous assessment mechanisms would be introduced to benchmark Grade 4 reading literacy? How would these provide both local benchmarks and internationally comparable data to guide policy and resource allocation?

On language and format of assessments, she asked if South Africa participates in PIRLS, would the assessment be administered in all official languages? If not, what criteria would determine the languages selected? She asked if the assessment would be paper-based or digital? If digital, what impact did the Department anticipate on results? She asked what measures would ensure that learners in under-resourced schools were not excluded from participation, particularly given that those learners should be the primary target for reading interventions.

On infrastructure funding and compliance, referring to the infrastructure presentation by Mr Mafoko, she raised questions about the proportion of EIG and ECD allocations that were spent versus returned to National Treasury in the most recent financial year. She asked how many provinces spent their full allocations and which did not. She asked whether provinces were tracking and reporting on compliance with minimum safety norms and standards for school infrastructure. She asked what consequence management measures were in place where there was failure to comply with basic safety requirements.

She also noted the concern that a large portion of infrastructure funding was still directed toward new builds rather than maintenance. What steps were being taken to shift decision-making toward preventative and ongoing maintenance? How was the Department addressing the culture of favouring new construction over extending the lifespan of existing infrastructure?

She observed that oversight visits often revealed older schools (dating from the 1950s to 1980s) where proper maintenance could potentially have prolonged usability, rather than necessitating new construction.

On unused or incomplete infrastructure, she referred to recent oversight visits and media reports, including facilities in Limpopo, and Ratanang Primary School in Hammanskraal, Gauteng. She noted cases where newly built or nearly completed facilities remained unused due to outstanding compliance documentation.

On municipal regulatory delays, she noted minor unfinished work such as wiring or compliance certificates. She said in some cases, schools reportedly occupied incomplete buildings due to overcrowding. How many completed or near-completed school facilities nationally remained unused due to regulatory or compliance issues? When would Ratanang Primary School in Hammanskraal be opened for public use?

On ECD infrastructure, she welcomed plans to collaborate with provincial and local government and the DPWI to repurpose government buildings and non-viable schools for ECD expansion. She expressed the view that this approach could significantly expand access to early childhood education, even though it was framed as a long-term strategy.

On nutrition and ECD, she raised concerns about the limited emphasis on nutrition in both the SONA analysis and the President’s address, particularly in the context of the literacy and malnutrition crisis. She noted that the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) primarily covered Grade R to Grade 12 learners and asked how nutrition support was regulated and delivered at ECD centres, especially given that many are privately owned .Was there a requirement within the per-child subsidy allocation that a portion must be spent on nutrition? How did the Department monitor whether registered ECD centres provided quality, nutritious meals on a daily basis?

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) welcomed the inclusion of a Theory of Change framework in the presentation, noting that he had previously encouraged the Department to adopt this approach. He also appreciated the quantification of the school infrastructure backlog, describing it as “painting by numbers”, as well as the Department's acknowledgement of data challenges affecting planning and the recognition of the need for technical capacity within the Department to address infrastructure challenges. He indicated that his remarks would focus on those four areas, while also raising constructive concerns.

Mr Mkhize suggested that the Theory of Change could be strengthened by clearly defining the DBE’s specific problem statement, rather than focusing primarily on broad government objectives. He emphasised that a theory of change must clearly identify the weaknesses that need to change, such as dilapidated infrastructure and systemic shortcomings.

He noted the specific departmental problems that the strategy sought to address, and by way of example, he noted that under Strategic Pillar Two, school infrastructure appeared framed mainly as a means to reduce the financial burden on households. He argued that infrastructure should instead also be explicitly recognised as a pedagogical factor essential to teaching and learning. Without adequate infrastructure, the Basic Education Laws Amendment (BELA) Act could not be properly implemented, digital transformation initiatives could not succeed, and teaching and learning were fundamentally compromised. He stressed that infrastructure was central to educational quality and societal development.

Mr Mkhize requested clarity on how key figures were determined for the estimate that approximately 3 500 new schools were required, the need for approximately 43 000 new classrooms, the target of 4 000 sanitation projects and the reported R120 billion infrastructure backlogs. What methodology was used to arrive at these numbers? Were the figures based on a comprehensive needs assessment on the ground, or on financial limitations? How could the Department be certain that no communities were excluded from the calculations? Did the R120 billion backlog figure include all components such as classrooms, schools, sanitation, libraries, laboratories, fencing, and water supply or only selected elements? He emphasised the importance of basing planning on actual needs rather than available budgets.

Mr Mkhize raised a concern about reliance on older data sources (ranging from 1996 to 2007) in 2026, asking how realistic and current such data could be. He further asked whether the statistics indicating 2% of schools were “poor” and 11% “very poor” had been verified. He asked how those classifications were determined and whether the Department had physically assessed all affected schools, especially given acknowledged data reliability challenges. He requested clarity on the infrastructure standards used to classify schools as poor or very poor and asked that those standards be shared.

On oversight and monitoring of provincial spending, he expressed concern about oversight of infrastructure work undertaken by provinces through conditional grants. While noting references to site visits and oversight travel, he suggested that the Department should introduce an online or electronic real-time monitoring system and a transparent tracking mechanism showing which projects were progressing and which were stalled. He argued that automated systems could provide more reliable oversight than relying solely on human reporting. He requested that the Department share a clear oversight plan.

Mr Mkhize asked whether the Department had a proactive strategy to respond to population growth. He noted that population growth statistics should enable forward planning, rather than being cited as a justification for infrastructure shortfalls. He urged the Department to plan strategically for the cumulative impact of growth over time.

He questioned why Strategic Pillar Two did not clearly prioritise the employment of additional teachers, particularly given existing teacher shortages, the implementation requirements of the BELA Act, and community-level shortages affecting teaching and learning.

Mr Mkhize raised concerns about inconsistencies in fencing statistics. He observed that when different fencing categories were totalled, they appeared to exceed the denominator of schools identified as needing fencing. He asked whether some schools may have been counted multiple times, or the denominator itself may be inaccurate. He stressed that the figures needed to be reconciled accurately.

He highlighted that infrastructure deterioration was cumulative and ongoing. Even as backlogs were addressed, additional deterioration continued. How did the Department plan strategically for the ongoing and increasing nature of school dilapidation? What proactive mechanisms were in place to prevent backlogs from escalating?

Finally, Mr Mkhize acknowledged the example of high-quality sanitation facilities shown in the presentation. He urged the Department to ensure that such standards were implemented across all provinces including the Free State, Mpumalanga, and North West so that rural and historically disadvantaged communities received the same level of dignity and quality infrastructure.

Ms P Mngadi (MKP) observed that, firstly, in Chapter 10, the document stated that access to schooling for the compulsory school-going age group of seven to 15 years had remained above 98% since approximately 2009. Mr Mafoko thanked the Minister and the Members of the Committee and proceeded to respond to several of the issues that had been raised. Mr Mafoko began by addressing the question regarding the ECD maintenance grant. He clarified that the grant was not transferred directly to ECD centres. Instead, it was managed centrally at the provincial level, in a manner similar to the EIG. He explained that there were three categories of interventions under the ECD infrastructure support framework, each with its own qualifying criteria.

The first category related to health and safety interventions. Those interventions were not dependent on land ownership. Whether an ECD centre was located on government land, tribal authority land, or land owned by a non-profit organisation (NPO), the centre could still qualify for this support. Under that category, the Department provided health and safety packs to help centres meet the requirements for registration. Those packs typically included items such as fire extinguishers and other safety equipment, which many ECD centres currently lacked. Addressing those deficiencies helped centres meet the basic compliance requirements needed for registration.

The second category involved maintenance and minor upgrades. That support applied to ECD centres that were conditionally registered. Once a centre obtained conditional registration status, it became eligible to receive assistance for maintenance work or small infrastructure improvements through the maintenance grant.

The third category related to new construction of ECD facilities. In that case, land ownership became a critical factor. New construction could only take place where the ECD centre was located on government-owned land, land under a traditional authority, or land owned by an NPO. Those were the qualifying criteria for infrastructure development under that category.

Mr Mafoko noted that during the Committee’s visit to Limpopo, there had been some misunderstanding regarding those qualifying criteria among officials dealing with infrastructure matters. He indicated that the Department had since clarified the issue with colleagues in Limpopo and that they were now aligned on how the framework should be applied.

Responding to a question from Ms Jordaan regarding whether funds from the EIG had been returned to the National Treasury, Mr Mafoko stated that no funds had been returned. He explained that the Department had established a system to prevent underspending. If a Provincial Education Department (PED) appeared likely to underspend its allocation, the national department engaged with that province as early as the first quarter of the financial year. By the third quarter, provinces that were likely to underspend were identified. At that stage, the Department worked with the National Treasury to redirect the funds to provinces that had the capacity to spend them. That process was permitted under the Division of Revenue Act and the grant framework governing the EIG. As a result of that system, the Department had generally avoided returning funds to the National Treasury, as allocations were redirected to provinces that could utilise them effectively.

Mr Mafoko then addressed questions raised by Mr Mkhize regarding infrastructure backlogs and funding shortfalls. He explained that the DBE developed what was known as a Capital Investment Plan. The plan was based on data submitted by PEDs. At a particular point in time, the Department compiled a snapshot of the available data, which was then used to estimate infrastructure needs. Using that dataset, the Department assessed factors such as the number of schools with poor or very poor condition ratings (ratings 1 and 2). The data also included schools that required libraries, laboratories, classrooms, and other facilities required under infrastructure norms and standards. He said the maintenance backlog and general condition of school infrastructure. Based on those needs, the Department then applied construction cost estimates, such as the average cost of building a classroom, library, or laboratory. Using those cost estimates, it calculated the total funding required to address the full infrastructure backlog.

Mr Mafoko emphasised that the department updates this information annually, as new data becomes available from provincial condition assessments. Because infrastructure conditions deteriorate over time, data from previous years may no longer accurately reflect the current situation. For example, while earlier reports indicated that 10% of schools fell within condition ratings 1 and 2, updated provincial assessments have since produced slightly different figures.

He therefore stressed that condition assessment was not a once-off event but an ongoing process, as the condition of infrastructure changed over time. The Department therefore relied on updated information from provinces rather than static historical data. He also clarified that the Department calculated infrastructure needs based on actual requirements, rather than working backwards from the available budget. In other words, the Department first determined what was needed to address the backlog, and then compared that requirement to the available funding.

Mr Mafoko noted that the Department relied on the Norms and Standards for School Infrastructure, which served as the primary policy framework for determining what facilities a school should have and the standards that must be met. He offered to provide this document to the Committee if required.

Responding to concerns about inconsistencies in fencing data, Mr Mafoko explained that a single school can have more than one type of fencing. For example, a school might have galvanised fencing in one area and brick-and-mortar fencing in another. In some cases, a school may have two or even three different fencing types across its perimeter. That was why the numbers presented in the data may not align exactly when compared at school level.

In response to a question from Ms Mngadi about strategies to address the infrastructure funding shortfall and the impact on the eradication of inappropriate structures, Mr Mafoko explained that the Department had been exploring alternative funding mechanisms. That work was being undertaken in collaboration with Infrastructure South Africa in order to help bridge the funding gap.

He also confirmed that the Department had submitted a Budget Facility for Infrastructure (BFI) application to National Treasury. The application was submitted on 7 January 2026, and National Treasury acknowledged receipt on 8 January 2026. Treasury indicated that the applications would be considered during meetings scheduled for 18–19 March, after which the Department would be informed of the outcome.

Regarding schools classified as being in poor or very poor condition (condition ratings 1 and 2), Mr Mafoko stated that those schools should receive priority attention when provinces submitted their infrastructure plans for the 2026/27 MTEF. The aim was to ensure that the number of schools in the worst condition categories is progressively reduced.

Mr Mafoko also addressed concerns about storm damage and climate resilience. He indicated that the Norms and Standards for School Infrastructure already outlined requirements for addressing climate-related risks. However, many schools may need to be retrofitted to improve resilience to climate change. As infrastructure projects such as repairs, renovations, and upgrades were implemented, those climate resilience measures were incorporated into the work.

Mr Mafoko noted that several Members had requested detailed reports and information, including data at district level and information about projects currently under construction. He confirmed that those details would be provided to the Committee in writing.

Finally, Mr Mafoko addressed the Chairperson’s question about the strategy to repurpose underutilised buildings for ECD centres. He explained that this formed part of the Department’s long-term strategy for expanding ECD infrastructure. The Department’s overall approach included short-term, medium-term, and long-term strategies, and the repurposing of unused government buildings fell within the longer-term component.

Currently, the Department is still in the early stages of implementing this strategy, as the ECD function had only recently been transferred and the Department is approximately two years into implementation. At present, the focus has been on upgrading existing early learning centres and supporting the development of new facilities.

The process of repurposing buildings required coordination with the DPWI, which was the custodian of government buildings. When a building became underutilised, it was first returned to the custodian department, which may then allocate it to another sector or purpose. Therefore, the DBE had to work with the DPWI to identify suitable buildings that had not yet been reallocated, so that such buildings could potentially be repurposed for ECD facilities.

Mr Mafoko emphasised that although the repurposing programme was still in the planning and coordination phase, it remained an important part of the Department’s long-term vision for expanding ECD infrastructure.

The Chairperson thanked the Minister and the departmental team for their participation in the meeting. She apologised for the background noise, explaining that Members were in transit to attend the afternoon plenary session.

She indicated that the Minister and her team were excused from the meeting, and expressed appreciation for the comprehensive responses provided to the Committee’s questions.

The Chairperson reiterated the Committee’s request that the Department submit the additional information that had been requested during the meeting. She noted that much of the outstanding information and responses were expected from the PEDs, many of whom had not been given sufficient time during the meeting to respond fully. She specifically asked that the Western Cape Provincial Education Department be informed that the Committee was still awaiting its written responses.

She further indicated that the Committee would communicate with the Minister and the Department regarding a future session that would focus specifically on the allegations that had been raised. The Committee would ensure that all relevant parties were notified in advance so that they could attend that session.

The Chairperson also thanked the implementing agents who had attended the meeting. She explained that their presence was important so that they could observe and better understand the Committee’s oversight processes, including the types of questions asked when the Committee examines infrastructure implementation.

She added that once the Committee had received the detailed written reports, a follow-up session might be convened. During that session, certain implementing agents could be called upon to respond to specific issues identified in the reports.

In closing, the Chairperson once again thanked all participants for their time and contributions and formally excused the Minister, the Department, and other attendees.

Report of the Portfolio Committee on Basic Education on an oversight visit to the Limpopo Provincial Education Department

The Chairperson noted that Members had been provided with the report for the past three to four weeks, as it was initially scheduled for adoption in the previous two meetings. She expressed the expectation that Members had reviewed and engaged with the report, and that any proposed amendments would have already been submitted.

She then formally called for the adoption of the oversight report.

Mr Louw stated that he wished to adopt the report, noting that it highlighted all the significant challenges that needed to be addressed. He further emphasised that the report provided clear recommendations aligned with the priorities of Parliament. He concluded by formally expressing his support that he moved for the adoption of the report.

Committee minutes

Minutes dated 3 February 2026, 17 February 2026 and 24 February 2026 were considered and adopted.

The Chairperson noted that she avoided giving strict timeframes to the Department because their presentations were very comprehensive and important for the Committee to go through. The Committee would advise Members on the special session with the Minister and the Department to address the issues it engaged on.

Committee programme

Regarding the programme for the next term, the Chairperson said she had noticed that Ms Jordaan indicated that the Committee had not adopted the first term programme. The Committee actually adopted it provisionally because the Office of the House Chairperson had not provided focus areas at the time. After receiving the focus areas, the Committee presented the programme again to Members, and no amendments were made, so it was adopted. What had not yet been adopted was the second term programme, for which the Committee was still awaiting focus areas. Once received, it would be presented to the Committee. If the second term programme was not received on time, a draft would be prepared and presented regardless.

She indicated that she was concerned about the adoption of minutes. Not all Members seem to participate actively. Members should allow themselves enough time to review the minutes so that any corrections or amendments could be made. Going forward, to avoid a backlog of minutes, the Committee would make it standard procedure to start each meeting with the adoption of previous minutes before addressing other items.

The Chairperson thanked Committee Members for their comprehensive engagement and discussions.

The meeting was adjourned.
