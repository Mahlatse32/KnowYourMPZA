Department of Correctional Services Q3 2025/26 Performance; with Minister

Correctional Services

17 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Ramolobeng (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Committee received a briefing from the Department of Correctional Services (DCS) on its third quarter performance in the areas of administration, incarceration, rehabilitation, care, and social reintegration. The presentation highlighted key issues. including the Integrated Inmate Management System (IIMS) rollout, inmate overcrowding, regional performance disparities, financial expenditure pressures, human resource challenges, and security incidents in the Western Cape.

The Department reported that of the 37 quarterly targets, 32 had been achieved (86%). The five unachieved targets were in administration (IIMS rollout), incarceration (overcrowding and offender profile approvals), and social reintegration (restorative justice and community initiative participation).

The IIMS rollout remained at 0% against an 8% target due to an ongoing arbitration process with the State Information Technology Agency. Inmate overcrowding stood at 56% nationally against a 50% target, with the Eastern Cape at 80%, Gauteng at 72%, and the Western Cape at 63%. Only Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Northwest, Free State and Northern Cape had met the target. The Department commented that its budget had been cut by R11.7 billion over five years, and called for legislative amendments to enable the repatriation of foreign nationals.

The Western Cape was the worst performing region, at 70%, driven by security incidents that included escapes and assaults. Limpopo had achieved 100% of its targets. The Department projected an overspend of R803 million for the financial year, driven largely by food supplies and municipal services. Social reintegration was the only programme that had underspent, at 95%. It reported that six out of nine senior management service (SMS) appointments in quarter three were women, bringing representation of women at the SMS level to 49.51%. It had spent R165 million on contracts awarded to designated groups.

The Department informed the Committee that it was finalising terms of reference for an internal panel to investigate challenges in the Western Cape. It also noted that the Correctional Services Amendment Bill had not been prioritised in the legislative programme for 2026/27.

The Committee expressed serious concern about the Western Cape's performance and the projected overspending. Members called for interim measures in the Western Cape without waiting for the panel's terms of reference to be finalised, and the Chairperson requested a written report on the interim measures to be implemented in the region.

Meeting report

The Portfolio Committee convened to receive a briefing from the Department of Correctional Services (DCS) on its third quarter performance for the 2025/26 financial year. The Minister of Correctional Services, the National Commissioner, and senior departmental officials reported on performance achievements, expenditure trends, regional challenges, and interventions to address overcrowding and security concerns.

The Chairperson reported that she had written to the Minister of Health regarding state patients detained in correctional facilities. The Minister had agreed to appear before the Committee on 31 March to address why state patients remained in correctional facilities, the legal framework governing them, and the mitigating measures being implemented.

She noted that Parliament's revised programme indicated the first quarter would conclude on 1 April, and a study tour would take place the following week, meaning the Committee would not sit again until 31 March.

Department of Correctional Services' Q3 performance report

Dr Pieter Groenewald, Minister of Correctional Services, said the Department had achieved 32 of its 37 quarterly targets (86%). The five unachieved targets were in administration, incarceration, and social reintegration. Administration performance was the highest in five years, at 86%, while incarceration performance was the lowest, at 71%. The unachieved targets related to the Integrated Inmate Management System (IIMS) matter, offender profiles not being properly prepared by case management committees (CMCs), and victims not always being willing to participate in restorative justice processes.

The Minister handed over to the National Commissioner, Mr Makgothi Thobakgale, who delegated to the Chief Deputy Commissioner (CDC): Strategic Management, Mr Joseph Katanga, to present the performance information, and to the Chief Financial Officer (CFO), Mr Lebogang Marumule, to present the financial performance.

Mr Katanga reported that of the 37 quarterly targets, 32 had been achieved. Certain indicators would be reported only in quarter four, including the Correctional Services Amendment Bill, employment equity targets, youth employment, audit outcomes, unnatural deaths, projects completed, and Grade 12 results.

Regional performance averaged 86%. Limpopo, Mpumalanga and North West (LMN) achieved 100% of their targets, while the Western Cape had achieved 70%. The Western Cape had nine unachieved indicators, all related to security. The regional commissioner was engaging bi-weekly with area commissioners, and preliminary indications suggested unachieved indicators would reduce to four in quarter four.

The Department had received acknowledgement of the tasking letter from the State Information Technology Agency (SITA) regarding the IIMS. The CDC responsible would meet with SITA the following day to progress the single-source proposal. Overcrowding stood at 56% against a 50% target. Only the Eastern Cape, Free State and Northern Cape were not overcrowded. Even with the strategy of moving offenders within the system, space was running out. The main intervention was a joint strategy within the Justice, Crime Prevention and Security (JCPS) cluster, with an action plan to which various departments would commit.

Financial performance

Mr Marumule reported that the allocated budget for the 2025/26 financial year was R29.2 billion. By the end of quarter three, the Department had spent R22.9 billion (78%), leaving R6.3 billion (22%) for the final quarter.

Per programme expenditure was:

  • Administration: allocation R3.9 billion, actual R4.1 billion (79%), variance R137 million;
  • Incarceration: allocation R13.6 billion, actual R13.8 billion (77%), variance R256 million;
  • Rehabilitation: allocation R1.7 billion, actual R1.8 billion (80%), variance R80 million;
  • Care: allocation R1.9 billion, actual R2.2 billion (85%), variance R286 million (cost drivers: food and food items); and
  • Social reintegration: allocation R955 million, actual R907 million (73%), variance R47 million (only programme not overspending).

Per economic classification:

  • Compensation of Employees: allocation R15.5 billion, actual R15.4 billion (75%), variance R65 million;
  • Goods and Services: allocation R5.7 billion, actual R6.4 billion (85%), variance R691 million;
  • Interest and Rent on Land: no budget allocation, R10 million spent. Engagement with Treasury was underway for future estimates of national expenditure (ENE) allocations; and
  • Buildings and Other Fixed Structures: allocation R232 million, actual R349 million, variance R116 million.

The Department projected an overspend of R803 million for the financial year, driven by food supplies (R523 million) and buildings and other infrastructure. Municipal accounts could not be delayed due to service provider threats, and the Department had engaged Treasury for an oversight visit. The Department had stopped non-essential procurement and was focusing only on essential items. Inflation adjustments for municipal services were not consistent with market demands, creating a shortfall year after year.

The infrastructure budget was R1.3 billion, with actual expenditure of R1.2 billion at quarter three. The projected overspending of R227 million was due to security-related projects that could not be delayed.

The personnel headcount indicated that the permanent funded establishment was 37 399 against filled posts of 35 892 (variance of 1 507). The Personnel Salary System (PERSAL) showed 42 445 posts against 35 892 filled (variance of 6 553). The vacancy rate on the Human Resources Budget Plan (HRBP) tool was 4%, while on PERSAL it was 15.44%. Alignment work was ongoing.

Regarding revenue collection, R161 million was collected against a projected R173 million for the quarter. Estimated annual revenue was R234 million, and the Department was optimistic of reaching this target.

National Commissioner's comments

Commissioner Thobakgale explained that high expenditure on infrastructure and municipal services was unavoidable, because it included integrated security systems. Stopping these services would lead to security breaches.

On the interplay between regional performance and national outcomes, he said some targets achieved nationally had regions performing above and below the target. When aggregated, the Department had performed. This meant non-performance in management areas had aggregated nationally, and head office branches had needed to provide adequate support. He gave the example of pharmacies, where a coordinating structure had improved performance. In contrast, community corrections had underperformed on restorative justice despite the margin being small. More discipline and stricter performance appraisals were needed.

The Department had completed workshops with case management committees (CMCs) to improve offender profile quality, guided by Auditor-General (AG) findings. A proposal was being finalised to augment CMCs with criminologists and psychologists.

He noted that LMN had achieved 100% of their targets, while the Western Cape was the worst performer. The report showed pressure points, and the Department was implementing interventions accordingly. The Department analysed performance and implemented interventions quarter to quarter, with stricter performance appraisals expected to yield improvement.

Discussion

Ms K Kgobisa-Ngcaba (DA) raised concerns about escapes in the Western Cape, noting that incidents at Allandale and Pollsmoor pointed to weaknesses in perimeter fencing. She questioned why there was no systematic plan to address these gaps, and asked whether someone would have to be killed before the Department took security seriously. On overcrowding, she argued that the Department measured outcomes over which it had no control, as it could not refuse to accept people sent by the courts.

She expressed similar concerns about economic opportunities for parolees, questioning why the Department acted as a job agency when it was struggling with core functions like security and infrastructure. On offender profiles, she argued that the metric should be whether complete profiles were submitted to CMCs, not approval rates, as this incentivised inappropriate releases. She noted the Commissioner's reference to "malicious compliance," and warned that the Department risked releasing people who should not be released.

On restorative justice, she questioned why victim participation was a metric when the Department could not compel victims to participate, and contended that the metric should focus on whether victims had been traced and informed. She asked the CFO to explain the difference between the blue and green categories on Slide 95.

Mr J Engelbrecht (DA) commented that while the spending of 78% was within parameters, the composition and quality of the spending, together with a deteriorating performance in critical areas, was deeply concerning. He asked how the Department would manage the remaining R6.3 billion, and what expenditure control measures had been implemented since quarter two. He raised concerns about the discrepancy between the PERSAL and the HRBP tool, questioning the reliability of the HR data.

He noted the overcrowding situation had deteriorated, despite the overcrowding reduction strategy being cited as corrective action. He asked when the new Burgersdorp facility would be operational, and how much it would reduce overcrowding in the Eastern Cape. He asked what national interventions had been deployed in the Western Cape since October 2025, and why they had been ineffective. He noted that the Allandale escape investigation remained outstanding, and asked for an update. He questioned why corrective actions appeared to be standardised texts, rather than responsive interventions, and asked who was held accountable when these actions failed. He expressed concern that the South African National Defence Force (SANDF) deployment would worsen the crisis in the Western Cape. He asked whether the Department had a plan to accommodate the expected influx.

Mr E Hendricks (PA) referred to the SANDF deployment, and observed that all other management areas were performing well, but the Western Cape was struggling. He asked whether the Department had considered the impact of the deployment, and whether there was a plan to accommodate the expected influx of inmates.

Turning to social reintegration on Slide 88, he said it was the only programme without overspending. He questioned why this area, which was critical to reducing re-offending, was not receiving more resources. He observed that social integration covered economic stability, employment, psychosocial support, health, and family networks, all factors that contributed to successful reintegration and reduced recidivism.

He recalled an oversight visit to Limpopo, Mpumalanga and North West, where he, along with the Chairperson, had met a young man in an overcrowded cell who was participating in rehabilitation programmes and appeared to be a suitable candidate for successful reintegration. He suggested that such cases demonstrated the importance of investing in social reintegration as a means of addressing overcrowding. He said that while food and other essential services required funding, social reintegration should not be the only programme underspending, given its role in preparing offenders for release and reducing the likelihood of their return to correctional facilities.

Mr E Cloete (ANC) said administration expenditure had exceeded projections, with spending reported at approximately 103% of the allocated budget. He asked for a detailed breakdown of the key cost drivers in administration, and what concrete cost containment measures were being implemented to ensure that administrative expenditure was reduced and directed towards core service delivery priorities.

He said overcrowding remained particularly severe in key regions, with the Eastern Cape at 80%, Gauteng at 72%, the Western Cape at 63%, and KwaZulu-Natal at 51% on Slide 33. He asked what specific regionally tailored interventions were being implemented in these prisons, and how the Department was ensuring that these interventions produced measurable reductions in overcrowding.

He commented that participation in DCS agricultural programmes remained below target in certain regions, with the Western Cape achieving approximately 74.84% against a 75% target. Given the importance of agricultural programmes in providing offenders with skills for economic reintegration, he asked what steps the Department was taking to improve participation rates and address the barriers affecting performance in this area.

On economic opportunities in social integration, he said the Department had exceeded the national target, with 633 opportunities facilitated against a target of 521. However, the reality was uneven across regions. He asked how many of these opportunities were sustainable, and how many offenders remained without any form of economic support after release.

He said the Department had achieved 32 out of 37 targets in quarter three, compared to 34 out of 41 in quarter two. He asked whether this represented progress or stagnation, as from the Committee's perspective it appeared to be stagnation rather than meaningful improvement.

Mr M Sokopo (ANC) said he wanted to acknowledge the Department's performance on administration, which had exceeded its target, but questioned whether administrative targets had a relatively low threshold. He asked whether this was deliberate or reflected uncertainty about capacity, and whether increasing the targets would improve accountability.

On community initiative participation, he said there had been a decline, and asked what concrete steps the Department planned to take to address the operational constraints, and how it would ensure that community reintegration programmes were expanded, rather than constrained.

He said the Western Cape continued to face challenges in social reintegration, particularly with victim participation, which stood at 1 258 against a target of over 6 000. He asked what targeted measures were being implemented to improve victim participation, and how the Department was working to strengthen restorative justice processes to ensure better outcomes.

The Department had reported overspending of approximately R663 million across programmes, and he asked for the specifics on the main contributors to this overspending, and why expenditure had not been contained despite previous warnings from the Committee.

He said corrective measures presented by the Department appeared repetitive across quarters, with limited evidence of measurable improvement. He asked how the Department was strengthening its monitoring and evaluation system to ensure that corrective actions led to tangible performance improvement, rather than recurring explanations.

The Western Cape continued to underperform across multiple indicators, and with gangsterism being rife in the region, he asserted that this would affect security and inmate management. He asked what the Department's diagnostic assessment was of the root causes of underperformance in the province, and what targeted interventions and additional oversight mechanisms were being implemented to stabilise performance.

He had previously asked whether the Western Cape should be placed under administration or receive additional assistance, given that the Department of Correctional Services was a national department with the ability to move personnel between regions. He suggested that the DCS consider redeploying managers from high-performing regions to the Western Cape, drawing on examples where officials had performed better after being moved to different roles. He cited the case of an official who had struggled in security and logistics, but excelled when moved to fleet management, and the just-retired Area Commissioner of Thohoyandou, who had stabilised concerns despite language challenges. He said similar interventions had improved the remand detainee section at Pollsmoor. He added that when officials were relocated, they should use state houses rather than hotels to avoid additional costs.

Mr M Moela (ANC) said agreed with the observation that underspending in the area of social integration was concerning, given its importance in preparing offenders for reintegration. He asked how the Department was strengthening restorative justice and community-based interventions as part of a broader strategy to reduce overcrowding and support offender reintegration, noting the clear relationship between reoffending and overcrowding.

He said the Department continued to report underperformance in certain rehabilitation outputs, often linked to planning and resource constraints. He asked how it was balancing staff training commitments with the need to ensure uninterrupted delivery of rehabilitation programmes, and what improvements had been made in planning to minimise disruption.

He said the current overcrowding reduction strategy was failing to yield meaningful results, as evidenced by the increase of 1 876 inmates during the quarter, and the national overcrowding rate of 56% against a 50% target. He asked what new time-bound interventions the Department was introducing to address both the rising inmate population and persistent overcrowding. This was particularly urgent, given the expected influx of inmates from the SANDF deployment. He asked what strategies the Department was employing to be proactive in dealing with this issue.

The presentation had indicated a 0% rollout on the IIMS due to the ongoing arbitration process. He asked for the current status and expected timeline for the conclusion of the arbitration, and what risks to service delivery, data integrity and offender management would arise if the matter remained unresolved into the next financial year.

He said the Western Cape was the least performing region in the quarter, achieving only 21 out of 30 targets, while Limpopo, Mpumalanga and Northwest had achieved 100%. He asked what the Department could do to assist the Western Cape so that it could also perform well, and what lessons and strategies were being drawn from the high-performing region to apply in the Western Cape.

Mr C Niehaus (EFF) said the quarter three report had revealed a department trapped in systemic failure. While the DCS claimed 86% achievement, this was identical to quarter three performances in 2021 and 2024/25, exposing stagnation over five years. The five unachieved targets struck at the core mandate of the Department.

He said the IIMS rollout remained at 0% against an 8% target, blocked by unresolved arbitration that left the system vulnerable to manual errors and tender irregularities. He asked the Department to keep the Committee informed about its contingency plans. Overcrowding had worsened to 56% against a 50% target, with the population surging by another 1 876 inmates between September and December 2025. The Eastern Cape was at 80%, Gauteng at 72%, and the Western Cape at 63%. Only Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Northwest, Free State and the Northern Cape had met the target. He said the Department's explanations, which included the admissions outpacing outflows, poor court responses to sections 49G and 63A of the Criminal Procedure Act, and remand detainees unable to afford bail, confirmed that the justice system criminalised poverty.

He said offender profiles approved for placement by Correctional Supervision and Parole Boards (CSPBs) had fallen to 67.1% against a 69% target, with regions missing targets due to missing stakeholder reports. Participation in restorative justice programmes had dropped from 4 203 against 4 425, and community initiatives had collapsed to 14 670 against 16 182. These failures, he said, exposed the fatal disconnect between prison-based skills programmes and post-release economic opportunities.

On regional performance, he said Limpopo, Mpumalanga and Northwest had achieved 100%, while the Western Cape had plummeted to 70% and remained the worst performer, driven by rising assaults and confirmed escapes at Allandale and Pollsmoor. He was not impressed that the Department continued to refer to erroneous releases when the Committee had established that one incident had been an escape.

Referring to the budget, he said the Department had spent 78% of its R29.2 billion allocation by quarter three, with a projected overspend of R803 million driven by food supplies and outsourced services. The Care programme overspend was 12%, while social reintegration underspent by 95%, proving resources had been prioritised for warehousing rather than reintegration. He asked for disclosure of the top suppliers, their broad-based black economic empowerment (B-BBEE) status, and any political connections or relationships with senior department officials. He asked how the Department justified billions spent on warehousing, while underspending on reintegration.

He asked what concrete legislative amendments the Department would propose, working with the Justice, Crime Prevention and Security (JCPS) cluster, to decriminalise poverty-related offences and expand community-based sentencing. He asked for the contingency plans for the IIMS until the matter was resolved.

He asked for more detail on escape prevention and assault prevention strategies in the Western Cape, with measurable key performance indicators (KPIs) and evidence of implementation. What specific interventions was the National Office making to stabilise the region, and what consequence management was there for district commissioners who were not managing the situation? He warned that if the Western Cape continued to deteriorate without adequate intervention, consequences would impact not only district commissioners, but also head office.

He asked for quarter three raw numbers on youth and female senior management service (SMS) appointments, and the exact rand value of contracts awarded to designated groups from April to December 2025. He asked for the detail of the backlog per region on offender approvals, and binding timelines for stakeholder reports. He asked what sanctions had been applied to centres that repeatedly failed.

On restorative justice and community initiatives, he asked for a list of the 50 largest companies approached for parolee jobs, their responses, and details on placements achieved. He asked for figures on how many technical and vocational education and training (TVET) graduates from quarter three had secured employment or cooperative membership by December 2025.

He concluded that the evidence of this report left no room for denial. These failures demanded fundamental transformation, decarceration of the poor, and an end to the cycle that turned prisons into factories for future offenders. None of this, he said, had been properly addressed in the quarter three report, and there had been regression rather than progress.

Mr M Gasa (MKP) said the Department was losing the battle on overcrowding, with the national target of 50% not being met, as the actual performance stood at 56%. Regions such as the Eastern Cape and Gauteng were in crisis, and the Department's own admission that more people were coming into the system than leaving confirmed that the overcrowding reduction strategy was failing in real time. He asked what radical new plan the Department had to get people out of facilities faster, as the current strategy was clearly not working.

Regarding technology and infrastructure, he said the Department had aimed to roll out the IIMS to 8% of sites in the quarter, but had achieved 0%, representing a complete shutdown of a crucial project stalled by arbitration. He noted that the Western Cape had experienced escapes and erroneous releases because of identity verification procedural failures, and that relying on paper systems or faulty processes led to dangerous security breaches. He asked how the Department could guarantee the safety of the public and the security of its facilities when its core inmate management system was at a standstill, and basic identity checks were failing.

With rehabilitation, he argued that the focus appeared to be on reporting numbers rather than on actual rehabilitation outcomes. He said Programme Three had shown high numbers, but the targets not achieved told a different story, with regions missing targets for correctional programmes due to officials being on leave or a shortage of staff. He asked how the Department was rehabilitating anyone if programmes stopped whenever officials were unavailable, and whether the Department was counting attendance instead of changed lives.

He asserted that administration failures were sabotaging the process. In the Western Cape, the approval of offenders for parole placement was at 56.48%, against a target of 69%, with outstanding documents such as police reports and sentencing remarks not being available. In Gauteng, placement could not happen because address confirmations were missing. This meant offenders who were legally ready to be released were sitting in cells, costing the state money and contributing to overcrowding, simply because of poor administration. He asked why inmates were being kept in jail past their potential placement date because officials could not file paperwork on time, and who was being held accountable for this administrative failure.

He noted that the DCS had spent 78% of its budget by December, but this hid the real story of a projected R803 million overspend. Goods and services were 12% over budget, partly because the Department was still paying last year's bills for food this year. He said this showed a complete failure in financial planning and cost control. He asked which critical services the Department was planning to cut to balance the books, and why taxpayers should fund this level of financial mismanagement.

The Chairperson said she wanted to raise a few matters before the Department responded. She noted that most of what she intended to raise had already been covered by Members, but she wanted to return to the issue of erroneous releases. The Department's presentations had continued to refer to certain incidents as erroneous releases, yet when the Committee met on 3 February, the Department had indicated, based on the investigation team's findings, that one incident was in fact an escape. She questioned why the Department was still characterising it as an erroneous release.

Referring to the financial report, she said Slide 88 reported overspending of R663.661 million, while Slide 91 indicated a projected overspend of R803 million. She asked the CFO to clarify which figure was correct.

She said Members had raised the Western Cape's poor performance extensively, noting that it was the worst performing region, with security breaches and assaults being the primary concerns. She said this was not the first time the Department had identified a crisis in the Western Cape. On 3 February, during discussions on investigations and unnatural deaths, the Minister and the National Commissioner had indicated that certain powers were being taken from the Western Cape and placed at the National Office, and that a commission was to be established. She asked whether those intervention strategies had started, and if not, when they would be implemented.

She said Mr Sokopo had asked whether the Western Cape was effectively being placed under administration, and the Committee had not received a concrete answer. She asked for clarity on whether the Western Cape was now under administration.

On the deployment of the SANDF to crime hotspots across the country, she noted the seriousness of gangsterism, particularly in the Western Cape. She asked whether the Department had started engagements with the South African Police Service (SAPS) and the SANDF to understand their plans and strategies, and to assess how the deployment would impact the Department. She said the Department would be on the receiving end of arrests made, and overcrowding would become excessively high if no preparations were made. She asked what mitigation strategies were being implemented to prepare for the expected influx.

Department's response

Minister Groenewald responded to the Chairperson's questions about engagements with the SANDF and SAPS regarding the deployment. He said the Department had not yet held any meetings, but he would follow up. He commented that even the Joint Standing Committee on Defence (JSCD) had difficulty obtaining operational details. He anticipated that there might be more arrests, and the Department would have to manage the resulting influx, as it had done with the "zama zama" arrests, where approximately 4 000 people had been taken into custody.

Turning to the Western Cape, he confirmed that he was finalising the terms of reference for an internal panel to investigate challenges in the region. The panel would not be a lengthy commission like the Madlanga Commission, but would operate for a maximum of two to three weeks, engaging directly with heads of correctional centres to develop solutions and recommendations.

He acknowledged that reintegration was a critical area where the Department faced significant challenges. Responding to a request for a list of companies providing jobs for parolees, he said securing employment for parolees was difficult because it required willing participation from businesses. He noted the public sentiment that offenders received three meals a day in prison and then were assisted with jobs, while law-abiding citizens faced unemployment. Despite this, the Department continued its efforts as required by legislation and its obligation to reduce recidivism.

Regarding overcrowding, he said the Department's budget had been cut by R11.7 billion over five years. He provided updated statistics showing that remand detainees had increased from 58 184 in December, to 62 780 in March, state patients from 387 to 405, and the total inmate population from 166 654 to 168 730. He said if the Department were allocated an additional R1.5 billion, it could create approximately 19 500 bed spaces, which would significantly relieve overcrowding and improve rehabilitation outcomes. Two new facilities planned for Nigel and Kirkwood would add about 5 000 beds, though they would take time to complete.

The Correctional Services Amendment Bill, which was essential for repatriating foreign nationals, had been reprioritised to 2027/28. He asked the Committee to assist in having it reinstated. He said the Department was not making excuses but was facing real challenges, including budget constraints and the broader crisis of criminality in the country. He expressed pride in the Department's officials for their efforts under difficult circumstances.

National Commissioner Thobakgale addressed the concerns raised by Ms Kgobisa-Ngcaba about the DCS's indicators, confirming that the Department agreed with the assessment that it was measuring outcomes over which it had limited control. These matters had been raised with the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) and the AG, where the Department had suggested alternative indicators that better reflected its mandate. The Department was restricted by the guidelines from the DPME and the AG's audit requirements, but it continued to engage on these issues.

Turning to the parole boards, he said the inputs from the Committee had been valuable. The Department could not have a parole board sitting with incomplete offender profiles. Workshops had been conducted with CMCs to improve the quality of submissions. A proposal was being finalised to augment CMCs with criminologists, psychologists and social workers, as legal counsel had advised that current capacity was insufficient to handle the volume of files.

Regarding expenditure, he said the remaining R6.3 billion was being managed on a daily basis. Approvals had been reduced to the lowest operational level, with every cost scrutinised to ensure only essentials were funded. This painstaking exercise would not correct the underlying budget issues the Minister had raised, but it was necessary to manage the current financial pressures.

Human resources presented its own challenges. The Department had not agreed to abolish posts, because that would leave the system without the minimum required capacity. Age retirements and attrition were expected to generate R400 million in savings.

Overcrowding measures were not achieving the expected results. Regional commissioners met monthly with magistrates and chief justices to discuss alternative sentencing, but meaningful impact would require legislative amendments to the Correctional Services Act and the Criminal Procedure Act. There were instances where remand detainees had been in the system for years with no progress on investigations. The Department had made inputs into the JCPS cluster's legislative review process, and hoped to see results once that process was completed.

The Allandale investigation report was complete, and would be submitted shortly.

Regarding the SANDF deployment, he said the Department had not received additional funds to match the increased capacity for arrests. The deployment augmented SAPS's capacity, but the same capacity was not reflected in correctional facilities. The Department had made inputs into the organised crime strategy being finalised by the JCPS cluster, calling for resources to strengthen security and rehabilitation programmes. At the administrative level, these inputs had been made, and executive-level engagements would follow, as the Minister had indicated.

The decline in community initiatives participation, particularly in the Western Cape, would be addressed as part of the broader interventions in that region. Overspending was being managed through instructions to stop non-essential expenditure. The Department reported monthly to Treasury on unavoidable expenses and continued to scale down where possible.

Commissioner Thobakgale acknowledged stagnation over five years, which was partly attributable to the budget reductions and personnel cuts the Minister had outlined. However, there were self-created problems related to performance at different levels of the Department. An analysis had been conducted, the weakest links identified, and work had begun to address them through stricter performance appraisals.

Young people appointed in the quarter numbered 441. The CFO would provide details on procurement targets and designated groups, and the Chief Deputy Commissioner responsible for Human Resources would address SMS appointments. The list of 50 large companies approached for parolee jobs would be submitted to the Committee as directed.

He agreed that overcrowding was not the Department's battle alone, but required system-wide interventions. The Burgersdorp facility was already in use, with offenders decanted from the old facility to allow for renovation and the creation of additional beds. The facility was operational though not yet officially opened due to final completion processes with the Department of Public Works and Infrastructure (DPWI).

Rehabilitation programmes were not merely box-ticking exercises. The Department had examples of successful reintegration, and was at an advanced stage of reviewing its programmes to improve efficacy where failures were identified.

He said that erroneous releases and escapes recorded in the third quarter would be reflected as such in the fourth quarter and annual reports.

A national team had been appointed to investigate security incidents in the Western Cape region. A team from Limpopo, Mpumalanga and Northwest had been deployed to stabilise Pollsmoor, demonstrating cross-pollination of good practices within the system. Threat and risk assessments had been conducted at Goodwood and Pollsmoor, with reports provided to the region for implementation. The Department was considering moving the implementation of these assessments to head office, while empowering the region to sustain the systems.

Deployments of managers were always considered alongside assessments and new appointments. An area commissioner had been appointed for Pollsmoor, and a head of remand detention had been placed. In Drakenstein, the acting area commissioner was leaving on early retirement, highlighting the need to fill positions at the production level. The Department had been pushing regions to fill these vacancies, as performance issues in management areas and correctional facilities could not be resolved without adequate staffing at those levels.

He agreed with Members that the Department needed to move from addressing individual incidents to taking an integrated approach to the security environment and performance in the Western Cape. The Department would review its approach and come up with measures to address the region in a more comprehensive manner.

Mr Katanga addressed two issues raised during the discussion. He explained that the Department was bound by the prescripts of the planning framework, which required that the annual performance plan contain output-defined indicators. He illustrated this with the example of restorative justice -- tracing victims was a process that led to the actual restorative justice event. If the Department were to measure success, it would need to measure the event itself, as tracing alone was a process-oriented indicator.

Mr Marumule responded to the question from Mr Niehaus on designated groups, providing the values for the three quarters. In quarter one, the total spend was R88 million, with women accounting for R28 million, youth R20 million, persons with disabilities R754 000, and black people R38 million. Quarter two saw women at R9.4 million, youth R10 million, persons with disabilities R367 000, and black people R13 million, totalling R34 million. In quarter three, women accounted for R13 million, youth R12 million, persons with disabilities R0, and black people R16 million, totalling R42 million.

Ranked across the three quarters, black people received the highest value at R68 million, followed by women at R52 million, youth at R43 million, and persons with disabilities at R1.1 million. The Department had spent a total of R165 million on contracts awarded to designated groups. This information was derived from the central supplier database (CSD), where service providers captured their information.

Responding to the Chairperson's question on the discrepancy between the R663 million and R803 million figures, he explained that the R663 million was the actual variance at quarter three, which was the difference between the year-to-date budget and actual expenditure. The R803 million was the projected overspending based on spending patterns.

On the action plan for the remaining R6.3 billion, he said the Department had stopped the procurement of non-essential goods and services at the start of quarter four, and was focusing only on essentials. This was intended to reduce commitments, as the Department had started the current financial year with R337 million in payables and accruals from the previous year. By stopping non-essential procurement, the Department had reduced accruals to R155 million to avoid carrying the same burden into the next financial year.

He noted that even with the review of meal plans, the Department was dealing with a daily increase in inmate numbers. Treasury had indicated it would conduct an oversight visit to the Department's centres, which might improve the situation, but the Department remained in a difficult position regarding reprioritisation and expenditure.

Ms Linda Bond, CDC for Human Resource, said that in response to Mr Niehaus's question on female appointments, six out of nine SMS appointments in the third quarter were women.

Ms Anna Molepo, Chief Deputy Commissioner (CDC): Incarceration and Corrections, said she would focus on correctional programmes in addition to what the National Commissioner had indicated. She responded to the suggestion from some Members that rehabilitation was merely a box-ticking exercise. The Department had 13 approved correctional programmes, all developed by its own officials. These programmes were delivered by correctional intervention officers, who were security officials trained to facilitate the programmes. Currently, there is no permanent structure for these officers, meaning the Department relies on security officials who are not permanently designated as correctional intervention officers. These were non-therapeutic programmes focused on awareness raising and life skills development.

She said a microstructure was being developed to establish permanent correctional intervention officers. The officials currently utilised held qualifications in correctional services, management, social sciences and behavioural sciences, and were trained by the DCS. These programmes were distinct from the therapeutic programmes rendered by social workers and psychologists.

Addressing the challenges highlighted in the third quarter report, she explained that some regions had experienced underperformance because correctional intervention officers had been diverted to custody and security duties. The Department had needed to train more correctional intervention officers, as they delivered programmes on an ad hoc basis. Despite these challenges, the Department had achieved on the indicator overall, with only three regions not meeting their targets for the third quarter.

Ms Cynthia Ramulifho, CDC: Remand Detention, said that during quarter three, the National Commissioner had approved letters to the Department of Home Affairs (DHA) to facilitate engagement between provincial heads and the National Office. This was intended to enable regional commissioners to work with Home Affairs, particularly on Section 34 of the Immigration Act, which dealt with undocumented foreign nationals requiring deportation. The process was beginning to yield results, with movement and transportation for deportation already evident in Gauteng, and expected soon in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape.

An intervention between quarter two and quarter three involved the National Commissioner working with the JCPS cluster on a joint interdepartmental overcrowding strategy with a 100-day timeline. The work was still in progress, noting that courts were on recess three times a year, which contributed to overcrowding. The Department of Justice and the National Prosecuting Authority (NPA) needed to assist in this effort.

She added that on 6 March, a meeting had been held at the operational and technical level with the NPA to address bail interventions for amounts less than R500 and less than R1000. Prosecutors were engaging with provincial heads on this matter. The Department's regional commissioners would begin with Gauteng on 16 April, to identify remand detainees with bail who could be moved out of correctional facilities immediately. This would help to create bed space and support the National Commissioner's efforts.

Training on identification, raised in quarter two, was being planned for the new financial year. The Department would host training together with the SAPS on identification procedures to rectify erroneous releases, specifically for the Western Cape and Eastern Cape, as previously recorded.

She appreciated the Chairperson's initiative on state patients, which formed part of the National Commissioner's engagement with the Department of Health, through letters regarding the 405 state patients within correctional facilities who contributed to overcrowding.

Mr Evans Maponya, CDC: Government Information Technology Office (GITO), addressed the questions raised by Mr Sokopo. Regarding the overachievement on ICT targets, he explained that at the beginning of the financial year, the Department had targeted a certain number of installations of automatic vehicle recognition (AVR) systems. When the project encountered delays, the Department had reprioritised and accelerated the work to catch up.

He also noted that SITA had reduced bandwidth costs, which had enabled the Department to upgrade more sites than originally planned. Turning to IIMS, he said the Department had been engaging with SITA and had exchanged correspondence on the matter. He was scheduled to meet with SITA the following day to finalise the procurement process, and was optimistic that the matter would be resolved.

National Commissioner Thobakgale added that the Department had achieved 49.51% gender representation at the SMS level by the end of the third quarter, commenting that this was a moving target.

Follow-up discussion

Mr Niehaus said he had two follow-up questions. Firstly, he asked why the Correctional Services Amendment Bill had been reprioritised to the following year, and whether there was anything the Department had missed that should have been done to ensure it was prioritised. He said the legislation was very important, and its delay was disappointing.

Secondly, he raised the issue of the SANDF deployment. He acknowledged the Minister's point that information on the plans was limited, which the Committee would pursue with the Joint Standing Committee on Defence. However, the plan was clearly to fight crime, and part of the crime identified by the President and by the SAPS and the SANDF was gangsterism in the Western Cape. He noted that gang-related crime was being organised from inside prisons. He asked what coordination was possible to curb this, and what steps could be taken beyond cell phone blocking systems, which had been described as having broader fallout. He asked what could be done in the coordinated effort between the SAPS and the SANDF.

Lastly, he thanked the Department for the quick response on the issues of people with disabilities, women, and black economic empowerment. He asked that those answers be sent through in writing as well.

The Chairperson said she also wanted to address the legislation matter, noting that Mr Niehaus had covered the issue of the Bill not being prioritised for the financial year. She asked what the reason was for it not being prioritised, and whether there was anything the Department had failed to do that had resulted in it being left off the list.

She also requested that the DCS provide a report on the demographics of the Department in its entirety, including new appointments. This would allow the Committee to assess how the Department was positioned in relation to its equity plan, and whether it was meeting its targets. She acknowledged that this was a broad request requiring processed data, and asked that the report be prepared for the week the Committee met with the Minister of Health, on 31 March. This would allow the Committee to determine whether to schedule the Department as a standing item for the second quarter.

Minister Groenewald responded to the follow-up questions on legislation and gang-related crime.

On the Correctional Services Amendment Bill, he said a legislative programme had been sent to all political parties. He had received documentation from Parliament indicating that 12 or 16 pieces of legislation had been reprioritised on grounds of urgency, though the criteria for urgency had not been defined. The Correctional Services Amendment Bill had been moved to 2027/28. He offered to provide the documentation and noted that the list was also available on Parliament's website. He was engaging with colleagues to understand why certain legislation had been prioritised, particularly within the criminal justice system, and asked the Committee to assist in having the Bill reinstated.

Concerning gang-related crime being organised from inside prisons, he said the most critical need was intelligence sharing. He cited an example where he had received information from agricultural unions about a specific individual allegedly organising crime from his cell, and the Department had acted immediately. Another example involved the Cape Crime Community Coalition (CCCC), which had alerted him to a transfer, and the matter had been dealt with promptly. He said the Department could not act without being given the names of individuals involved. He had previously raised this with the Minister of Police, asking why gang leaders whose names were known had not been arrested, and he was still waiting for an answer. He said there were committees where the National Commissioner served, and he would follow up with the Minister of Police and the Minister of Defence on the matter.

He noted that the SANDF had been deployed to assist SAPS with gangs before, and gangs typically lay low during such deployments. The President was required to specify the duration, number of personnel, areas of deployment, and cost. The SANDF did not have arrest powers and only provided support -- the SAPS still had to make the arrests. He reiterated that the question remained why known gang leaders were not being arrested under existing legislation.

Chairperson's closing remarks

The Chairperson said the Committee would wait for the letter the Minister had referred to regarding the reprioritisation of bills. According to her understanding, the Cabinet sent a list of prioritised bills to Parliament through the Leader of Government Business, and Correctional Services was not on that list. She said that the discussion on prioritisation should have happened at Cabinet level, with the Department presenting why its Bill was important. The Committee would wait for the letter to clarify the matter.

She said the Committee remained concerned about the Department's budget overspending, which stood at R663.661 million by December. She urged the Department to seriously consider ways to minimise overspending and to promote self-sufficiency and sustainability frameworks. She reiterated that serious interventions were required to stabilise the DCS.

With regard to overcrowding, she agreed it was a JCPS cluster matter, and the Department was at the receiving end. She said overcrowding had reached a crisis point and would worsen with the current deployments, which could lead to inhumane incarceration and security risks for both offenders and officials. She noted that the overcrowding reduction strategy needed to be improved, and expressed hope that the JCPS cluster strategy Ms Ramulifho had spoken about would yield results.

She commended the Department on the recent SMS appointments, noting that six out of nine were women, and encouraged the Department to continue taking counsel from the Committee on employment equity.

She congratulated the LMN region on achieving 100% of its targets, noting that the Regional Commissioner's detailed knowledge of facilities was a model for other regions. She urged the National Office to provide support to ensure the region did not drop its systems.

On TVET college programmes, she noted that participation in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape was very low, with 63 and 61 offenders respectively. She encouraged the Department to partner with nearby institutions to market the programmes and increase participation.

She welcomed the CFO's engagement with Treasury on interest on land and rent, and urged the Department to avoid continuous overspending under this item.

The Chairperson expressed serious concern about the Western Cape region's performance, which had contributed to security breaches and assaults. She said the process to stabilise the region should be fast-tracked. While waiting for the terms of reference for the internal panel to be finalised, she said the situation was worsening, and would not wait. She called for interim measures to be implemented immediately, and requested a written report by the following Tuesday on the measures being put in place.

She noted allegations regarding an incident at the Drakenstein facility, and urged the Department to investigate. She said the same strategies used to stabilise Pollsmoor should be applied to Drakenstein. She suggested that the Committee might need to schedule a dedicated session on the Western Cape to drill down into the issues, including whether the regional management team was functional, or whether vacancies were hindering performance.

She reminded the Department of the outstanding report on officials redeployed to community corrections following disciplinary processes, which had been requested some time ago.

The meeting was adjourned.
