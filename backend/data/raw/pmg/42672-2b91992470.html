Progress report on Executive Undertakings made by the Minister of Basic Education

NCOP Public Petitions and Executive Undertakings

24 March 2026
Chairperson: Mr O Mokae (DA, Northern Cape)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

NCOP House Sitting of 08 July 2025

The Select Committee held a meeting with the Minister of Basic Education and her team to provide Members with the implementation progress report on the Executive Undertakings made by the Minister during the National Council of Provinces plenary of 8 July 2025.

The Minister remarked that progress had been made across a number of the Undertakings. Regarding Early Childhood Development, the Bana Pele Mass Registration Drive exceeded the target number of applications and the review of the National Catalogue had been finalised with implementation planned for the 2027 academic year. Teacher training across all provinces in Mother Tongue-Based Bilingual Education was completed and bilingual learning materials had been distributed. In addition, there were thousands of bursaries awarded through the Funza Lushaka Bursary Scheme which strengthened the pipeline of qualified educators entering the system. Despite the progress made, significant challenges remained. These included fiscal constraints, infrastructure backlogs and uneven implementation across provinces. The Department also reported that learning outcomes remained a major concern and overall performance levels were still low compared to global standards.

Members were concerned about the Deputy Minister’s absence at the meeting, particularly because the Minister had to depart early. Concerns were also raised about whether the Department exercised adequate oversight over provinces where there were cases of unregistered schools continuing to operate and infrastructure delays exceeding three-year timelines. Other concerns included inequality in infrastructure investment between rural and urban schools, whether the mass registration programme was reaching rural areas, infrastructure misalignment that created gaps in the system, the involvement of learners in crime and reports of confidentiality breaches in the Department’s recruitment processes. Further concerns included incidents of food poisoning in schools and specific challenges facing boy learners who had higher dropout rates than girl learners. Members stressed the important role of constituency offices in disseminating information and asked for clarification on concepts such as Bronze and Silver levels of registration to be able to inform communities. The Committee was impressed with the progress made in Mother Tongue-Based Bilingual Education and undertook to assess its implementation during the constituency period.

Meeting report

Opening

The Chairperson greeted Members who were in attendance physically and those who had joined virtually. The Committee’s support staff were thanked for facilitating the meeting’s logistics. The Minister of Basic Education and her team from the Department of Basic Education (DBE) were welcomed.

Ms Siviwe Gwarube, Minister of Basic Education, greeted the Chairperson and advised that she would be leaving early due to a clash in programmes between the two Houses.

Mr Nkanyiso Mkhize, Committee Secretary, noted apologies from Mr M Billy (DA, KwaZulu-Natal) and Adv I Nonkonyana (ANC, Eastern Cape). Ms J Mananiso (ANC, Gauteng) would be joining the meeting late.

Ms O Medupe (ANC, North West) asked for reasons for the Deputy Minister’s absence.

The Chairperson said the Minister would provide opening remarks, after which Members could raise questions before the Minister departed.

Mr E Noe (ANC, Free State) agreed with Ms Medupe.

The Chairperson said the Committee’s role was two-fold. First, Members received petitions from communities across the country in various matters and second, the Committee dealt with executive undertakings made by Ministers. The purpose of the meeting was to engage with the Executive Undertakings made by Minister Gwarube during the National Council of Provinces (NCOP) plenary of 8 July 2025.

Minister’s opening remarks

Minister Gwarube said the Deputy Minister was in attendance at a different meeting. The understanding was that the NCOP did not accept Deputy Ministers representing Ministers in relation to Members’ questions. The view was that since the Executive Undertakings were made by Minister Gwarube herself, it was important for her to account for them.

The engagement with Members was welcomed. The Committee played a critical role in ensuring the translation of national commitments into measurable progress and delivery across provinces. The Executive Undertakings were practical commitments aimed at strengthening the basic education system, particularly Early Childhood Development (ECD), infrastructure, curriculum and learning materials, school nutrition, teacher development and language and learning outcomes. The Undertakings continued to guide the Department's work. They remained closely aligned to the broader priorities of the 7th Administration, including those outlined by President Ramaphosa in the State of the Nation Address.

Despite operating within a constrained fiscal environment, the Department made meaningful progress across a number of the Undertakings. Regarding ECD, the Bana Pele Mass Registration Drive yielded over 19 000 applications with more than 11 000 centres approved at Bronze level. This represented progress towards the Department’s commitment to expanding access to quality early learning. On the National Catalogue for Grades 1 to 3, the review process was finalised with procurement to take place this year and implementation planned for the 2027 academic year. This was critical to ensuring that learners received high-quality curriculum-aligned learning material and to support learning outcomes. Consultations on the review of infrastructure regulations and the development of disaster management guidelines were ongoing.

The National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) was being reformed to include a standardised procurement process, enhanced monitoring systems and improved coordination across all levels of implementation. Regarding Mother Tongue-Based Bilingual Education (MTBBE), teacher training across all provinces was completed and bilingual learning materials were distributed. These efforts were supported by targeted funding and were essential to improving literacy and learning outcomes, particularly in the foundation phase. On the Funza Lushaka Bursary Scheme, thousands of bursaries were awarded in priority areas such as Mathematics and Science. This strengthened the pipeline of qualified educators entering the system.

While progress had been made, there were significant challenges regarding fiscal constraints, infrastructure backlogs and uneven implementation across provinces. Addressing the challenges would require continued collaboration between spheres of government, strengthened oversight and discipline implementation. The challenges were continuously raised with the Council of Education Ministers (CEM) particularly when issues were identified in specific provinces. The Department remained committed to implementing the Undertakings and welcomed the Committee’s role in ensuring accountability.

Discussion

Mr Noe thanked the Minister for her overview which had referred to the commitment to strengthen oversight. What was the Minister’s view of the meaning of oversight and how was it implemented? Oversight remained a significant challenge. As public representatives with constituency offices, Members regularly engaged with communities and came across matters affecting local, provincial and national departments. There were two matters to raise. The first was related to private schools, which the Department’s engagements were often silent on. There was a private school in Harrismith that had been operating since 2018. The district focused on that school’s unregistered status and its occupation of government buildings; however, it was not providing any assistance to the school, which had continued to enrol learners. Now, the school was facing eviction despite paying rent. What would happen to the children who were attending classes? Further, the district advised that the driver of the school bus had died. There was no new driver and young children were frustrated because it was raining. There was no update from the district on this and the communication had been poor.

Mr Noe said the second matter related to the significant delay in a school infrastructure project. Construction had exceeded its timeline by three years with little visible progress, despite ongoing engagements with Members of the Executive Council (MECs) for Public Works and Infrastructure and Education. In the meantime, learners had been forced to remain in temporary structures for over ten years. What does oversight mean under these circumstances?

The Chairperson said the bulk of the work was done at provincial level. It was reported that there were collaboration efforts with CEM. Could the Minister provide Members with a high-level understanding of how provinces were supporting key reforms, particularly the NSNP rollout and the mass registration of ECD centres?

Minister Gwarube asked Mr Noe to share the details of both schools, including the names, districts and nature of the issues, to enable the Department to escalate it to the relevant MECs. Regarding oversight, it was a concurrent function shared between national and provincial governments. While provinces did the bulk work of implementation and service delivery, the national Department played a coordinating role. This included engaging provinces when issues were raised by Parliament and ensuring that responses were provided to Members.

The concurrent arrangement required collaboration and partnership rather than a top-down approach. MECs reported to their respective Premiers and not to the Minister. CEM was an important mechanism to facilitate robust discussions with provinces on delays in infrastructure delivery, underperformance and other challenges. The Department was responsible for setting policy, legislation and norms and standards for provinces to buy into. Maintaining a collegial working relationship with provinces was essential. Although there were challenges, such as the financial issues in KwaZulu-Natal, there was generally a strong working relationship with all stakeholders, who remained aligned in the shared goal of serving 13.7 million learners in the country. Any issues among stakeholders were resolved through communication channels.

DBE briefing: progress report on Executive Undertakings

Dr Stephen Taylor, Director: Research, Monitoring and Evaluation, DBE, reported that more learners attended school, fewer dropped out and efficiency in the system had improved. Policies, such as no-fee schools and the NSNP, contributed to greater equity and support for disadvantaged learners. Despite these improvements, learning outcomes remained a major concern and overall performance levels were still low compared to global standards. Financial constraints required the prioritisation of interventions to directly improve learning outcomes.

Turning to the Executive Undertakings, the Department’s progress included the following:

Undertaking 1: Registration of 10 000 ECD centres

Through the Bana Pele Mass Registration Drive, 19 376 registration applications were received, exceeding the target of 10 000. Out of the 19 376 applications, 14 442 were completed and 11 347 were approved at Bronze level. The Department was currently preparing for Silver and Gold levels.

Undertaking 2: Updating of National Catalogue for Grade 1 to 3

The review of the National Catalogue had been finalised. Procurement would take place in 2026 and implementation was planned for the 2027 academic year.

Undertaking 3: Review of Public School Infrastructure Regulations

The review process was supported by a dedicated task team and external stakeholder engagements were in progress.

Undertaking 4: Development of Disaster Management Guidelines

The guidelines were at consultation stage with relevant stakeholders. Once approved, they would be published for public comment.

Undertaking 5: Review of provincial implementation models of the NSNP

A reform process was initiated to standardise the NSNP across all provinces. The project plan, focused on procurement reforms and digital monitoring systems, would be finalised by the end of the 2025/26 financial year.

Undertaking 6: Allocation of R162 million for ECD infrastructure in poor communities

The funding was used to conduct minor infrastructure upgrades in conditionally registered ECD centres, construct new facilities in high-demand areas and provide health and safety support.

Undertaking 7: Allocation of R57 million for MTBBE

On teacher training and capacity development, the medium term focus was to extend teacher orientation to Grades 5, 6 and 7 and to implement continuous professional development through digital platforms. Regarding assessment and learning outcomes, bilingual Grade 4 assessments were completed and preparatory work for Grade 5 assessments were underway.

Undertaking 8: Funza Lushaka Bursary Scheme

In quarter one, 8397 bursaries were awarded. This figure had increased to 8647 in quarter two. The bursaries were awarded to foundation phase, intermediate phase and senior phase within the Bachelor of Education degree.

Discussion

Ms Medupe welcomed the presentation and the extensive work undertaken by the Department to implement its commitments. Regarding the Deputy Minister’s non-attendance, Minister Gwarube’s understanding was incorrect. In the absence of the Minister, the Deputy Minister must be part of the meeting because there must be political ownership for any questions directed to the Department. Although the Minister herself made the Undertakings before the House, those Undertakings guided the Department and not the individual. Undertakings had financial implications in the Department’s Annual Performance Plans.

Ms Medupe said the progress made regarding Undertaking 7 was commendable. How had the Department ensured that infrastructure investment directly addressed budget inequality in rural and township areas? How would the revised infrastructure regulations improve accountability in provinces that were responsible for infrastructure, delivery and maintenance? Minister Gwarube raised concerns about the Department’s limited enforcement authority in particular provinces. How would the revised regulations be of assistance? What measures were being put in place to ensure that bilingual education would not deepen inequalities between well-resourced and under-resourced schools? Regarding the NSNP, had a recent independent assessment on meal quality been conducted? If so, what were the assessment’s findings?

Mr P Phala (ANC, Limpopo) thanked the Department for its presentation. The work of the Department was important to change the fate of the country and shape young people into the country’s next generation of leaders. The Department presented on sector priorities and long-term plans. This involved the commitment to ensuring that no learner was left behind with a focus on access, equality, inclusivity and quality. However, there was a matter which was not included. This was the encouragement of patriotism in learners.

Mr Phala said that during the Joint Standing Committee on Defence’s meeting last week, an important question was raised on infrastructure misalignment. There were a number of young children who had fallen into cracks created by misalignment and were involved in crime. What would the Department do to reduce those cracks which children fell into? The Department and Members needed to work as a team to ensure the reduction of crime committed by young people. Last year, Members had visited Pollsmoor Prison which was filled with young people who were meant to be the country’s future leaders. In one of the meetings with the Minister of Correctional Services, it was recommended that more money would be required to build more prisons. Instead of building more prisons, could the focus be on building more schools?

Mr N Gotsell (DA, Western Cape) said the Minister had made a comment last week that it was just as important to focus on children being able to read by the age of six as it was to focus on those who matriculated. What indicators could Parliament use to ensure there were more children reading by the age of six? On system capacity, as ECD registration and foundational learning programmes expanded, what assurances could be provided to Members that provinces had the capacity, staff, resources, materials and monitoring to translate access into real improvements in literacy and numeracy outcomes? Regarding governance and recruitment integrity, there were alarming news reports of interference and confidentiality breaches in the Department’s recruitment processes. What safeguards were in place to protect the integrity of appointments and ensure that the implementation of the Undertakings was not compromised?

Mr Noe welcomed the Department’s presentation. It gave hope that serious work was undertaken to implement the Undertakings. It also indicated that work was needed in areas outside of the Undertakings. Regarding the presentation’s layout, diagrams were useful in workshops but were often difficult to follow in Committee engagements. Members appreciated the detailed explanations behind the diagrams more than the diagrams themselves, which were confusing to those not in the system. The development of the eCare platform was encouraging. However, rural communities often had limited access to technology. To what extent was the Department ensuring that not only urban areas would benefit from the eCare system? What was being done to ensure that rural areas also benefited? Parliamentary constituency offices disseminated information to the public. It was important for the Department, when presenting, to recognise these offices. Could the Department explain what Bronze and Silver levels of registration meant? Members needed to be able to explain it to communities.

Mr Noe said the presentation did not name the non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and forums that worked with the Department. Could the names and nature of the organisations be provided, including their roles played at local level? The presentation also did not elaborate on the National Catalogue. What does it entail? It was good that the Department was ensuring alignment between the status and registration of ECD centres. What was the Department’s position on unregistered private schools? The private school in Harrismith has been in operation since 2018, but was not registered. It continued to teach and enrol learners. Were such operations allowed? If not, who bore the responsibility of enforcement? The district complained about the school but did not stop enrolment. It was important to protect learners from potentially non-compliant schools. The progress made on MTBBE was impressive. Members would be on constituency next week. What should Members observe when visiting a Grade 4 classroom to confirm that MTBBE was being implemented?

The Chairperson asked the Department to outline the main challenges it experienced with the mass registration programme, especially in hard-to-reach rural and farm areas. How was the Department reaching rural areas to ensure their registration? Rural areas were part and parcel of infrastructure development. They were also in the mainstream because Grade R was now compulsory. Many ECD practitioners had been operating without formal training and necessary qualifications. The partnership with the University of South Africa (UNISA) to upskill these practitioners and the efforts to professionalise the sector were welcomed. It was important to prepare children, as young as two years old, in basic numeracy and literacy. That was the right way to develop the nation. Once the foundation was strengthened and the progression of learners was ensured, it would ultimately provide a better result. A strong and solid foundation in the early years was critical for educational success.

The Chairperson said that during oversight visits, Members were informed about the Department’s policy that learners were not allowed to repeat a Grade twice and were progressed to the next Grade. There may be a number of reasons for the policy, such as age considerations, but it was concerning because children progressed through the system without being sufficiently prepared. This would ultimately create challenges at the higher education level. Regarding accessibility to information, most communities were unaware of teaching opportunities and bridging programmes offered by UNISA. How did the Department make these opportunities accessible to communities? On the Funza Lushaka Bursary Scheme, how did the application process work? Did students first apply directly to the Department and then apply to universities? How was the Scheme administered? This information was important for Members to share with constituencies and communities.

Ms Mananiso asked who had benefited locally from the Bana Pele Mass Registration Drive. Were there any backlog registration cases and could reports on those cases be provided to the Committee? On the multilingualism programme, could the Department provide more detailed information on how the programme was being implemented in practice? Regarding the mitigation measures outlined in the presentation, could the Department provide greater detail on which beneficiaries were being targeted across different contexts and communities? In addition, could Members be provided with more information and details on the ECD infrastructure programme? Where were the 22 targeted centres located and how were they selected?

DBE responses

Ms Simone Geyer, Deputy Director-General (DDG): Delivery and Support, DBE, replied that the Department’s response to the question regarding confidentiality and recruitment processes would be provided in writing. On the ECD programme, attention was being shifted away from Grade R and Grade 1 to focus on children aged between 0 and 4 years old, so that early learners developed foundational skills before entering formal schooling. There was a large amount of funding required for this. When the ECD function was transferred from the Department of Social Development on 1 April 2022, DBE inherited a model that was largely community-driven, providing care and employment opportunities, especially for unemployed women. The model lacked monitoring, speeding up of ECD centres’ registrations and a focus on early learning outcomes. DBE was working to strengthen the model without disrupting the livelihoods it supported. One of the key priorities was improving registration and extending data systems to ensure the capturing of more centres in the system. Registration had been accelerated and last year’s target was exceeded.

Ms Geyer replied that the Bronze level included centres that were captured in the system. Silver level included centres that had high levels of compliance and qualified for subsidies. Registration allowed the Department to identify where centres were located to understand their needs and to allocate resources. The Department was extending its schooling data systems to include ECD centres. The lack of formal land ownership in rural areas complicated compliance requirements and the Department was working with other units to find practical solutions in informal settings. On partnerships, the Department worked with a list of NGOs and donors, including large international partners and smaller local organisations. This list could be provided to the Committee. The Department was using registration data to direct support where it was most needed. The per-child subsidies had increased to R24 per day and were used by centres for nutrition. Resource packs were also being provided to learners, which could be used over a period of time.

Dr Granville Whittle, DDG: Social Mobilisation and Support Services, DBE, responded that the Department promoted national identity by providing national flags to schools and teaching communities how to raise flags. School events and activities opened with the singing of the national anthem and this was being extended to include the African Union anthem. A competition on the singing of the national anthem was also introduced in choral competitions. In addition, learners were taught to recite the preamble of the Constitution. A few years ago, the My Country South Africa book was developed for learners to understand national symbols and heritage. There was no budget to continue the project but this book could be provided to the Committee.

Dr Whittle replied that boys tended to fall behind as early as age five, struggled through the system and often dropped out in Grade 10. Boys were also involved in crime and this had to do with the punishment of boys at home. Although corporal punishment was banned in 1996, it remained the biggest category of violence in schools. There were more girls passing the National Senior Certificate and attending higher education. While support for girls must continue, there is a need to address the challenges facing boys.

Dr Whittle said that independent assessments on meal quality were limited due to the affordability of service providers. However, the Department tried to work with partners who would provide some funding to conduct assessments. Meal quality inconsistencies often arose because service providers prioritised cheaper products. To address this, the Department was introducing a standardised product catalogue which would specify approved food items that met quality standards. The product catalogue was being piloted in one of the provinces. The Department needed to constantly improve its monitoring because when unannounced inspections took place, they revealed that there was poor-quality food at schools.

Mr Ramasedi Mafoko, Acting Chief Director: Infrastructure Planning and Delivery, DBE, requested the school’s name from Mr Noe to allow the Department to investigate the three-year construction delay and provide a response on the challenges affecting the project. Regarding inequality in infrastructure investment, there was no differentiation between rural, township or urban schools in national norms and standards. All learners, regardless of location, should have access to the same quality of infrastructure and school buildings. However, historical disparities, such as older schools in disadvantaged areas, still reflect lower standards. Population movement from rural to urban areas was a current challenge affecting infrastructure planning. There were currently 22 000 public schools, 379 000 classrooms and a shortage of 43 677 schools. The learner-classroom ratio was 33 to 6. As a result, there was a surplus of classrooms to accommodate all learners. The problem was that the available classrooms were located in the wrong places. Families were moving from rural areas to urban areas for economic opportunities. Many schools were closing down in rural areas leaving classrooms empty and resulting in overcrowding in urban areas.

Mr Mafoko replied that one of the ways in which accountability and oversight over provinces was enforced was through provinces’ annual infrastructure plans. At the end of each financial year, provinces must report on their performance against the targets set in their plans, provide reasons for any shortfalls and timelines for the completion of outstanding projects. The Department monitored financial performance and delivery progress on a regular basis throughout the year. The Department was moving towards establishing a dedicated unit to improve communication between provinces and communities because provinces were slow to respond to communities’ concerns.

Mr Enoch Rabotapi, Acting DDG: Teachers, Education Human Resources and Institutional Development, DBE, replied that the UNISA partnership applied to Grade R teachers and not ECD teachers. The partnership aimed to support historically underqualified Grade R teachers to achieve the same qualification levels as other school teachers. Regarding the Funza Lushaka Bursary Scheme, outreach was conducted through social media platforms to advertise the Scheme. There were also two types of recruitment programmes. The first was district-based which targeted current Grade R teachers and encouraged them to pursue teaching as a profession. The second was community-based which focused on young people outside the school system who were interested in teaching. Through these initiatives, the Scheme attracted a large number of applications. The application process required candidates to first apply to universities and once accepted, they could then apply to the Scheme.

Mr Xhanti Mhlubulwana, Chief Director: National Language Unit, DBE, replied that during 2025, MTBBE had been implemented nationally for Grade 4 in 11 948 schools. The highest uptake was in the Eastern Cape, followed by KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and Mpumalanga. In teaching Mathematics and Natural Sciences bilingually, the model for 2025 was 80% home language and 20% English language for Grade 4. For 2026, in Grade 5, the model would be 70% home language and 30% English language. By Grade 7 in 2028, learners would be fully bilingual with a 50% split. This approach aimed to improve access to Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics by ensuring that learners engaged with concepts in a language they understood. Standardised bilingual terminology, specific to Mathematics and Natural Sciences, had been developed across all official languages.

Mr Mhlubulwana replied that MTBBE was a system-wide curriculum delivery platform integrated into annual teaching plans, worksheets and workbooks. The Pan South African Language Board (PanSALB) played a critical role in maintaining linguistic integrity, standardising terminology and certifying the use of terminology. The Department could not serve as both a referee and a player. PanSALB played a huge role in planning. Regarding key risks and mitigation, terminology standardisation and distribution of materials were priorities. Mitigation measures included accelerating verification up to Grade 7 within this year and providing hard copies of educational resources for Mathematics and Natural Sciences while ensuring digital access to the resources. Lessons from Grade 4 assessments conducted in November 2025 were being analysed with findings expected by 31 March 2026.

Further discussion

Mr Noe said the MTBBE programme was very interesting and exciting. He would be visiting each and every school in his area to assess its implementation. He had promised the principal of the school in Harrismith to follow up. It was frustrating when those who were accountable were not responding. Regarding the corporal punishment policy, it appeared that the policy was implemented without regard to feedback. One contributing factor to boys falling behind in schools was the absence of a family unit, particularly of fathers, which meant that boys were growing up without role models. Mothers also felt the absence of fathers. The other contributing factor was child pregnancy. It made boys become fathers who felt they no longer needed to attend school and rather find work. The use of drugs and lack of recreational facilities in rural areas were also contributing factors. Children who arrived late at school had to stay at the gate until 12h00. That was a form of punishment. If teachers opened the gate and children had to clean up the garden as punishment, children were comfortable waking up late. Members needed to engage on the issue of corporal punishment.

Regarding NSNP, prior food poisoning incidents were concerning. Improvements to meal quality and the quality of food sold near schools were needed. Lastly, the My Country South Africa book would be a valuable tool to foster patriotism.

The Chairperson said that written responses would be provided to some of the questions raised by Members.

Ms Medupe asked for written responses on the findings of independent assessments that were conducted on meal quality.

Dr Whittle replied that there had never been any food poisoning in schools linked to food supplied by the Department. All past incidents were traced to external sources. In response, the Department had strengthened its monitoring and quality control measures to be able to deal with those incidents. Written responses would be provided on the specific school infrastructure project.

Mr Mhlubulwana replied that parliamentary oversight was important in strengthening the implementation of MTBBE. The Department would share with Members the list of schools that were currently implementing the bilingual programme. Parliamentary oversight would mean observing bilingual teaching in Mathematics and Natural Sciences, availability of learning materials and evidence of learner engagement and participation. These observations would form part of the Department’s follow-up actions.

Closing remarks

The Chairperson thanked the Department for its cooperation and commitment to providing written responses. Members and parliamentary support staff were thanked for their continued commitment to the work of the Committee.

The meeting was adjourned.
