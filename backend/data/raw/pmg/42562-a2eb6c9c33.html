Ad Hoc Committee on Gen Mkhwanazi's Allegations

The Ad Hoc Committee heard oral evidence from Lieutenant General Francinah Ntombenhle Vuma who joined the South African Police Service (SAPS) in 1987 and served until her retirement in February 2026 after a suspension beginning in July 2022. She had held the position of Deputy National Commissioner since December 2017.

Lt Gen Vuma described her suspension for alleged breaches of the Independent Police Investigative Directorate (IPID) Act. She said she conducted multiple investigations into irregularities and misconduct within SAPS, targeting senior officials suspected of wrongdoing. These investigations covered cases such as irregular contracts, recruitment process complaints, and forensic audits. After submitting protected disclosures about her findings, Lt Gen Vuma faced harassment and victimisation within the SAPS.

She identified interference and pressure from senior police leadership and political figures who attempted to silence her, including efforts to have her sign non-disclosure agreements. Lt Gen Vuma rejected these attempts, maintaining her integrity despite adverse consequences. She highlighted abuses within the SAPS disciplinary system, which lacked provisions for adequate legal representation, causing her significant personal and financial hardship.

The Committee engaged with Lt Gen Vuma's detailed testimony and discussed parallel disciplinary and legal actions. She warned about systemic problems in SAPS leadership and corruption, requesting urgent reforms by Parliament to rebuild public confidence and the effectiveness of the criminal justice system. Lt Gen Vuma called on the committee and Parliament to implement fundamental changes to restore integrity and trust in law enforcement institutions in South Africa.

The entire process was followed by Lieutenant General Jacobs because he needed to apply to the National Treasurer when the National Commissioner endorsed his signature and supported it. The only challenge arose after the authorisation. Remember, you cannot request an exemption certificate if you do not have authorisation and approvals to purchase.

The process needed to be followed to obtain those authorisations so that when the stock arrived, they were ready to implement. Hence, I am coupling both of them: the Minister had a responsibility to facilitate those exemption certificates because he had to liaise with the other State Department in that regard.

Mr Skosana: Now, as a result, that equipment is lying somewhere unused. It is like it is a waste now.

Lt-Gen Vuma: They were lying somewhere, but they started to use them, I think, in 2022. So, they were unused from 2020 to 2021, and almost two years and a couple of months.

Mr Skosana: Now, what investigation did you conduct against General Jacobs?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I conducted the investigation against General Jacobs regarding the circumstances under which Lieutenant General Kinnear was killed and his protection withdrawn. I also conducted an investigation regarding their purchasing PPE utilising the Secret Service account, which is normally referred to as the slush fund.

Mr Skosana: So, what were the findings against them?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The recommendations were for disciplinary hearings for his actual omission in those matters, which I did not even attach because they were classified, but I produced those reports.

Mr Skosana: Why do you then allege that there was ministerial interference in such an investigation? What happened?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The minister wrote in black and white to the National Commissioner to say he must put those investigational suspensions of Lieutenant General Jacobs in abeyance until, I do not know, what he wanted to do. The annexures are attached to that record.

Mr Skosana: Now, you told us that you, as well as an investigator, became a victim. How so?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The minister called me to meetings and complained to me that, yeah, why did you recommend that General Jacobs must be taken to disciplinary hearings? You should not have done that. In fact, according to him, General Mfazi and General Sibiya should have been the ones taken to those disciplinary hearings.

I had to explain to the minister that, no, Minister, when I conduct an investigation, I can safely assure you no one can coerce me to change my recommendation because I obtain statements, I interview people, I analyse documents. So, based on that, this is why I came up with those recommendations.

Mr Skosana: Then what ultimately happened to that investigation?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Those investigations, General Jacobs took them to SSSBC for the matters to be allocated to an independent commissioner because he relied on a document which was once submitted by Kinnear, where he regarded this also as part of the protected disclosure.

Now, while those cases sat in SSSBC since that time up until last year, I think it was in September or October, I cannot remember exactly, the annexure is attached where the National Commissioner withdrew those cases. He was never subjected to a hearing regarding those matters.

Mr Skosana: Now, let us go to the removal of General Nkosi. What was the role of this position called audit executive?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The audit executive is the role, it is the, I am trying to think now, my mind is getting tired. It is the tool in the organisation that is used by mostly all organisations to have an inward look at processes, prescripts and, amongst others, detect irregularities within the organisation before external auditors come into the organisation.

It is a tool to assist the National Commissioner to ensure assurance within his areas of responsibility.

Mr Skosana: Now, I think there was an issue about General Nkosi. Was he qualified for that position?

Lt-Gen Vuma: According to the report that was submitted to the subs, at the time he was appointed he did not have the prerequisite qualifications to be appointed to the post. As a result, the Public Service Commission recommended disciplinary measures to the panel that appointed him. And she was recommended to be demoted to a level of prejudice.

Mr Skosana: So why was the minister not happy about that?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The minister was not happy because General Nkosi was one of the minister's close allies. As such, if there was an audit conducted at the supply chain regarding the PPE, when an audit is conducted before a final report is submitted, the business unit that has been audited sits together with the auditors.

Many times, some of these auditors are young people still in the internship programme who do not have experience and do not understand some of the prescripts and policies.

So there is engagement to discuss the outcome of the audit so that they are able to clear some of the matters and leave those that are genuine. There was an audit which General Nkosi's office leaked to the media for the SAPS.

That audit highlighted R1.6 billion irregular expenditure in the SAPS for PPEs. Now, it was leaked before, while it was still in a draft format. As time progressed, the auditors had an opportunity to engage supply chain, and almost most of those presumed suspected irregularities were addressed.

Based on that media leak, the PCOP for the police invited us to present to the portfolio committee on that irregular expenditure.

Mr Skosana: Now, what step, because there should be some consequence management, what step, if any, or mechanism was taken against General Mgwenya for participating in this appointment of General Mkhwanazi?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I only became aware of that report of the Public Service Commission when I was busy with the matching and placing in the SAPS. I became aware because there was a reminder or a letter from the Public Service Commission wanting to know how far we had been able to implement their recommendations.

That is when I requested the report. Unfortunately, during that time, Lieutenant General Mgwenya was already dismissed from the service.

Mr Skosana: But who is General Mgwenya in short? Because I think when we had Lieutenant General Sibiya, her name came up here as well. If I am right, I think she has a baby or a child, if I am correct.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Yeah, as far as my information is concerned, they have a child together, a son, General Mgwenya and General Sibiya.

Mr Skosana: So she was working in the province or was she at head office?

Lt-Gen Vuma: She was a Lieutenant General who worked at head office as a Deputy National Commissioner. She was responsible for human resource management.

Mr Skosana: Now, when it comes to the issue of PPE, was she the one who authorised procurement of PPEs at that time when we had that problem? As you said, the Treasury had to be involved. You had to go to the Chinese to make sure there was stock.

But was she the one authorised to do this PPE procurement to General Reit, if I remember?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I cannot say that she was authorised to do that because General Reit was liaising constantly with National Treasury. We had to implement the directives as published and submitted to all government departments by National Treasury. Hence, most of the suppliers came from them, even though they granted us blanket authority that based on the shortage we could explore.

Mr Skosana: Okay. Now, earlier, you mentioned the issue of Minister Bheki Cele. But at that time, he was a minister. Now, you said to us here that there were names given to you and other members. I think you mentioned one member, if I remember. A list of suppliers for these PPEs. And you were forcefully pushed into a corner to accept that list.

Do you still have that list or the names? Maybe you can mention a few of those names, if you have.

Lt-Gen Vuma: I do not have a list. But if it is required, General Reit can be able to give it to me, even though he has since retired. But I am sure he can be able to.

Mr Skosana: I guess the records are still there.

Lt-Gen Vuma: They are still available, yes. I have one here which I mentioned earlier on. It is a skincare and whatever.

Mr Skosana: Now, you told us that you were victimised for not appointing these companies which were given by General Mgwenya. The minister as well was very upset, Minister Cele, because things did not go her way.

Apparently, as you told us, you wanted the processes to be followed to the T. The issue of you being victimised, in your view, for you to sit at home for three and a half years, derived from you refusing to toe the line. Am I right?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Yes.

Mr Skosana: Now, what was the response from General Mgwenya when Minister Cele berated you in their presence for not appointing those companies, the list which they gave to you?

Lt-Gen Vuma: It was a very bad and hectic meeting in the office of the minister. We were berated. It was not a nice meeting, I must say.

Mr Skosana: Did you take any steps to report this matter or escalate it to the National Commissioner? Because if I remember, the National Commissioner was not part of that meeting.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Yes. Immediately when we left, in fact while I was still there, I sent a message on WhatsApp to the National Commissioner: please come and attend this meeting. Myself and General Reit were going back to the office.

Fortunately, while I was driving in the basement, I met the National Commissioner parking there, and I gave him full details of what had happened.

Mr Skosana: Then what were the circumstances that led to the dismissal of General Mgwenya?

Lt-Gen Vuma: General Mgwenya was disciplined. She was found to have been involved in the contract of the blue lights, with all the people appearing in court in that particular matter.

Mr Skosana: Now, on that, there was an allegation that Brigadier Ramanjalum said you wrote a letter exonerating him from arrest. Is that true?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Do the blue lights matter? Yes. It is not true because he is still appearing with them.

Mr Skosana: Yes. Hence, I want you to clarify, because there are a lot of things going on. When we sit in the Police Portfolio Committee, we receive many complaints.

Now, Minister Cele was not happy about you, in particular, the outcome which led to the dismissal of General Mgwenya. Am I right?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Yes.

Mr Skosana: This incident led you to again be victimised by Minister Cele, in your view?

Lt-Gen Vuma: In my view, yes.

Mr Skosana: Now, let us come to the issue of General Jacobs. What investigation did you conduct against General Jacobs?

Lt-Gen Vuma: It is the two investigations, the one involving Lieutenant Colonel Kinnear and the one involving the purchasing of PPE.

Mr Skosana: Then what was the finding, if I may ask?

Lt-Gen Vuma: The finding recommended that General Jacobs had a case to answer. He needed to be subjected to a disciplinary process.

Mr Skosana: Now, before I close, Chair, I just want to find out from General Vuma, in your view, General Mkhwanazi's press conference on the 6th of July, was it a milestone? Was it something that would help this country, particularly the security cluster, both the Department of Justice, though we had our own shortfalls in terms of bringing magistrates and judges, and we had prosecutors here.

But in your opinion, in closing, do you think General Mkhwanazi, being a provincial commissioner, has helped this country to heal and reset, particularly the South African Police Service, that we must maintain the integrity of men and women in blue and ensure whatever happened must never repeat itself? What is your opinion? Was he right in doing what he did, or do you have mixed feelings?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Thank you very much, Honourable Chair. I think, as I said earlier, my analysis of what General Mkhwanazi did is that to a certain extent, it has assisted the country. It was a moment of saying to the country, hold on, what is happening here? Especially in the criminal justice system.

We are seeing many things, some very scary. People are getting killed for testifying. We are seeing a lot of rot which we were not aware of. We were seeing certain minor things we were exposed to, but the more we sit and look, we realise there are bigger problems in the country than we expected.

However, I must say that each person is unique. I also felt, as you said, in terms of mixed feelings, that maybe the approach of the media briefing had an impact on the political sphere of the country, as well as the government, making it look to the outside world as if they are not in touch with the criminal justice system.

To me, it posed a particular risk, the approach. However, I am not saying it is wrong.

Mr Skosana: No, I understand you. Because for some people, it felt completely wrong. They were even mentioning the uniform, which, for me, is childish and immature. Because he was wearing a special task force uniform, there is nothing wrong with the uniform or the posture.

But for me, from where I am sitting, this country is facing serious issues in terms of drugs. If you go to our rural areas, let me make a disclaimer here. General Vuma, I came a little late into the police force in 1994. Now, where you used to be at Siyabuswa, you know Siyabuswa?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I started there.

Mr Skosana: Yes. I was kept in that police station cell. I think it is a 30-square-metre police station. In 1986, I was kept there. I will never forget that. I was transferred to other police stations.

Today, when you go to Umhlanga, when you go to Kwaggafontein, go to Siyabuswa and other areas, you will cry. On every street corner, there are children. Drugs have completely destroyed our kids.

We need men and women in uniform to help, and society as well. We are not blaming them. I do not blame the police because, as parents, we must also take responsibility. We cannot delegate our responsibility to the police. We must work together. There must be social cohesion.

I am saying, please, as well, you have been there for 39 years. Please encourage those young men and women who want advice from you to make sure that the integrity of the South African Police Service is restored. I thank you. Thank you, Chair.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Thank you, Honourable Chairperson. Thank you, members.

Chairperson: General Vuma, you have been in the SAPS for 38 years plus. Am I correct?

Lt-Gen Vuma: It is correct, Honourable Chair.

Chairperson: In the many years that you have worked in the SAPS, because I think now you are not afraid of career-limiting statements. You are a patient. In the many years that you have been there, can you say to us that I have not observed corrupt behaviour within the system, or I have observed corrupt behaviour?

That is what we are investigating. We want to check if there are corrupt practices, and some of them would be those that you would have alleged here today which affected your personality. What is your response?

Lt-Gen Vuma: Thank you very much, Honourable Chairperson. In my career in the police, I have observed a lot of corruption, and I was exposed to corruption when I was a warrant officer. It is a long story, but I can summarise it.

While I was a warrant officer, also working under the very same environment of procurement, I had a few members reporting to me who were sergeants and constables. Now I had to take study leave. It was October, November. When I came back from study leave, I found, and I think due to my spiritual intuition, I went straight to the other phone books that were utilised for corruption, unaware. I started to conduct my audit and gave each and every one their books. I gave, for example, Ms Sangoni’s books. He ran on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday all over. He was not coming to the office. I would ask, where is so and so? They would say we saw him, but he is not here.

The following Monday, when we arrived in the office in the morning, we received a call that he shot himself at Kwaggafontein under a tree. He tried to hang himself with a tie. It could not work. Then he shot himself. I was still not aware what exactly happened until I was called by the commissioner. It was still KwaNdebele Police. When the commissioner called me, he said, man, go and check properly in your books. There are a lot of rumours that this person was carrying 40 000 cheques, 30-something thousand cheques. And those times that were a lot of money. I am sure it was like the value of a million.

Then I said, no man, I have checked all the books. He said, no, check. So we were not printing our own cheques. We had to go to the Department of Finance to print the cheques. I went there to check. What I found was amazing. More than a million in that one month was spent by this guy. Now interestingly, when he died he left a note. The note said, in every success of a man there is a woman behind. We assumed he was talking about his wife because he also had girlfriends. But as time progressed we discovered that I am the woman behind his downfall because I discovered this. He thought I had already discovered the whole thing, and it was not.

Now my life became a misery thereafter. It is a topic for another day.

Chairperson: So tell me, and I am asking you not the details, but based on the story that you have just shared with us, practices like that could also have happened elsewhere in the police service, not only confined to the environment where you could have been a senior with people reporting to you. They could have also replicated elsewhere in the system.

But to your knowledge, or to the whispers or the gossip that would have come around, were such activities known to the authorities? Maybe let us say I am a station commander at Madikwe Police Station.

In my position as a station commander, does it occur that I come to know that in a police station where I am the supervisor or the head, there is so and so who may be doing something? Even if evidence may not be conclusive, do we sometimes in the corridors hear such whispers?

For instance, in the position where you were as a senior ranking officer, does it occur that these whispers reach your ears?

Lt-Gen Vuma: It might be a challenge, but as a seasoned police officer it is easy to obtain such information and it is easy to dig into people without them understanding. I do not mean formally.

Chairperson: You know, when you are a coach of a football team, you can go to a party or a funeral elsewhere. And then one of the supporters of your team asks you, but coach, why do you never give Ramano a chance to play? Because the few moments that we saw him he is a good player.

So I am just saying, maybe from concerned or interested members of the public, when you walk on the streets, do you sometimes come across people who say, " But guys, we do not want to talk too much, but you are people?”

Lt-Gen Vuma: They do. They do. They do a lot.

Chairperson: And then how has it been difficult for the leadership of the police to act on such matters if those whispers come across?

Lt-Gen Vuma: They have been attended to. As I said earlier, Honourable Chairperson, SAPS is a very big organisation.

We also have a component in the division inspectorate which is responsible for complaints against the police. Some of the complaints come from a presidential hotline. I do not know if it is still there. It was there during the time of our previous president.

Those matters get investigated, and we equally utilise those reports to conduct awareness campaigns in different provinces and districts.

However, human beings are human beings. The road has become bigger than it used to be. I do not know, maybe part of the reasons might be what I said earlier on, when I was responding to the Honourable Day, that we have lost the moral fibre of our country, of the people of South Africa. We need to really go back to the basics.

Chairperson: And then you said the lieutenant generals are few. Most of them are divisional heads, correct?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I said they are few, most of them are?

Chairperson: Divisional heads.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Most of them are divisional.

Chairperson: I think you mentioned about 10.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Yes.

Chairperson: If you ask these honourable members, in the statements that they have, for sure by now there would be a sentence or a paragraph where all of those lieutenant generals have been mentioned before us here. I can, you see standing here, tell you a general so and so, there is this allegation about him. Another general so and so, there is this allegation about him. And the aggregate of it is that this committee has heard allegations about all those positions that you talk about here.

Of course, we may not say those allegations are true, or they have to be believed, but as a retired senior ranking officer, you are also coming here where there have been allegations and members have been asking you about them. Where does it put the image of an institution like the SAPS, particularly as a very important institution that must win the confidence of the people?

Lt-Gen Vuma: It puts it into very big disrepute. It means it cannot be trusted. The leadership is rotten. The list is endless.

Chairperson: If you were to advise us now, to say honourable members of this Parliament of the Republic, you may be left with a week now to conclude your business, this is the way that I leave with you. What are the fundamental interventions that you think this Parliament must make to reset the criminal justice system in the country?

You have told us about everything, where you say officials from IPID will even come to SAPS and want to advise you on where to proceed. This is alleged corrupt practices with regard to abuse of organisational authority. Where do we go from here? You have been there at senior management level. What is it that you think? One or two or three things. What has worked for us as a country and what is not working?

We do not want a situation where we wake up one day and there is load shedding in the country. If there is load shedding in the police, the country can go dark with the police service. Because once the lights come back there may be two or three people left in the country. We will no longer be a country again, so the lights can never go dark in the police service.

Lt-Gen Vuma: It is a difficult one. However, I think and I believe that there is a need for reflection in the organisation with deep analysis, trying to look at what went wrong, when did it start, how did it start, and really go deep with introspection at all levels. What might be the other influential factors that affected the organisation? What are the roles of leadership? The roles of leadership are also equally important, including parliamentarians and their contribution to these challenges that we are reflecting on.

From that introspection it will be necessary to come up with reforms based on the findings of those analyses. Come up with proposals and reforms and, if possible, these matters should also involve certain community leaders of credibility to assist in this process. It should not only be people from the criminal justice system or Parliament only. We also need leaders in our communities who can contribute towards this. There is definitely a need for reforms.

Chairperson: Adv Mkhize, you said you have one question, but being the benevolent Chair, even if you have five or ten it is still okay.

Adv Mkhize: I would not want to detain anybody any longer than they ought to be here.

Lieutenant General, my question just piggybacks on what Honourable Wessels asked you. You responded and said that you were responsible for supply chain. It fell under your division. If you would just please tell the committee, how do you deal with sponsorships, particularly for recognition awards? It was testified yesterday that service providers are approached by SAPS to provide sponsorship for recognition awards.

Lt-Gen Vuma: Thank you very much, Honourable Chair. In the police there is a policy which is referred to as Standing Order 58 that regulates the request and acceptance of gifts. Before any business unit approaches any company for sponsorship, an application must be made by that particular business unit and list all the service providers that you intend approaching, and it is referred to supply chain management.

Supply chain management must check whether those service providers are not rendering services to the police and do not have contracts, because you cannot have sponsorship from someone rendering services to you, as that would lead to corruption. Supply chain may, for example, out of 10 say two are fine, maybe eight we have contracts with them, so those are a no-no. They cannot be approached. Many times, the standing order avoids the monetary value of sponsorship. It requires a donation in kind. The donation should not be conditional. A company cannot donate and dictate where the donation must be used. After receiving sponsorship you must still apply to supply chain management for acceptance of the sponsorship based on this standing order, and once approved you can utilise the donation.

Adv Mkhize: Thank you, Lieutenant General.

Chairperson: Thank you so much, Adv. General, are you a strong personality?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I am very fragile, Honourable Chair.

Chairperson: So what kept you going after you were told that you were suspended? And how have you survived during the years of suspension?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I think it is my spiritual reliance that has kept me this far and also the support from my children and my family. I am also a very positive person. Even if I experience challenges I always believe that these things will pass. Maybe based on those values that even Jesus Christ went through trials and tribulations. If I am a follower of Jesus Christ, certainly I will be met by trials and tribulations and they will not stay with me forever. With God by my side nothing can destroy me.

Chairperson: And you still say again that there is nothing wrong you did.

Lt-Gen Vuma: I believe in that.

Chairperson: What is your message to many young South Africans or middle-aged South Africans who have been suspended from work because they refused to do wrong things?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I would say to them perseverance is the mother of success. They must persevere and continue to do positive things and find ways of addressing the challenges where possible.

They must knock on doors even when they believe those doors are locked.

Chairperson: Young South Africans who come to you and say, Mom, I refused to sign these documents and I was suspended. I was even told before that if you do not do this we will suspend you, and then that eventuality came.

Then the person comes to you and says, I saw you before Honourable Sauls, before Honourable Sangoni telling us the story of your life. How do you encourage such a victim to believe that one day the dark cloud shall lift?

Lt-Gen Vuma: I believe in some of the systems and bodies that this beautiful government of ours in South Africa has established. There are institutions still doing good work.

They must not believe that every door that is closed is locked. I will be able to advise them, assist them and refer them. We have institutions that take these cases and will be able to assist them.

Now I am a senior citizen. I can advise better. I am no longer afraid of anything. Thank you, Chairperson.

Chairperson: Thank you so much. We have come to the end of our proceedings with you.

Let me take this opportunity to formally thank you and also admire you for your fortitude. We are releasing you from the oath and thank you for staying until we release you.

Thank you. We have come to the end of the proceedings. Thank you so much, honourable members.

We are resuming at 10:00 tomorrow. Tomorrow we are at the NCOP building 512. We are starting at 10:00. We are going to break here at one o'clock and Parliament is sitting in the afternoon.

When the business of Parliament ends at five o'clock we will come here and work until the late hours.

Friday is bumper-to-bumper action from 10 o'clock until Saturday morning. We have four witnesses on Friday coming before us.

Honourable members, may you please stand up and leave the chambers.

The meeting was adjourned.
