Price Review of Food Supply Contracts (with Ministry)

Correctional Services

12 May 2026
Chairperson: Ms A Ramolobeng (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committee received a briefing from the Department of Correctional Services (DCS) on progress made on the review and renegotiation of prices under Contract H04/2023 for perishable and non-perishable food supply contracts across all six regions of the Department.

The Committee heard that DCS had identified multiple items procured at excessively inflated prices and had subsequently entered renegotiations with suppliers to reduce those prices. Examples presented to the Committee included cooking oil, gravy powder, flour, spices, cauliflower, and pawpaw. Members heard that one litre of cooking oil had previously been procured at prices ranging between R672 and R726 before being renegotiated down to between R26 and R29.

Members expressed serious concern about how such prices had initially passed internal procurement controls and argued that the issue was not merely the reduction of prices, but the apparent systemic failures within supply chain management systems that allowed irrational pricing to be approved.

The Committee was concerned about possible procurement manipulation and collusion between suppliers and departmental officials, considering the extent of the inflated pricing. Members questioned how irrational prices had initially passed procurement controls and stressed the need for stronger accountability and consequence management measures against officials found to have acted improperly. Concerns were raised about the absence in the presentation of clear information on disciplinary action, criminal referrals, recovery of funds, and the implementation of forensic investigations and lifestyle audits, especially for officials involved in supply chain management.

Members highlighted weaknesses in the Department’s supply chain management and internal control systems, arguing that the procurement failures exposed serious deficiencies in oversight, verification, and market benchmarking. The Committee cautioned that while the DCS move towards centralised procurement was intended to reduce fragmented abuse across correctional facilities, over-centralisation without adequate controls could simply centralise corruption on a larger scale.

The Committee asked if the Department possessed sufficient internal capacity to effectively manage and monitor the procurement review process, particularly following the reduction in the use of external consultants. Members raised concern on the Department’s continued dependence on external suppliers despite the existence of agricultural land, bakery infrastructure, and inmate labour capacity within correctional facilities. The Committee stressed the need for the Department to strengthen self-sufficiency initiatives, improve maintenance of bakery infrastructure, and develop a more sustainable internal food production system.

Members argued that the Department’s approach appeared reactive rather than preventative. They noted the Department’s description of procurement verification processes as “cumbersome” and emphasised that constitutional financial oversight obligations should never be viewed as administrative inconveniences.

The Committee requested detailed reports from the Department, including reports on disciplinary processes, criminal referrals, lifestyle audits, bakery infrastructure repairs, contract renegotiations, procurement controls, and other outstanding information by the end of May 2026.

The Committee resolved that DCS should return later in 2026 to provide a progress update on the renegotiation process and implementation of corrective measures.

Meeting report

The Chairperson welcomed the Minister, Deputy Minister, National Commissioner, DCS officials and stakeholders. She stated that the purpose of the meeting was for the Committee to receive a briefing from the Department on progress made in the review and renegotiation of prices for perishable and non-perishable contracts under Contract H04/2023.

Minister of Correctional Services Dr Pieter Groenewald stated that the Department appreciated the opportunity to account to Parliament on the ongoing procurement review process. The Department took the Committee’s oversight role seriously and acknowledged the public concern on pricing irregularities that had emerged within the contracts. DCS had initiated engagements with suppliers and internal reviews aimed at ensuring that food procurement pricing aligned more closely with prevailing market conditions.

Deputy Minister Lindiwe Ntshalintshala stated that the Department remained committed to strengthening accountability, improving supply chain management processes, and ensuring value for money in public expenditure. The Department recognised the seriousness of the issues raised publicly and would cooperate fully with parliamentary oversight processes.

Price Renegotiation Process: DCS presentation

Ms Dikeledi Molaba, DCS Director: Procurement Administration, stated that the price renegotiation process was continuous and followed engagements with suppliers across all six regions. The Department had identified several items with exorbitant pricing, including pawpaw, cauliflower, assorted spices, white flour, gravy powder, and pan-release oil. The presentation excluded bread pricing, which had already been presented to the Committee.

Contract HO4/2023 had been concluded with 115 service providers for the supply, delivery, and off-loading of perishable and non-perishable goods across all six regions of the department. The contract period commenced on 1 April 2025 and would continue until 31 March 2030.

The Department’s original procurement approach required all suppliers within the same region to charge identical prices for the same items. However, after implementation, several prices were later identified as excessive and significantly above the prevailing market prices. Contractual provisions permitted price reviews and renegotiations, which allowed DCS to engage suppliers for downward adjustments on excessively priced items.

The renegotiation process remained ongoing because additional items continued to be identified as overpriced. Negotiations were conducted in accordance with the Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), the Special Conditions of Contract (SCCs), and applicable procurement frameworks. Suppliers had agreed to significant price reductions on several items. Renegotiations were not based on Consumer Price Index (CPI) or Producer Price Index (PPI) formulas, but rather on direct market comparisons and prevailing market prices.

The revised prices were uniformly applied across suppliers within each region, consistent with the original pricing equality principle. All revised prices became effective during the 2025/26 financial year.

Current Internal Situation

Suppliers had already begun applying for further price increases during 2026. Approximately 4 600 transactions were currently under review by Supply Chain Management and Contract Management teams. The review process was extensive, tedious, and cumbersome due to the volume of transactions involved.

DCS had previously relied on external consultants during the development phase of the project, but due to cost-cutting measures and recommendations from the Portfolio Committee on reducing dependence on consultants, it was now conducting the process internally. It anticipated concluding the review process by 30 June 2026, with revised prices expected to take effect from 1 July 2026.

Special Conditions of Contract

The special conditions of the contract are that:
prices remained fixed for the first six months after contract signing;
prices could thereafter be reviewed every six months;
price adjustment applications were required to include documentary proof;
adjustments were ordinarily linked to CPI and PPI formulas; and
contractual formulas governed permissible price adjustments.

Conclusion

Negotiations with suppliers had produced substantial savings across all six regions.

The revised prices would provide financial relief to DCS over the remaining duration of the five-year contract.

However, rising fuel prices continued to place pressure on suppliers and complicated efforts to negotiate further downward price adjustments.

Following the presentation, the National Commissioner, Deputy Minister, and Minister indicated that they had no additional remarks and awaited Members’ questions.

Discussion

Mr M Gasa (MKP) raised extensive concerns on the procurement failures reflected in the presentation. He questioned how such inflated prices had initially passed procurement systems and if there had been deliberate collusion between suppliers and departmental officials. He referred to the extremely inflated costs of basic items such as cooking oil and gravy powder and questioned how ordinary market comparisons had not been conducted earlier. He asked if internal warnings had been ignored by management and if DCS had undertaken proper due diligence before approving the contracts.

Mr Gasa asked what mechanisms existed to prevent similar abuses from recurring under the renegotiated contracts. Did DCS possess sufficient internal supply chain management capacity to monitor procurement effectively without external consultants? He raised concern on the sustainability of the DCS procurement model and asked if greater self-sufficiency through internal production could reduce dependence on external suppliers.

Ms K Kgobisa-Ngcaba (DA) questioned the governance failures and oversight weaknesses. She asked why internal controls had failed to detect irrational pricing before contracts were approved and if consequence management had been initiated against officials responsible for approving the contracts. She asked how DCS intended to strengthen procurement verification moving forward and if National Treasury had been engaged on contract oversight.

She raised concerns on the DCS description of procurement verification processes as “cumbersome”, arguing that compliance obligations could not be treated as administrative inconveniences. She questioned if the DCS renegotiation process had been independently verified and if Parliament could be assured that the newly negotiated prices genuinely reflected prevailing market conditions.

Mr C Niehaus (EFF) raised concerns on accountability, contract management, and long-term procurement sustainability. He questioned how DCS intended to reduce reliance on external suppliers beyond bread production and if it planned to expand in-house production of other food items such as pawpaw and cauliflower. He questioned how ongoing payment delays to suppliers, which had been highlighted in the Auditor-General report, were affecting supplier relations, contract compliance, and the DCS negotiating position during future price reviews.

Mr Niehaus asked what key performance indicators and independent verification mechanisms would be implemented to ensure that the new prices taking effect from 1 July 2026 remained aligned with market realities throughout the remainder of the five-year contract.

He stated that the presentation left the Committee with a negative impression and described the DCS report as reactive rather than proactive. He argued that the Committee should demand a detailed written report within 30 days containing quantified savings, transaction-level data, evidence of market comparisons, and proof of compliance with Special Conditions of Contract clauses.

Mr Niehaus proposed that the Committee request an independent review by National Treasury or the Auditor-General to verify savings and compliance. He said DCS needed a detailed supply chain management capacity-building plan, including training, improved internal resource allocation, and clear timelines for institutionalising proper financial management systems, particularly given that many of the concerns had already repeatedly been flagged by the Auditor-General.

He further proposed that the Committee require progress reports on bakery expansion and feasibility studies into in-house production of additional perishables. He stressed the need for lifestyle audits, disciplinary processes, and criminal referrals for procurement irregularities and possible collusion. He also proposed that the Committee conduct six-monthly follow-up briefings aligned with contractual review cycles to monitor future price increase applications and supplier payment performance. He suggested that National Treasury become involved in contract oversight and that alternative procurement models be considered if procurement performance did not materially improve by the end of 2026.

Mr Niehaus stated that these interventions would assist in converting what he termed “reactive savings” into sustainable long-term value for money, while also strengthening public accountability and protecting inmate nutrition.

Mr E Hendricks (PA) welcomed the DCS presentation and raised a set of focused technical questions on procurement compliance and pricing methodology. He questioned how the department justifies the use of market-based renegotiation of prices when Clause 38 of the Special Conditions of Contract prescribes CPI and PPI as the basis for price adjustments, seeking clarity if deviation from the contractual formula is legally and procedurally sound. He asked if DCS had obtained legal advice confirming that such market-based renegotiations would not expose it to litigation or contractual disputes, particularly from suppliers who may argue non-compliance with agreed contract terms.

Mr Hendricks referred to the uniform pricing across regions, asking how DCS ensures that this approach does not disadvantage smaller or rural suppliers with higher logistical costs, potentially affecting fair competition. He requested clarity on how DCS determined that certain prices were exorbitant, including what objective benchmarks, market data, or comparative analyses were used to assess pricing levels. He asked if any suppliers had been questioned or penalised for submitting prices significantly above market norms, and what safeguards exist to ensure that uniform pricing structures do not inadvertently enable price coordination or collusion among suppliers.

Mr M Moela (ANC) welcomed the presentation but raised detailed concerns on procurement transparency and accountability. He requested clarity on specific items referenced in slide 7, particularly where suppliers were reportedly reapplying for price increases, and asked DCS to specify which goods are affected and what itemised reasons suppliers have provided, noting that broad references to fuel and transport costs are insufficient without detailed breakdowns. He challenged the language used in the presentation, particularly the description of procurement processes as “cumbersome and tedious”, arguing that procurement is a legal obligation involving public funds and should not be framed as an administrative burden but rather as a governance responsibility requiring strict compliance.

He questioned if DCS had conducted benchmarking against retail or market prices prior to approving quotations, given that some figures appeared significantly higher than ordinary consumer prices, raising concerns about oversight gaps or possible supplier exploitation. He requested a detailed breakdown of potential cost savings from internal production capacity, particularly through correctional centre agriculture and bakery operations,. There is clear potential for cost reduction if these systems are fully operational, but he expressed concern about maintenance failures, slow response times, and poor implementation follow-up undermining these gains.

Mr Moela raised a firm accountability concern on R7.7 million in irregular expenditure linked to procurement non-compliance, requesting details on consequence management outcomes. He asked which officials had been disciplined, suspended, or subjected to consequence management, if any contracts had been cancelled, if any criminal cases had been opened, and how much money had been recovered. He emphasised that the Committee requires evidence of actual enforcement rather than theoretical assurances of accountability.

Mr M Sokopo (ANC) spoke to the importance of honesty and practicality in advancing the DCS self-sufficiency agenda, particularly as a strategy for cost reduction. He argued that while broader food production goals exist, DCS should prioritise bread production as the most immediate and realistic cost-saving intervention, rather than expanding into more complex production lines such as tomato puree or mayonnaise. He referenced previous discussions and ministerial indications on plans to expand bakery infrastructure by around 2027–2028, stressing that these commitments must be supported through proper planning, maintenance, and operational readiness to achieve meaningful savings.

He highlighted the importance of forward cost projections and planning, arguing that each management area should have clear estimates of annual expenditure (for example, bread procurement costs per region) to enable comparison between external procurement and internal production costs. Internal bakery production could significantly reduce costs per unit compared to external purchasing, provided infrastructure is functional and properly maintained. He raised concern over observed operational failures in facilities such as Drakenstein, where bakery equipment reportedly remains non-functional, including mixers and ovens, which undermines self-sufficiency goals.

Mr Sokopo questioned the procurement of specific food items such as pawpaw and cauliflower, asking for their nutritional justification and relevance within correctional diets. He raised concerns about public perception, suggesting that such procurement decisions may be seen as excessive, and questioned if these items could instead be sourced through internal agricultural production. He challenged references to “substantial savings” in the presentation, requesting concrete financial figures to support such claims. Finally, he questioned pricing logic in high-volume procurement items such as bread, arguing that bulk purchasing should result in lower costs due to economies of scale, and expressed concern that current pricing (e.g. R24 per loaf compared to retail benchmarks) appears inconsistent with expected procurement efficiencies.

Ms N Nolutshungu (EFF) raised serious concerns about the DCS presentation, particularly the narrative around “savings” achieved through renegotiations. The framing of reduced prices as success is misleading, as it obscures what appears to be systemic overpricing at the point of contract award. Specific reference was made to contract H04-23, where several items were initially approved at highly inflated rates. She questioned how such pricing could have passed supply chain management controls in the first place, suggesting that the scale of the discrepancies points beyond ordinary market variation or administrative error and instead raises concerns of possible collusion or procurement manipulation within DCS systems.

Ms Nolutshungu provided examples of the alleged price distortions to support the concern. These included, among others, a 10 kg item initially priced at approximately R781 and later reduced to around R160, a one-litre bottle of oil originally priced at about R726 and later reduced to around R29, and gravy powder originally priced at R3 735 and later adjusted to around R920. Such drastic differences cannot be explained through normal market fluctuations and she questioned the integrity of the approval process that allowed these prices to be accepted. Further concerns were raised about uniform pricing arrangements across regions and the use of consultants in procurement processes, which she argued may indicate weakened internal capacity and increased risk of external influence in contract management.

Ms Nolutshungu called for stronger accountability and consequence management. Renegotiation alone is insufficient. Suppliers linked to inflated pricing should be subject to investigation, blacklisting, and possible exclusion from state contracts. She requested a comprehensive breakdown of all affected products, including original and revised prices, suppliers involved, and an assessment of financial prejudice to the state. She questioned if any forensic investigations, SIU referrals, or disciplinary actions had been initiated against both officials and suppliers. She concluded by asking if DCS accepts that the pattern of pricing irregularities may indicate a broader, coordinated procurement corruption scheme rather than isolated incidents.

The Chairperson sought clarity on three key issues about departmental contracts and procurement practices. Firstly, she requested information on the extent of supplier continuity, specifically how many of the contractors currently awarded contracts had previously been providing goods and services to DCS before the initiation of the new contract. This question was aimed at understanding if there was continuity in service providers or a significant shift in the supplier base.

Secondly, the Chairperson raised concerns on pricing structures and potential cost variations over time. She requested comparative pricing data for goods supplied under previous arrangements versus the current contract. Using the example of a basic commodity such as bread, she asked what the price per unit had been under prior supply arrangements (for instance, R15 per loaf in previous contracts) and how those prices compared to those now charged under the newly awarded contract. The intention was to assess if there had been significant price escalations.

Lastly, she questioned if the previous contracts for similar goods contained renegotiation clauses, and if so, if they had been invoked. She asked for the outcomes of renegotiation, particularly what the final agreed pricing structures were following such adjustments. This question focused on contract flexibility, adherence to contractual mechanisms, and the final financial implications arising from any price revisions.

Minister Response

Minister Groenewald responded by outlining both the legal framework governing procurement and the corrective steps already underway within DCS. He began by emphasising compliance with the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), stating that it strictly prohibits any political office bearer, including ministers, from interfering in tender processes. He clarified that ministerial involvement is only appropriate after procurement processes have been completed, at which point oversight and review can take place.

The Minister said that in 2024/25 he had already initiated a review process focusing on both perishable and non-perishable goods contracts, triggered in part by complaints received on the bread supply contract. He rejected the suggestion that the review was solely driven by pressure from the Portfolio Committee, stating that the process had begun earlier, although he welcomed collaborative engagement between DCS and the Committee to improve outcomes.

The Minister explained that, following complaints about pricing, particularly the bread contract, the National Commissioner, as accounting officer, had centralised procurement processes. Previously, procurement had been managed at the regional level, which he noted created inconsistencies and potential opportunities for corruption. Centralisation was introduced to strengthen oversight, improve price control, and reduce procurement irregularities.

Addressing bread pricing, the Minister stated that an intervention had revealed that DCS was previously paying approximately R24.50 per loaf, which he described as unreasonably high when compared to retail market prices. Following a review process, the price was renegotiated down to approximately R15 per loaf. He presented this as evidence that the ongoing review process was yielding tangible results in correcting inflated pricing.

He highlighted progress towards improving self-sufficiency within Correctional Services, particularly in bread production. DCS is currently about 96.4% self-sufficient in bread supply and aims to reach 100% self-sufficiency by 2028 through the expansion of internal bakery capacity, including plans to operationalise seven additional bakeries. This shift would reduce reliance on external suppliers and result in significant long-term savings for the state.

On broader procurement reforms, the Minister confirmed that DCS is continuing to review other perishable and non-perishable contracts where prices appear inflated. He agreed with Members that further investigation is necessary to determine why prices were previously so high before renegotiation, although he noted that he could not account for pricing structures predating his tenure.

He stated that, where necessary, DCS would escalate matters to the Special Investigating Unit or initiate forensic investigations, while also acknowledging that such processes are costly. These decisions would be guided by the outcomes of ongoing internal reviews.

On agricultural self-sufficiency, the Minister supported expanding internal production of certain food items, such as cauliflower, noting that DCS already produces crops like cabbage. However, he cautioned that agricultural expansion requires significant investment in equipment and infrastructure, citing the example of a tractor costing approximately R1 million. Despite the costs, he maintained that self-production would ultimately generate savings and strengthen long-term sustainability.

He concluded by calling for cooperative engagement between DCS and the Portfolio Committee, emphasising that while complaints and concerns are taken seriously and investigated, not all are necessarily substantiated. He reiterated that the review process had already begun earlier in 2025, including in response to external reports, and that results were already emerging. He deferred detailed technical responses to the National Commissioner, who would provide clarification on operational matters.

Deputy Minister Response

The Deputy Minister situated the discussion within the framework of the PFMA, specifically section 38, which assigns accountability to the accounting officer to ensure that procurement processes are compliant, transparent, and aligned with legislative requirements governing state contracts.

She emphasised that ministers and deputy ministers are not involved in procurement adjudication or tender processes due to PFMA restrictions. Their role is limited to oversight after procurement processes have been concluded. Contract review clauses are already embedded within procurement agreements, allowing periodic review of pricing and conditions.

The Deputy Minister explained that pricing structures and cost build-ups should be interpreted within an administrative framework that includes overheads such as transport, fuel, packaging, and logistics, cautioning against oversimplified comparisons with retail purchases.

She acknowledged Members’ concerns on insufficient disclosure of savings achieved through renegotiation and agreed that detailed financial breakdowns should be provided by technical officials, particularly the National Commissioner and Chief Financial Officer (CFO).

She confirmed that significant differences had been achieved between initial contract pricing and renegotiated rates, resulting in notable savings, although the exact figures were not presented at this stage. She noted that renegotiation is permitted under contractual clauses, typically every six months, particularly where market conditions change materially.

She highlighted external economic pressures, such as fuel price volatility, as contributing factors affecting supplier cost structures. She noted that procurement centralisation at the head office level was intended to streamline processes previously fragmented across regions, although this has resulted in increased administrative workload.

The Deputy Minister deferred detailed technical explanations to the National Commissioner and management team, reaffirming that procurement oversight is a collective administrative responsibility.

Department Response

Ms Molaba provided a detailed technical explanation of the procurement and pricing processes. DCS had received 934 bids for the contract, which were reduced to 115 qualifying suppliers after evaluation stages, including mandatory compliance and functionality assessments. Pricing negotiations occur only at the final stage before awarding contracts.

Pricing anomaly explanation (pan release oil example)

She addressed concerns on extremely high unit pricing (e.g.R700+ per litre), explaining that this was due to a typing error in the bid documentation, where “1 litre” was incorrectly stated instead of “25 litres.” Because the document had already been published and submitted through the official procurement portal, it could not be amended. The department instead renegotiated pricing based on correct unit measurements, reducing the cost significantly (approximately R29 per litre average across regions).

Ms Molaba clarified that all revised prices were agreed voluntarily with suppliers, documented, and considered legally sound, with minimal risk of litigation, as no party objected to the revised agreements.

Price benchmarking and market alignment

A pre-award market reference pricing unit has been established within Supply Chain Management to ensure that negotiated prices are aligned with market rates and public sector expectations. Suppliers justified price increases primarily due to rising overhead costs, including fuel, labour, and logistics.

CPI vs contract-based pricing mechanism

The department did not use CPI or PPI adjustments, but rather a special condition of contract pricing formula, as the objective was not inflationary adjustment but correction of previously inflated pricing. CPI/PPI mechanisms were deemed unsuitable as they are designed for upward adjustments rather than price reductions.

Centralisation of procurement

Ms Molaba explained that procurement was previously managed at the regional level across approximately 243 centres, which created inefficiencies and administrative delays. Centralisation to the head office was introduced to improve control and reduce corruption risks, though it has increased workload and processing time. She clarified that the system is not isolated from regions; regional officials still participate in procurement where capacity is required.

DCS CFO, Mr Lebogang Marumule, provided clarification on implementation timelines, pricing context, and governance safeguards.

He confirmed that the contract commenced on 1 April 2025 and was audited by the Auditor-General of South Africa (AGSA) between May and July 2025.

Timing and pricing clarification

The CFO clarified that the “exorbitant” prices reflected in documentation were not historical payments made by DCS, but rather initial bid-stage or market analysis figures identified at the contract implementation stage, which triggered immediate renegotiation within the first two months of the contract.

The revised implementation took effect on 1 June 2025, following corrective pricing negotiations.

Contract design and risk management

He explained that the contract included a specific clause allowing periodic price renegotiation based on market analysis, separate from standard annual adjustments. The early renegotiation was therefore contractually compliant and risk-managed within the first implementation phase.

He confirmed that a dashboard system has now been developed to track pricing across regions and service providers, addressing previous gaps where DCS lacked visibility over fragmented procurement pricing.

Market-based negotiation approach

DCS deliberately used market analysis rather than CPI-linked adjustments to ensure pricing reflected actual market conditions and prevented automatic inflationary increases. Mr Marumule emphasised that DCS is confident that prices remain fair within the supplier panel framework and that continuous monitoring is in place.

DCS National Commissioner Makgothi Thobakgale expanded on procurement reform, audit outcomes, and system-level reforms.

Historical procurement context

He explained that DCS previously relied heavily on a quotation-based system, which contributed to over R4 billion in irregular expenditure due to unstructured and inconsistent pricing across procurement transactions. To address this, DCS centralised procurement into structured national contracts, reducing price variability and improving financial control.

Use of consultants and audit oversight

He confirmed that consultants were initially appointed to support DCS due to limited internal expertise in large-scale procurement reform, particularly for perishable goods, coal, and cleaning materials contracts. The intention was to transition towards internal capacity over time.

He confirmed that these contracts were audited by the Auditor-General, who did not find evidence of corruption or criminal conduct. Instead, audit findings focused on improving market analysis and pricing methodologies.

Savings and financial impact

He indicated that a comparative analysis has been conducted between quotation-based procurement and current contract pricing, showing measurable savings. DCS will submit detailed financial comparisons to the Committee.

Legal and compliance matters

He confirmed that no legal advice was required on non-compliance, as all renegotiated prices were mutually agreed and contractually valid. Suppliers did not contest the revised pricing.

Special dietary items and procurement complexity

He explained that certain line items (e.g. special diet foods such as “pawpaw”) are prescribed by nutritionists and do not apply universally to all offenders. These items are procured in limited quantities and remain part of ongoing discussions on cost efficiency and dietary reform.

Strategic reform and sustainability

He emphasised that procurement reform is linked to broader sustainability and self-sufficiency objectives, including agricultural production initiatives within correctional facilities.

The National Commissioner confirmed ongoing improvements to Supply Chain Management capacity, continued involvement of regional offices in procurement processes, and strengthened oversight mechanisms to monitor budget impact and ensure compliance with the Correctional Services Act and PFMA.

Ministry follow-up response

Deputy Minister Ntshalintshali said that the DCS officials had provided clarity on the procurement from initiation through to implementation. The procurement process begins with a call for proposals, followed by supplier pricing submissions, after which negotiations are undertaken. She emphasised that the objective of negotiations is to secure value for money, prevent overpricing, and ensure that DCS is not exploited in the awarding of contracts.

Supply chain management is governed by the PFMA which requires transparency, accountability, and value-for-money considerations in all procurement activities.

She maintained that DCS is satisfied that the processes followed meet these requirements, as they promote transparency and ensure that public funds are utilised efficiently. She affirmed that DCS remains committed to transparent procurement practices and accountable financial management.

Minister Groenwald said that the DCS approach to procurement reform is centred on collaboration, transparency, and corrective action where necessary. A key concern raised by Members and the public is if corruption exists within procurement process. He assured the Committee that consequence management is taken seriously and that, where corruption is identified, appropriate action will be taken, as DCS is responsible for managing taxpayer funds.

AGSA did not identify corruption in the audited procurement processes. Instead, audit findings related primarily to process improvements, particularly pricing methodologies and market analysis.

The Minister acknowledged that the National Commissioner has already implemented structural reforms, including the centralisation of procurement as a preventative measure against corruption and irregularities. These reforms as deliberate steps aimed at strengthening internal controls and improving oversight.

DCS maintains an internal investigative capacity and routinely investigates complaints received from members of the public and stakeholders. Where contraventions are identified, disciplinary or corrective action is taken accordingly.

The Minister emphasised that transparency remains a core principle guiding DCS reforms. He is currently considering if it would be legally permissible to publish procurement information in the public domain, including tender submissions and awarded contracts, in the interest of openness and accountability. While he is not yet certain of the legal framework governing such disclosure, he personally supports greater transparency where legislation allows it.

Finally, the Minister thanked the National Commissioner and DCS officials for their work in implementing reforms and defending the integrity of the procurement system. Ongoing efforts aim to secure better pricing outcomes for DCS, reduce inefficiencies, and ultimately ensure value for money for South African taxpayers

Follow-up Questions

 Top of Form

Mr M Sokopo (ANC) stated that the responses provided did not sufficiently engage with the concerns he had raised on the DCS self-sufficiency programme, bakery infrastructure expansion, and timelines for operational implementation.

He particularly sought greater clarity on the DCS plans to expand bakery facilities across correctional centres and requested more precise implementation timelines for the additional bakeries. The responses also did not adequately explain how DCS intended to resolve persistent maintenance failures affecting bakery infrastructure already existing within correctional facilities.

Ms N Nolutshungu (EFF) welcomed the responses provided by DCS but noted that several questions remained either partially answered or outstanding. The National Commissioner had already acknowledged that certain matters would require further written responses and proposed that clear timelines be set for submission of these responses. She would forward additional questions formally to ensure that all matters are comprehensively addressed.

She raised a specific request for confirmation on pricing adjustments on gravy powder, noting a reported initial price of R3,735, which was allegedly renegotiated down to approximately R920, and sought formal confirmation of these figures.

She questioned the management of contingency provisions in tender documents, particularly if a percentage is typically allocated within procurement contracts to account for unforeseen cost increases such as fuel and operational expenses.

Mr C Niehaus (EFF) confirmed that the Economic Freedom Fighters (EFF) would consolidate outstanding questions to be formally forwarded to DCS.

Mr Niehaus noted that the Minister's remark on the high cost of forensic audits could be interpreted as a reluctance to pursue investigations in cases where corruption or mismanagement is evident. Accountability should not be undermined by cost considerations. Forensic investigations are essential in cases of apparent corruption or misappropriation of funds. Failure to investigate thoroughly would not only allow past wrongdoing to go unaddressed but also increase the risk of future misconduct.

He argued that DCS should not appear to justify inaction in cases of alleged wrongdoing, particularly where evidence suggests excessive or irregular expenditure. Consequence management and accountability must remain central to procurement oversight, even where systemic reforms are underway.

Minister Response

The Minister clarified that there should be no misunderstanding on the Department's position on corruption investigations. He stated explicitly that where there is evidence of corruption or wrongdoing, DCS will pursue forensic investigations regardless of cost implications.

His earlier reference to the cost of forensic audits was intended to highlight that such investigations are resource-intensive, particularly where allegations are unsubstantiated or lack an evidentiary basis. However, where credible evidence exists, DCS will initiate full forensic investigations and consequence management.

He said that the National Commissioner would respond to specific pricing-related queries, particularly those predating his tenure.

The Minister reaffirmed the Department’s commitment to transparency, accountability, and continuous procurement reform. Ongoing reforms are aimed at ensuring value for money and protecting public resources.

The Deputy Minister clarified that concerns raised by Members on “exorbitant prices” stemmed from initial bid submissions provided by service providers at the time of the call for proposals. She reiterated that these initial prices represent supplier submissions, which are then subject to negotiation by DCS.

She emphasised that procurement pricing is determined through a structured process involving comparison with market-related benchmarks, including CPI, overhead cost considerations, and operational inputs. The Department's role is to negotiate in the best interest of government to ensure value for money and prevent overpricing. Any previously identified inflated prices were addressed through negotiation processes, although in some instances the process was slower than desired. This was a matter of administrative improvement rather than misconduct.

The Deputy Minister acknowledged that errors in procurement documentation, including proofreading and specification issues, had contributed to confusion in certain pricing examples. These administrative weaknesses are being corrected to improve future procurement accuracy.

She cautioned against automatically interpreting procurement price discrepancies as corruption, emphasising that the Auditor-General findings did not indicate irregularity or corruption in the processes under review. Where savings have been achieved, they should be acknowledged as successful outcomes of procurement reform.

The Deputy Minister concluded by encouraging continued transparency and all follow-up written questions by Members would be formally responded to.

The National Commissioner provided further technical clarification on procurement pricing methodology and negotiation processes.

He confirmed that initial bid prices are those submitted by suppliers at the close of the tender process. In line with standard procurement practice, these prices are evaluated, averaged, and then subjected to negotiation, with the aim of securing the lowest feasible price.

He explained that although DCS negotiated downward from initial submissions, the resulting average prices remained higher than expected when benchmarked against market rates. This necessitated further comparison with retail and market-going prices to determine fair value. The contract includes a provision allowing price reviews every six months, which was specifically designed to address fluctuations and correct pricing anomalies identified after contract implementation. This mechanism allows DCS to revisit pricing structures through structured negotiations with suppliers, ensuring that prices remain both market-aligned and sustainable for service providers.

He reaffirmed that DCS remains committed to implementing all directives from the Committee and will submit additional responses within the timelines requested.

Follow-up Question

Ms N Nolutshungu (EFF) raised a final outstanding issue on contingency provisions in procurement tender documents. She sought clarity if a contingency percentage is built into contracts to account for unforeseen cost increases, such as fuel price fluctuations and other operational eventualities. This had not been addressed in the earlier departmental responses.

DCS Response

The Minister said that the question on contingency was a technical matter and requested that the National Commissioner provide a detailed response. He added that DCS would formally respond to all outstanding written questions and reaffirmed that the engagement process would continue through administrative channels.

The National Commissioner explained that contingency allowances are not universally applied across all procurement contracts but are typically used in specific categories such as maintenance and construction contracts, where scope variations and unforeseen cost adjustments are more common. The current contract under discussion does not include a separate contingency percentage provision. Instead, the pricing structure is composed of two core components:

Commodity cost – the actual cost of producing or purchasing the goods (for example, bread or other food items); and

Overhead costs – operational expenses incurred by the supplier in delivering goods to DCS, including transport, fuel, and logistics.

These two components collectively constitute the final bid price submitted by suppliers. As such, all foreseeable costs are expected to be incorporated into the initial pricing structure.

The Commissioner concluded that, since overheads are already embedded in the bid price, no separate contingency mechanism is required or applied in this contract. The pricing model is therefore designed to account for all expected delivery-related costs within the negotiated unit price.

Conclusions

The Chairperson noted that several responses were not yet fully detailed. There was a clear commitment from DCS that additional written information would be submitted to the Committee. These further submissions would be necessary to ensure that Members receive sufficiently comprehensive answers to inform their oversight role. The Committee would finalise its recommendations and observations based on the oral responses received and the additional information that would be forthcoming.

She highlighted a concern on inconsistencies in the Department's framing of its procurement, particularly referring to renegotiated prices as “savings” while the initial prices appeared significantly inflated. In her view, the core issue was not the subsequent reduction in prices but rather the governance weaknesses that allowed such elevated figures to be accepted in the first instance. She cautioned DCS against treating corrective action as a performance achievement, noting that such adjustments instead point to weaknesses in internal supply chain controls and oversight mechanisms.

The Chairperson warned that while moving away from fragmented RFQs toward centralised contracts might reduce some forms of abuse, over-centralisation could equally centralise corruption at a larger scale if strong controls were not implemented.

She raised concerns on the DCS self-sufficiency programme, particularly bakery infrastructure. Referring to oversight visits conducted by the Committee, she stated that much of the bakery equipment remained dysfunctional for prolonged periods. She cited examples where bakeries operated with only one functioning mixer or oven while other equipment remained unrepaired. She instructed DCS to submit reports by the end of May 2026 detailing timelines for repairing bakery equipment and expanding bakery infrastructure.

The Chairperson requested reports on disciplinary processes, suspensions, cancelled contracts, criminal referrals, and recovery of funds linked to procurement irregularities. All reports requested during the meeting should be submitted by the end of May 2026. The DCS would also return later during 2026 to provide a comprehensive update on the renegotiation process, which was currently reported as being 75% complete.

The Chairperson reminded Members of the upcoming Correctional Services Budget Vote debate in the Good Hope Chamber before adjourning the meeting.
