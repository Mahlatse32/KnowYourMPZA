Department of Basic Education Q3 2025/26 Performance: with Minister

Basic Education

10 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms K Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Minister and Deputy Minister of Basic Education were in attendance as the Department of Basic Education (DBE) briefed the Committee on its third-quarter performance for the 2025/26 financial year. The Department reported an overall achievement rate of 75% for its quarterly performance indicators. Key highlights during the quarter included the administration of the 2025 National Senior Certificate examinations, the launch of the Funda Uphumelele National Survey to assess foundational reading outcomes, the ongoing mass registration of Early Childhood Development centres, and the implementation of a joint school safety protocol with the South African Police Service. Expenditure was 80% against its adjusted budget of R38.2 billion. However, the report highlighted significant operational deviations, most notably a severe under-expenditure of 40% on the Schools Backlogs Infrastructure Grant.

The Committee expressed profound frustration at the persistent under-expenditure on infrastructure amidst a staggering national backlog and severe classroom overcrowding. Members demanded the implementation of stringent accountability measures to arrest the return of unspent funds to National Treasury and urged the Minister to elevate infrastructure to a standalone primary performance indicator. The discussion interrogated the systemic human resource failures resulting in critical shortages of subject-specific educators. The Committee strongly questioned the Department's failure to absorb unemployed, state-funded teaching graduates while institutional mismanagement concurrently forced existing educators to instruct subjects entirely outside of their academic specialisations.

Members raised grave concerns on the escalating prevalence of gender-based violence, bullying, and criminal activities within schooling environments, questioning the tangible efficacy of the school safety protocol given recent reductions in provincial security personnel. Further rigorous inquiries were directed at the statistical methodology utilised to establish Mathematics and Science performance targets, the structural funding and infrastructure provisions for Early Childhood Development centres, and the specific pedagogical interventions required to rectify the weak literacy proficiencies identified by the national survey. The Committee rebuked the Department's purely quantitative reporting format, formally directing the inclusion of cumulative progress tracking and comprehensive qualitative narratives in all future reports to facilitate meticulous parliamentary oversight. Additionally, the Minister was strongly advised to implement a digital performance monitoring system to enhance executive oversight across all departmental programmes.

The meeting concluded with the Chairperson issuing a directive to the Department to systematically issue formal responses to unresolved community grievances, proposing an upcoming technical session to track provincial resolutions via a consolidated oversight mechanism.

Meeting report

Opening Remarks by Chairperson

The Chairperson said that quarterly reporting is a crucial instrument for parliamentary oversight and monitoring the Department's performance against its targets. DBE's presentation would be strictly limited to 20 minutes to ensure Members had ample time for engagement, as they were expected to have already reviewed the report thoroughly.

Minister’s remarks
Minister of Basic Education Siviwe Garube provided a contextual overview of the third quarter, noting that it coincided with the critical preparation and administration of the 2025 National Senior Certificate (NSC) examinations. She reported that more than 920,000 candidates wrote the examinations across approximately 6,800 centres. The Minister stated that the Department maintained a central focus on strengthening the foundations of learning in the early grades. In this regard, the Department launched the Funda Uphumelele National Survey to gather crucial evidence on reading outcomes and continued the expansion of the Bana Pele mass registration drive for Early Childhood Development (ECD). She highlighted the prioritisation of learner safety through the strengthening of the joint Safe Schools protocol with the South African Police Service (SAPS) to mitigate violence and bullying. Finally, she noted that the Department had to adapt certain targets in the current financial year in response to severe budget constraints.

DBE Quarter 3 Performance 2025/26
Ms Nosipho Mbonambi, DBE Director for Strategic Planning and Reporting, reported that the Department achieved 75% of its 24 quarterly indicators. Three indicators were partially achieved, and three were not achieved. Underachievement on the number of schools monitored on reading norms, the incremental introduction of African languages, and the multi-grade toolkit—were primarily due to monitoring resources being heavily front-loaded and expended during the first quarter. She assured the Committee that the respective annual targets remained on track to be fully achieved by the end of the financial year.

Highlighting key programmatic achievements, she noted that 10,374 Funza Lushaka bursaries were awarded, with an additional 5,024 bursaries specifically directed toward foundation phase students. The National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) successfully provided nutritious meals to over 9,486,104 learners during the quarter. On infrastructure, while no new schools were completed under the Accelerated School Infrastructure Delivery Initiative (ASIDI) in the third quarter, 11 new classrooms were provided and 56 classrooms were renovated to alleviate overcrowding.

Mr Pat Khunou (DBE) presented the financial report, noting that the Department’s adjusted 2025/26 budget R38.2 billion had an expenditure rate of 80% by the end of Q3. He explained that while this mathematically exceeded the standard straight-line norm of 75%, the variance was primarily driven by the predetermined, front-loaded payment schedules for conditional grants, such as the NSNP and Funza Lushaka transfers. The Schools Backlogs Infrastructure Grant (SIBG) reflected significant under-expenditure and he clarified that this under-spending was a deliberate result of the impending closure of the grant, meaning no new contractors were being appointed. The remaining allocations would subsequently be absorbed into the Education Infrastructure Grant (EIG) in the next financial year.

Discussion
Mr S Mkhize (MKP) expressed grave concern on the Department’s reported financial performance, specifically the 80% expenditure rate, which he saw as a 20%) under expenditure. Underperformance directly hindered service delivery and the necessary transformation required within the schooling system. He requested that the Minister submit a definitive, diagnostic plan to arrest the under expenditure and prevent the return of unspent funds to National Treasury. This plan must clearly outline how the Department would curb fruitless, wasteful, irregular, and unauthorised expenditures. Since 90% of the budget was allocated to conditional grants, this area required the most rigorous monitoring, planning and accountability measures, which appeared to be currently lacking.

He strongly criticised the 40% under expenditure on school infrastructure labelling it as gross misconduct. He argued that when the government provided funds to change conditions on the ground, failing to utilise those funds was unacceptable, regardless of the explanations provided. He demanded that officials be held accountable for this failure. Given the reported R130 billion infrastructure backlog, he suggested that the government would likely attach strict timeframes and targets to any future funding to upscale implementation. Consequently, he urged the Minister to consider elevating the school infrastructure indicator to a standalone programmatic indicator, rather than keeping it under Programme 4. He pointed out the irony that Programme 4 currently lacked any performance indicators, which he found alarming given the massive infrastructure challenges South Africa faced. He argued that a standalone primary performance indicator, which he found alarming given the massive infrastructure challenges the country faces. A standalone primary performance indicator for infrastructure, tracked quarterly and annually, would be essential to ensure dedicated monitoring to prevent return of funds.

Mr Mkhize took issue with the Department’s reporting format, noting that progress against annual targets was not progressively traceable. He criticised the practice of only reviewing performance per respective quarter without showing cumulative progress, stating that this approach blindfolded the Portfolio Committee. He requested that future presentations include a dedicated column reflecting cumulative progress towards annual targets so the Committee could accurately track the Department’s trajectory over the 12-month period and identify areas needing intervention. He questioned the level of involvement of the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) during the development stages of the Annual Performance Plan (APP), noting that specialist guidance from DPME was seemingly absent given the observed gaps in the performance indicators.

He noted that the presentation was silent on the employment of new, additional teachers. He asked how the Department planned to staff the anticipated 3 500 new schools, pointing out that teachers could not simply be shifted from the current baseline. Employing more teachers, especially by absorbing Funza Lushaka graduates, presented an obvious opportunity for the Department to ease the financial burden on households, create jobs and address critical shortages in the system.

To improve oversight, Mr Mkhize suggested that the Minister implement an electronic performance and monitoring system. The Minister required a system that provided both a bird’s eye and granular view of performance across all programmes to provide strategic and technical guidance effectively, rather than relying solely on verbal reports from the Director General.

Mr Mkhize highlighted severe crises at specific schools in the Mambeni community to illustrate the impact of oversight failures. Amazizi Secondary School faced a ratio of 100 learners to a single teacher, forcing parents to crowdfund to build the school themselves. Mambeni Primary School, a school for learners with special educational needs, operated without adequately trained personnel or equipped facilities for learners with special needs. It was an injustice that the Department continued to refer learners there.

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) asked when DBE intended to publish the full findings of the literacy survey, Funda Uphumelele National Survey which covered 710 schools and 27 838 learners. She requested clarity on how these survey findings would influence future literacy interventions and what immediate interventions were planned for provinces that demonstrated weak reading performance. How did the survey align with DBE’s broader foundational learning reforms and what critical lessons had been drawn from previous literacy interventions that failed to improve national outcomes?

Turning her attention to ECD, she noted that it remained one of the largest growth areas within the Department’s mandate. She asked what percentage increase in registered ECD centres had been achieved through the Bana Pele registration campaign. Which provinces recorded the highest numbers of unregistered ECD centres? She asked how DBE ensured that newly registered centres met the required quality standards and if there was sufficient provincial capacity to physically inspect and support these newly registered centres. What are the specific challenges slowing down ECD expansion in rural areas?

Ms Xaba-Ntshaba noted that for teacher development and placement, DBE had awarded 10 374 Funza Lushaka bursaries plus an additional 5 024 bursaries dedicated to foundation phase students. She asked what percentage of these Funza Lushaka graduates had been successfully absorbed into permanent teaching posts. She questioned why some provinces were still struggling to place qualified graduates and asked which subject areas remained critically under-supplied despite the substantial bursary investments. How was DBE measuring the actual classroom impact of its teacher development programmes and what measures were being to ensure that rural schools benefited equally from these development initiatives?

Ms Xaba-Ntshaba, finally addressed the infrastructure report, pointing out that no new schools were implemented during the third quarter, with only 11 classrooms completed and 56 classrooms renovated during the year. She asked for reasons no new classrooms were implemented during Quarter 3. She requested details on which specific infrastructure projects were currently delayed and what the underlying causes for these delays were. She asked how DBE justified its slow infrastructure delivery despite having the available funds.

Mr T Louw (ANC) questioned the financial performance presented by DBE by noting that out of the adjusted budget of thirty eight point two-five-eight billion rand (R38.258 billion) for the 2025/26 financial year, DBE had spent only thirty point zero-six-three billion rand (R30.063 billion) for the conclusion of the third quarter. He acknowledged that while this represented roughly an eighty-point one percent (80.1%) overall expenditure rate, which mathematically exceeded the standard straight-line norm of seventy-five percent (75%), there were still underlying financial discrepances that required explicit clarification. Specifically, he drew the Committee’s attention to a variance of two hundred and sixty-two million rand (R262 Million) below DBE’s projected expenditure for the quarter under review. He asked DBE to provide a comprehensive explanation of the specific factors, administrative bottlenecks, and programmatic delays that contributed to this shortfall.

Mr Louw then highlighted the structural composition of the budget, pointing out that ninety percent (90%) of DBE’s financial allocation was transfer-based. He stated that this heavy reliance on transfers meant the bulk of the funds were sent to Provincial Education Departments (PEDs) in the form of conditional grants. He expressed significant concern on this funding model, arguing that it exposed the basic education sector to a persistently high risk of under-expenditure, particularly in critical service delivery areas such as infrastructure development and the mass rollout of ECD programmes. He further asked DBE to detail the oversight mechanisms, stringent monitoring tools, and early warning systems it intended to implement to ensure that provinces utilised these conditional grants efficiently and without the administrative delays that typically led to funds being returned to National Treasury. He requested a risk profile breakdown from DBE, asking which specific provinces currently presented the highest risk of under expenditure on their grant allocations and what targeted support DBE was providing to mitigate this risk.

Mr Louw interrogated DBE’s targets and oversight methodology with respect to Programme 2 (Curriculum Policy, Support and Monitoring) by referring to the performance indicator detailing that seventy-four (74) schools had been monitored for the availability of textbooks and stationery during the quarter. He questioned the statistical validity of this sample size, probing DBE as to if monitoring merely seventy-four (74) schools could be considered an adequate or scientifically sound representation of the procurement and delivery conditions experienced across the more than twenty-three thousand (23 000) public schools nationwide. He asked DBE to unpack qualitative findings from these oversight visits, specifically requesting details on the major curriculum implementation gaps and material shortages that the monitors had identified on the ground.

Mr Louw enquired about the modernisation of the curriculum to suit current economic demands. He requested a detailed explanation of how entrepreneurship education was being integrated into daily curriculum delivery. He stressed that the Committee needed to understand how entrepreneurship was being taught practically to equip learners with tangible economic skills, rather than it merely serving as a theoretical compliance metric in DBE’s quarterly monitoring tools.

Mr Louw addressed Programme 5 and the pressing issue of school safety by commending DBE for its collaborative initiatives with the SAPS to establish the School Safety Protocol. He however requested tangible evidence of its on-the-ground implementation. He asked DBE to provide exact figures on how many schools had successfully and fully implemented the safety protocols to date. To better understand the scope of the crisis, he asked for a geographic breakdown indicating which provinces and districts were currently recording the highest incidents of school-based violence, gangsterism, and bullying. He concluded his engagement by asking DBE what additional financial, infrastructural and human resources would be required to ensure that the School Safety Protocol was not just a national policy document, but a fully funded reality implemented across all high-risk schools in the country.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) welcomed DBE’s renewed focus on strengthening foundational literacy, characterising it as a positive step toward evidence-based education reform. She noted that this shift was evident in DBE’ report, particularly referencing the recent release of the Funda Uphumelele National Survey results at the close of the academic year. She however observed that implementation at PED level remained a significant structural challenge. She pointed out that persistent under-expenditure on infrastructure grants, coupled with severe overcrowding and poor school facilities continued to directly hamper the delivery of quality learning and teaching. She requested that DBE’s responses explicitly identify which provinces were failing to deliver on their mandates and detail the specific accountability measures being implemented to address these failures. She emphasised that this accountability must specifically cover the provision of Learning and Teaching Support Material (LTSM), National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) provision, literacy promotion, infrastructure spending, and the safeguarding of schooling communities.

Mr Jordaan asked how DBE planned to ensure that provinces actively implemented evidence-based reading programmes informed by the Funda Uphumelele National Survey’s outcomes. She further questioned what oversight mechanisms were currently in place to increase and improve the actual classroom time dedicated to literacy instruction. In terms of broader community support, she asked how DBE’s literacy promotion goals and the building of local literacy ecosystems would be impacted by the reality that municipal libraries and support systems were entirely dysfunctional in many municipalities across the country.

Ms Jordaan raised concerns about the structural flow of ECD funding, noting that additional financial allocations largely went into the per-child subsidy rather than toward infrastructure development. She cited statistics indicating that seven hundred thousand (700 000) children aged three to four urgently required learning spaces, while the Thrive by 5 Index showed that only forty-six percent (46%) of four-year-olds were developmentally on track. She argued that physical infrastructure directly influenced the quality and outcome of the ECD educational provision. Consequently, she asked if DBE imposed strict spending prescriptions requiring ECD centres to utilise their subsidies specifically for infrastructure and nutrition, akin to the norms and standards applied to public schools. She also pointed out that the quarterly presentation failed to indicate the actual performance of Early Learning National Assessment concerning literacy, numeracy and the ongoing nutritional stunting crisis facing South African children.

Ms Jordaan asked if DBE was genuinely satisfied with its current targets for the number of schools monitored with respect to the NSNP. She specifically requested the detailed outcomes and departmental conclusions drawn from the special monitoring focus conducted in the Limpopo province.

Ms Jordaan commended the recent anti-Gender-Based Violence (GBV) engagements involving seven hundred and thirteen (713) participants and stakeholders in the Ekurhuleni North and Johannesburg Central education districts during October. She however noted recent crime statistics and stressed that school violence required continuous intervention until it is completely eradicated. She asked DBE if there was empirical evidence showing that the DBE-SAPS School Safety Protocol was successfully reducing incidents of GBV, general violence, and bullying. She concluded by raising a critical concern on recent reports that the appointment of two hundred and seventy-three (273) security personnel had been cut, including some stationed at high crime hotspot areas, due to provincial budgetary constraints and financial reprioritisation. She asked how this specific reduction security personnel would affect the practical implementation of the DBE-SAPS School Safety Protocol and if DBE was actively monitoring provincial compliance as part of its overarching performance measurements.

Ms P Mngadi (MKP) directed her focus to the monitoring mechanisms outlined in Programme 2 and noted that while the programme monitored a vast array of critical curriculum initiatives, the sample sizes used for these evaluations were concerningly small. She specifically pointed out that DBE reported monitoring entrepreneurship education in only eighty-three (83) schools, the implementation of reading norms in a mere eight schools, and the multi-grade toolkit in just five (5) schools. Given that DBE’s national mandate requires it to oversee more than twenty-three thousand (23 000) public schools, she strongly questioned the statistical validity of these oversight exercises. She asked DBE to explain how monitoring such a minuscule sample size could possibly provide a reliable and representative basis for evaluating the implementation of national policies. She also asked how such limited data could be trusted to inform systemic interventions and strategic planning for the upcoming 2026/27 academic year.

Ms Mngadi highlighted a glaring contradiction on DBE’s budget and its expenditure on goods and services. She noted that DBE had previously stated that certain service delivery targets had to be reduced due to severe budget constraints, operating within an overall adjusted budget of thirty-eight point two-five-eight billion rand (R38.258 billion). She pointed out a significant expenditure variance within the goods and services allocation. The financial data showed that only two point three-four-two billion rand (R2.342 billion) had been spent against a projected allocation of three point four-four billion rand (R3.4 billion), resulting in a massive one point one billion rand (R1.1 billion) under expenditure. She requested that DBE provide the specific, detailed reasons behind this substantial under-spending on goods and services. She critically asked how DBE could reconcile this one point one billion rand (R1 100 000 000) under expenditure with its prevailing narrative that a lack of funds necessitates the reduction of core service delivery targets.

Ms Mngadi addressed the critical issue of teacher development and employment. She observed from the report that a substantial number of Funza Lushaka bursaries had been awarded, specifically noting the ten thousand, three hundred and seventy-four (10 374) general bursaries and an additional five thousand and twenty-four (5 024) bursaries dedicated to foundation phase students. She contrasted these high training figures with the actual employment data, which indicated that only two thousand, six hundred and sixty-two (2 662) young, qualified educators (aged thirty years and below) had been appointed in schools for the first time during the third quarter. Highlighting this stark disparity between the large number of newly trained teachers and the very small number actually absorbed into the education system, she asked DBE to outline its intervention strategies. She asked what concrete steps DBE was taking in active collaboration with PEDs, to ensure that the massive financial investment in pre-service teacher training directly translated into actual classroom appointments that would alleviate the critical teacher shortages experienced by schools.

Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) requested that the Chairperson provide a specific date for an in-house committee meeting to address the Minister’s recent media statements on an advertisement’s qualifications. Mr Shikwambana requested a provincial breakdown of the newly registered ECD centres as part of the mass registration of ECD centres; he requested a such provincial breakdown to include the exact geographic locations of ECD centres as this would facilitate targeted oversight by the Committee. He asked if the mass registration process indicated that DBE was taking over the ownership of these privately owned centres, and if not, if there were any future plans to assume ownership.

Mr Shikwambana raised severe concerns on ongoing school safety issues, stating that schools remained unsafe environments plagued by bullying, substance abuse, and criminal activities. Mr Shikwambana expressed deep frustration over the critical shortage of qualified teachers in school specifically citing an oversight visit to a school that lacked teachers for Agriculture, English, and Life Sciences. He noted that such vacancies often forced principals to temporarily use existing staff to teach subjects for which they were not qualified while waiting for delayed provincial appointments. He contrasted this severe shortage at the school level with the high number of unemployed Funza Lushaka graduates currently sitting at home He asked DBE to provide a clear explanation as to why it was not absorbing 100% of these state-funded graduates when the schooling system desperately needed their services.

Mr L Komane (EFF) asked how DBE established its newly introduced target of forty-four thousand, nine hundred and forty-four (44 944) learners achieving above sixty percent (60%) in Mathematics and Science. He specifically requested the baseline enrolment numbers for those subjects for the 2026/27 academic year to understand exactly what percentage of the total enrolled learners this target represented.

Mr Komane expressed his support for the earlier request to track annual targets using clear milestones and cumulative reporting. He urged DBE to provide a qualitative narrative in future quarterly reports rather merely presenting quantitative numbers and box-ticking metrics. The Committee needed to clearly understand the actual findings, on-the-ground challenges, and the specific interventions resulting from DBE’s school monitoring activities, such as those concerning reading norms and the incremental introduction of African languages.

To illustrate the necessity of this qualitative narrative, Mr Komane recounted a oversight visit to an ECD centre in Limpopo. The Committee found the centre was severely underfunded due to provincial budgetary constraints, receiving subsidies for only 80 children despite having 95 children who met the funding criteria. Such contextual realities must be detailed in the reports to assist the Committee in understanding the practical challenges and the efficacy of government interventions.

This detailed breakdown would greatly assist the Committee in conducting its own targeted follow-up oversight visits to verify if DBE’s documented interventions were yielding tangible results on the ground.

The Chairperson expanded on unqualified educators raised by Committee members, sharing direct observations from an oversight visit to a school in Limpopo that had recorded a matric pass rate below thirty percent (30%). On probing the causes of this underperformance, the Committee discovered the principal had been teaching three matric subjects including one for which he was entirely unqualified. She cited another example of a school that achieved a mere sixteen percent (16%) pass rate in Economics because the subject was taught by an educator who was not qualified in that specific field. She stressed that regardless of DBE’s formal definitions, the reality on the ground was that learners were taught by educators lacking the requisite subject-specific qualifications, which visibly damaged learner outcomes.

The Chairperson raised a significant compliance concern on the employment practices of independent schools. The South African Council for Educators (SACE) had explicitly confirmed to the Committee that independent schools employed a high number of foreign national teachers. This practice occurred even in subject areas where there was absolutely no shortage of qualified local graduates. The state was investing heavily in educating and creating local teaching skills, only for the private sector to ignore those locally produced skills. She asked if DBE was conducting proactive advocacy work to address this trend and the specific challenges or barriers preventing the absorption of local educators into the private sector.

Ministry & Department response

Minister Siviwe Gwarube agreed with the Committee on the need for meaningful targets that measured qualitative outputs rather than mere inputs. She supported the call for a narrative in future reports to ensure that achieving targets translated into tangible shifts within the education system moving away from a box-ticking exercise.

The Minister concurred that provincial spending needed dramatic improvement insofar as infrastructure is concerned. She stated that she had been vociferous with the provinces, making it clear that under-spending on infrastructure was unacceptable given the one hundred and forty billion rand (R140 billion) national backlog on infrastructure. She warned that under-performing provinces would lose their allocated infrastructure grants to better-performing provinces, as DBE resolutely refused to return unspent funds to National Treasury. She added that because the full Education Infrastructure Grant (EIG) would now sit directly with the provinces, DBE’s internal infrastructure unit would execute far greater monitoring capacity and conduct physical verifications of projects.

The Minister clarified the perceived under-expenditure on the ECD grant, explaining that this was directly caused by delayed budgetary allocations from the National Treasury due to parliamentary processes. This delay significantly shortened the timeframe available to provinces to spend their allocated R2 billion, and she asked for the committee’s indulgence on the slight under-expenditure in this specific area.

The Minister clarified in response to questions about the absorption of Funza Lushaka graduates, that holding a bursary did not guarantee automatic placement in a school. She explained that graduates still had to apply for fully funded vacant posts, the availability of which was entirely determined by the fiscal envelopes of the respective provincial departments. Acknowledging the broader issue of severe teacher shortages and high teacher-to-learner ratios, she announced that an active review of the post-provisioning norms (PPN) was currently underway. She noted that these norms had not been updated in twenty years and needed to be modernised to reflect the current shape and size of the education system.

The Minister assured the Committee that the DBE-SAPS School Safety Protocol was highly active. She cited recent unannounced school raids in KwaZulu-Natal that successfully resulted in the confiscation of weapons and drugs. She recounted her unannounced oversight visit to a school where a fatal stabbing had occurred confirming that safety committees and protocols were actively in place. She reminded the Committee that schools inevitably reflect the broader societal crime and gangsterism prevalent in their surrounding communities, necessitating a “whole of government” approach to effectively combat the crisis.

The Minister confirmed that DBE’s research into Mother-Tongue-Based Bilingual Education (MTBBE) proved that learning in a mother tongue was not a hindrance, but rather a critical enabler for literacy. She stated that this specific research would directly inform how DBE took the Funda Uphumelele survey results forward to ensure tangible impact.

The Minister clarified that the BEEI was a Presidential programme spearheaded by the Presidency and the Department of Employment and Labour. Consequently, DBE was merely a beneficiary and did not control the programme’s funding or set its employment targets. On the mass registration of ECD centres, she categorically confirmed that DBE was not taking over the ownership of private centres. Instead, the registration drive was designed to integrate the seven million children aged between two and five years into a supported system, providing centres with a structured cognitive development curriculum, nutritional support, safety packs, and access to the R24 daily subsidy per child.

Deputy Minister of Basic Education, Dr Reginah Mhaule, addressed the Committee's request for cumulative reporting on performance targets. DBE's traditional reporting norm is strictly segmented per quarter, meaning they report specifically on the activities planned and executed within that specific three-month period. While the first quarter's presentation did provide some indication of progress, she acknowledged the Committee's new directive and agreed that DBE would look into incorporating cumulative tracking in future reports, although she cautioned that it might present certain practical difficulties.

The Deputy Minister stressed that the rapid growth in learner numbers consistently outpaced available financial resources. She highlighted the sheer scale of the crisis by revealing that the system had received an influx of over 100 000 new learners in the current year alone. Accommodating this sudden influx would practically require the immediate construction of 20 to 30 new schools, which was a logistical and financial impossibility. She reiterated the Minister's point that bringing school infrastructure on par with the growing learner population would require an investment exceeding R150 billion. While acknowledging DBE's responsibility, she emphasised that this infrastructure deficit was a stark reality that directly caused the overcrowding observed during Committee oversight visits.

The Deputy Minister clarified that with respect to the persistent challenges surrounding the appointment of teachers and the absorption of Funza Lushaka graduates, there was an administrative process involved. She explained that the absorption rate heavily depends on the number of retiring educators and the specific subject needs identified at the district level. Crucially, she corrected a common misconception by stating that the actual appointment of teachers is conducted by DBE, or by PEDs. While provinces are responsible for issuing the official vacancy lists, the legal responsibility to conduct interviews and make the actual appointments rests entirely at the school level with the School Governing Bodies (SGBs). She noted that when schools complain about a shortage of teachers, it is often due to delays by the school's own SGB in convening interviewing committees once the vacancy list is published.

The Deputy Minister addressed the pressing concerns on school safety and the DBE-SAPS School Safety Protocol. She frankly stated that placing a dedicated police officer at every school was a logistical impossibility. She illustrated this by pointing out that a single police station often services an area containing between thirty-eight (38) and fifty (50) schools, making it entirely unfeasible given that stations may only have ten (10) to fifteen (15) officers available. As a practical alternative, she explained that the protocol assigns a single police officer to be responsible for a cluster of six to ten schools, working in conjunction with Community Policing Forums (CPFs) and SGB security committees. she echoed the Minister's sentiment that the violence seen in schools is a direct spillover of the broader social ills, unemployment, and criminality prevalent in the surrounding communities. She concluded that combating these social ills required a united, multi-departmental effort, as it extended far beyond the sole mandate of DBE.

DBE Director General Mr Mathanzima Mweli addressed the Committee’s concerns on entrepreneurship education, explaining that a pilot project had commenced in the General Education and Training (GET) phase, with intentions to expand it into the Further Education and Training (FET) phase. He noted that DBE was working in partnership with the Maharishi Institute, which had seconded officials to DBE to assist PEDs. While acknowledging that the rollout had not yet reached all schools, he reported that initial feedback indicated high levels of excitement among teachers and learners.

The Director General clarified DBE’s operational definition when it came to the issue of unqualified teachers, stating that an "unqualified teacher" strictly referred to an individual who possessed no formal teaching qualification whatsoever. However, he fully conceded the reality raised by Members, acknowledging that poor human resource planning at the school level frequently resulted in a mismatch of skills. He provided an example where a school might have a surplus of language teachers filling the post establishment, preventing the hiring of an agriculture teacher and forcing a language specialist to teach a subject they were not qualified for.

Dr Barney Mthembu DBE DDG Curriculum Policy, Support and Monitoring addressed the request for the exact enrolment figures for the 2026 Mathematics and Science cohorts. He confirmed that these specific registration numbers were still being processed and compiled by the provincial Education Management Information Systems (EMIS). He committed to submitting the preliminary figures to the Committee as soon as they became available.

Mr Enoch Rabotapi, Acting DDG: Teachers Education & Human Resources further clarified the status of unqualified educators, confirming that the mainstream schooling system did not employ individuals entirely lacking teaching qualifications. He noted that the only exception was within the ECD sector, specifically Grade R practitioners who were historically absorbed into the formal system without proper qualifications. He assured the Committee that DBE was currently actively assisting this specific cohort in acquiring their appropriate formal teaching qualifications.

Ms Simone Geyer, DBE DDG: Delivery and Support, detailed the financial prescriptions governing the ECD subsidy. She explained that the subsidy operated on a strict pro-rata formula: up to fifty percent (50%) must be allocated directly to nutrition, thirty percent (30%) is designated for learning resources and educational materials, and the remaining twenty percent (20%) is allocated for staff remuneration. She stressed that the subsidy is explicitly not intended for physical infrastructure repairs or building maintenance, as that competency falls under the Department of Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs (COGTA). However, she noted that the recent increase in the daily subsidy from seventeen rand (R17) to twenty-four rand (R24) per child would directly translate to a slight improvement in the monetary value of the twenty percent (20%) allocated for practitioner salaries.

Follow-up discussion

The Chairperson expressed confusion over DBE’s inability to provide the exact baseline enrolment numbers of the new Mathematics and Science targets. She argued that the target of forty-four thousand, nine hundred and forty-four (44 944) learners achieving above sixty percent (60%) applies to the current Grade 12 cohort, meaning these learners had already selected their subject streams when they were in Grade 10. Consequently, PEDs should already possess concrete data on exactly how many learners are registered for “pure maths” and science streams.

The Chairperson also questioned the logical methodology behind setting a specific numerical target of forty-four thousand, nine hundred and forty-four (44 944) without first having knowledge of the total number of matriculants actually participating in those subjects. She pointed out that if the total number of enrolled learners hypothetically only twenty thousand (20 000) a target of forty-four thousand, nine hundred and forty-four (44 944) would be impossible, illustrating why the baseline must dictate the target. She stressed that because cohort sizes vary from year to year, the target must be explicitly formulated as a percentage of a known enrolled baseline rather than relying on an isolated numerical estimate.

Mr Shikwambana utilised his follow-up opportunity to clarify hs earlier submissions on the prevalence of unqualified educators, directly challenging DBE’s assertion that no such category existed within the formal records. He explained that the practical reality on the ground was driven by severe administrative inefficiencies rather than a complete lack of foundational teaching qualifications. He outlined a recurring scenario where a school successfully advertises a vacancy for a specialised subject, such as Agricultural Science, and concludes the requisite interview processes, but it is subsequently subjected to prolonged delays while awaiting the formal appointment letter from the PED. He stated that during this administrative lacuna, the school remains entirely devoid of an appropriately qualified educator for that specific subject. Consequently, school principals are compelled to implement emergency stop-gap measurers by assigning existing staff members to instruct subjects for which they possess no formal academic qualification or specialisation. He expressed profound frustration that schools are forced into this compromised academic position due to lethargy caused by PEDs, particularly when fully qualified candidates have already been interviewed and are merely waiting outside the system for bureaucratic absorption.

Mr Mkhize reiterated his inquiry on the implementation of an electronic monitoring system. He stressed that such a system was indispensable for the Minister to effectively provide strategic, tactical, and technical guidance to DBE. He requested explicit confirmation of if the Minister possessed a digital interface that afforded both a granular and a bird's-eye view of performance across all departmental programmes, arguing that without such a tool, comprehensive executive oversight remained compromised.

Mr Mkhize addressed the Deputy Minister, clarifying that he was not requesting an entirely new presentation format, but merely the structural addition of a column to reflect cumulative progress towards annual targets. Performance deficits from previous quarters must logically be tracked moving forward, and visualising this cumulative trajectory was necessary for the Committee to conduct rigorous, data-driven oversight.

Mr Mkhize referenced DBE’s stated strategic priority to ease the financial burden on households and contribute to national job creation. He argued that absorbing unemployed teachers presented an obvious strategic opportunity. Employing these available graduates would simultaneously strengthen pedagogical efficacy in schools and addressed national youth unemployment, effectively achieving two critical objectives simultaneously.

Ministry & Department response

The Deputy Minister intervened prior to the departmental officials' final responses to mandate that several unaddressed inquiries previously submitted by Mr Komane be formally recorded and answered. She directed the delegation to provide clarity on the strategic prioritisation of the foundation phase, specifically concerning pedagogical interventions for learners who currently lack fundamental reading proficiencies.

The Deputy Minister requested DBE clarify its statutory position and infrastructural policies on schools situated on private land and if permanent infrastructural developments could be legally erected on such properties. DBE should also detail their programmatic interventions for severely dilapidated schools and those recording a 0% matriculation pass rate. She requested an explicit operational explanation on the academic absorption of learners who failed the previous academic year – how they are structurally and administratively accommodated within schools alongside the newly promoted cohort.

The Director General apologised for the earlier misunderstanding on “unqualified teachers”. He now understood that the Committee was referring to fully qualified educators who are forced to teach subjects outside of their academic specilisation due to poor human resource planning at the school level. He agreed that this misalignment occurs, noting instances were a school’s post establishment is filled with surplus language educators, preventing the hiring of critically needed agricultural science or economics educators.

The Director General elaborated on the systemic delays in appointing educators, revealing that PEDs, including those in historically affluent provinces like Gauteng and the Western Cape, were experiencing severe cash flow problems and budget cuts. These financial constraints left provinces virtually “on their knees”, rendering them entirely unable to fund new appointments, hire subject advisors or absorb available Funza Lushaka graduates. 

On Mathematics and Science targets, the Director General conceded that the baseline for the current APP must indeed be derived from the 2025 academic year data. He apologised for the initial confusion and committed to extracting and providing those exact baseline figures, confirming that the data was available. The performance pipeline for subjects like Mathematics, Science, and Accounting is rigorously tracked long before matric. He noted that foundational confidence is built as early as the intermediate and senior phases (such as tracking Economic and Management Sciences to predict Accounting success), which directly informs the streams learners confidently select in Grade 10. He assured the Committee that to protect the integrity of these targets, DBE strictly prohibits learners from changing their subject streams during Grade 12.

Ms Stella Mosimege, DBE Chief Director for Strategic Planning, Research, Evaluation and Monitoring, gave the methodology used to establish the Mathematics and Science targets for the APP. The baseline for the 2026/27 APP was computationally derived from the 2024 National Senior Certificate examination results. Programme managers calculate the percentage of learners who passed at sixty percent (60%) and above and generally project future targets by adding a five percent (5%) increase to that baseline. Addressing the Committee's request for cumulative reporting, she pointed out that the current presentation tables did include rows tracking outputs for quarters one, two, and three, culminating in a total column for quantitative indicators. However, she acknowledged deficiencies in qualitative narrative reporting and committed to ensuring that future reports thoroughly detail the milestones, contextual challenges, and remedial strategies for annual targets.

The Minister addressed Mr Mkhize’s request on an electronic monitoring system. She explained that to maintain a bird’s-eye view of DBE’s performance, she held strict monthly ministerial management meetings with all branch heads to track progress against the APP, the Medium-Term Development Plan, and SONA priorities. She stated that this, combined with regular one-on-one engagements with the Director General, provided her with a comprehensive and accurate sense of where performance was lagging and required strategic intervention. She also confirmed that the DPME was intricately involved in DBE’s strategic planning and target setting processes.

Chairperson’s concluding remarks

The Chairperson declined a late follow-up question from Mr Komane, asking him to submit it in writing to accommodate due to time constraints. She requested members to submit their proposed focal areas to assist in drafting the second term Committee programme. Several strategic priorities for upcoming engagements included the ELRC report, the Children's Amendment Act, BELA regulations, and a joint meeting dedicated to learner transitioning plus an upcoming oversight visit to Mpumalanga.

The Chairperson confirmed that a dedicated committee meeting would be convened on 17 March to formally probe the Minister's recent media statements as well budget presentations from DBE, UMALUSI, and SACE.

In her final remarks to the Minister and Director General she expressed dissatisfaction with DBE's ongoing failure to issue formal responses to grievances lodged by communities. To rectify this administrative failure and safeguard parliamentary responsiveness, she proposed scheduling a focused technical session designed to systematically track and enforce provincial resolutions via an existent consolidated oversight spreadsheet.

The meeting was adjourned.
