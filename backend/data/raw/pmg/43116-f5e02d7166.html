Implementation of BRRR recommendations; Update on FMD outbreak; with Deputy Minister

Agriculture

26 May 2026
Chairperson: D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

The Portfolio Committee on Agriculture met to consider the Department of Agriculture’s responses to the Committee’s Budgetary Review and Recommendation Report (BRRR) recommendations and to receive an update on the Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) outbreak. The Department reported progress in improving governance, audit outcomes, organisational restructuring, food security planning, and filling critical vacancies. Efforts included strengthening performance monitoring, developing a new National Food and Nutrition Security Plan, supporting agricultural entities such as the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP), and the National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC), and pursuing funding for a domestic FMD vaccine production facility.

On transformation and farmer support, the Department highlighted programmes such as the AgriBEE Fund, blended finance initiatives, agricultural cooperative training, and support for veterinary services. Challenges remained regarding Farmer Production Support Units, agricultural colleges, veterinary retention, and Land Bank accountability.

The Department reported that FMD outbreaks had spread to all provinces, increasing from 932 cases in March 2026 to 2 034 by May 2026. More than 5.2 million vaccine doses had been secured from Argentina and Türkiye, but only about 3.35 million animals had been vaccinated. Challenges included shortages of ear tags and syringes, travel restrictions on veterinary personnel, and uneven provincial implementation. Additional vaccines and animal health technicians were being deployed, while diagnostic capacity was being expanded through new laboratories.

Members raised concerns about the slow vaccination rate, weak animal traceability systems, provincial accountability, biosecurity failures, and the effectiveness of government support programmes. Questions were also asked about the recent court judgement allowing private vaccine imports, agricultural colleges, transformation, and monitoring of public funds.

Department officials explained that the newly gazetted Section 10 Animal Diseases Scheme provides a legal framework for private-sector participation in vaccine procurement and administration under government oversight. They emphasised the need for collaboration with organised agriculture to achieve the target of vaccinating 80% of South Africa’s estimated 14 million livestock population. The Committee stressed the importance of accountability, implementation, and tangible improvements for farmers and rural communities.

Meeting report

The Chairperson welcomed Members of the Portfolio Committee and officials from the Gauteng Provincial Government (GPG) and the Department. The meeting commenced slightly later than scheduled due to technical difficulties.

The Chairperson outlined the agenda, which consisted of three items: a presentation and progress report on the Department’s responses to recommendations previously made by the Committee, consideration of outstanding reports and recommendations, and an update on the implementation of matters relating to company employees and associated support measures.

The Committee Secretary reported apologies from several Members and stakeholders who were unable to attend due to official commitments, medical appointments, travel arrangements, and stakeholder engagements. It was further noted that the Minister was out of the Country on official business.

The Chairperson added an apology from Ms L Arries (EFF), who was absent due to the passing of her sister. The Chairperson informed the Committee that he had spoken to her and that she was still grieving. Members were informed that the funeral would take place on Saturday and that those able to attend could do so on behalf of the Committee.

Response To the Report of the Portfolio Committee on Agriculture Onn 2025/26 Annual Performance Plan and Budget of the Department of Agriculture (DOA) and Its Entities: Vote 29, Dated 4 July 2025

Mr Bonga Msomi, Acting Deputy Director-General (DDG): Food Security and Agrarian Reform, DOA, took Members through the briefing. The Department of Agriculture responded to the recommendations made by the Portfolio Committee on Agriculture in its Budgetary Review and Recommendation Report (BRRR) of October 2025. The response outlines actions taken to improve governance, audit outcomes, organisational capacity, food security programmes, and support for agricultural entities.

Audit and Financial Management

The Department stated that it regularly engages with the Auditor-General South Africa (AGSA) through proactive reviews of its Annual Performance Plans (APPs). Internal audits are conducted quarterly, and audit improvement plans are developed to address findings and strengthen accountability. Progress reports are submitted to structures such as EXCO, National Treasury, the Audit Committee, the Minister, and Parliament. The Department aims to achieve unqualified audit outcomes and prevent repeat findings.

Performance Monitoring and Agricultural Programmes

The Department acknowledged the need for measurable indicators in its APP. It revised standardised indicators for provincial departments and indicated that conditional grant performance will be monitored more effectively from 2026/27. Although the Presidential Economic Stimulus Initiative (PESI) has ended due to funding constraints, support to vulnerable households continues through the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme (CASP).

The Department linked the Agro-processing Master Plan (AAMP) to its strategic objectives, including increasing the participation of Black farmers in agricultural production and supporting producers through blended finance initiatives.

Food and Nutrition Security

The Department is developing a new National Food and Nutrition Security Plan. The revised plan focuses on reducing food insecurity through a food systems approach, stronger intergovernmental coordination, greater involvement of private and civil society actors, and targeted interventions for vulnerable groups. The plan is expected to run from 2024 to 2033, with the initial phase concentrating on building systems and implementation capacity.

Organisational Restructuring and Vacancies

The Department reported progress on developing its Fit-for-Purpose organisational structure. Consultations with employees, labour unions, and the Department of Public Service and Administration (DPSA) have been completed. Key vacancies are being addressed, including the appointment of a Chief Financial Officer (CFO), Deputy Director-General, Chief Directors, and Directors. The Department reported an overall vacancy rate of 9%.

Digital Infrastructure

The Department reported that the Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP) is upgrading its Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system to improve data collection, analysis, record-keeping, and financial management processes.

Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) Vaccine Facility

The Department continues to engage with National Treasury regarding funding for the Agricultural Research Council’s (ARC) proposed FMD vaccine manufacturing facility. Treasury requested a revised Budget Facility for Infrastructure application, which the ARC submitted for consideration.

Commercialisation and Resource Mobilisation

The National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC) Resource Mobilisation Strategy has been approved by the Minister. The ARC is pursuing approval to establish a commercialisation entity and is engaging Treasury and the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC) to develop public-private partnerships and generate additional revenue streams.

State Entities

The Department reported that entities such as the ARC, OBP, and NAMC are implementing audit improvement plans. The SAVC continues to receive clean audits, while the PPECB has no outstanding audit action plans. Progress reports will continue to be submitted to Parliament.

OBP and NAMC Staffing Challenges

The OBP has advertised its CEO position and is using recruitment agencies to attract candidates. It is improving its production equipment. The NAMC continues to experience difficulties filling finance vacancies because it cannot offer competitive salaries. To retain staff, it relies on non-financial incentives such as flexible work arrangements, training opportunities, and staff recognition programmes.

Seed Banks and Food Banks

The Department clarified that seed banks fall under the Directorate for Plant Genetic Resources, while food banks are primarily the responsibility of the Department of Social Development.

Veterinary Skills Retention

The Department noted that public-sector veterinarians receive salaries equivalent to medical doctors. A study is being conducted to understand why many veterinarians leave South Africa after completing compulsory community service and to identify ways to improve retention.

The response demonstrates that the Department has taken steps to address the Committee’s recommendations, particularly in improving audit outcomes, strengthening governance, filling vacancies, advancing food security planning, supporting agricultural entities, and pursuing infrastructure investment for FMD vaccine production. However, several initiatives remain in progress and will require ongoing monitoring by Parliament to ensure implementation and measurable results.

Department of Agriculture Response to the Portfolio Committee's 2025/26 Annual Performance Plan and Budget Recommendations

The Department of Agriculture responded to recommendations made by the Portfolio Committee on Agriculture regarding governance, biosecurity, farmer support, veterinary services, monitoring and evaluation, agricultural colleges, and transformation programmes.

1. Capacity Building and Performance Management

The Department stated that it works closely with the Agricultural Research Council and the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation to strengthen planning, monitoring, and evaluation. Ongoing capacity development is being implemented to improve performance and alignment with national priorities.

2. Stakeholder Coordination and Accountability

Several coordination structures are in place, including the Chief Executive Officer Steering Forum, MinMec, MinTech, and agricultural value chain roundtables. The Department relies on internal audit, risk management, and audit committees to strengthen accountability and governance.

3. Foot-and-Mouth Disease and Biosecurity Funding

The Department is engaging National Treasury to secure approximately R1.2 billion for a Biosafety Level 4 Foot-and-Mouth Disease Vaccine Production Facility at the Agricultural Research Council. The Minister has encouraged public-private partnerships and interim licensing arrangements to reduce dependence on imported vaccines.

4. National Biosecurity Hub

The National Biosecurity Hub, established through a partnership between the Department of Agriculture, the Department of Science and Innovation, and the University of Pretoria, is being formalised through a Charter and governance structure. Funding has been secured from several institutions, and activities include:

- Hosting a National Biosecurity Summit.

- Developing strategic biosecurity priorities.

- Training interns and scientists.

- Improving surveillance and early warning systems.

5. Veterinary Services

The Department indicated that reports on the Community Service Programme for veterinarians and plans to address shortages of veterinary professionals have been prepared and attached as annexures.

6. Legislation and Policy Reviews

A legislative and policy review programme with timelines and resources has been compiled and submitted as an annexure.

7. Farmer Production Support Units

The Department acknowledged challenges affecting Farmer Production Support Units, including governance disputes, vandalism, staff shortages, and inactivity. Following the transfer of responsibility to the newly established Department of Agriculture, efforts are underway to restore functionality through security measures, provincial support plans, and targeted interventions.

8. Conditional Grants

Detailed annual implementation plans for the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme, Ilima Letsema, and LandCare grants have been prepared and submitted to Parliament.

9. Monitoring and Evaluation

The Department has developed frameworks to monitor the performance of provincial departments and public entities. Quarterly reviews, site visits, evidence verification, and collaboration with National Treasury and Statistics South Africa are used to improve accountability and programme implementation.

10. Blended Finance Scheme and AgriBEE Fund

The Department reported that:

- Ten AgriBEE Fund applications worth R279.6 million were received.

- Four applications worth R138.7 million were recommended for due diligence.

- Six approved projects worth R87.9 million were under implementation.

- The Fund balance stood at approximately R454.9 million by June 2025.

11. Agro-processing and Cooperative Development

Support programmes included:

- Training preparation for eight agricultural cooperatives in the Eastern Cape.

- Food safety training initiatives for agro-processing entrepreneurs across several provinces.

- Expected benefits for approximately 89 cooperative farmers and 100 agro-processing enterprises.

12. Governance of Public Entities

The Department confirmed quarterly engagements with the Agricultural Research Council, the Onderstepoort Biological Products organisation, and the National Agricultural Marketing Council to address governance issues and critical vacancies.

13. Agricultural Colleges

The Minister of Agriculture and the Minister of Higher Education and Training agreed to accelerate the integration of agricultural colleges into the higher education system. Approval of the Institutional Types Policy was targeted for October 2025.

14. Trading Accounts for Agricultural Colleges

The Department acknowledged limited progress in reinstating trading accounts for agricultural colleges but stated that discussions with provincial departments and provincial treasuries are ongoing to resolve the issue.

15. Livestock Trade and Protection of Local Farmers

The Department noted that imports are regulated through the Animal Improvement Act and the Animal Diseases Act to protect local livestock industries from disease risks and inferior breeds. It indicated that trade agreements must comply with animal health standards.

16. Land Bank Accountability

The Department has begun improving reporting systems and information-sharing arrangements with institutions involved in agricultural funding. However, discussions regarding the Land Bank's broader developmental role have not yet taken place.

17. Farm Worker Development

The Department committed to increasing awareness of the role of farm workers and ensuring that farmer support programmes encourage compliance with labour standards and provide training opportunities. It called for greater involvement by AgriSETA and agricultural colleges in skills development initiatives for farm workers.

The Department generally accepted the Portfolio Committee's recommendations and reported ongoing actions to strengthen governance, biosecurity, monitoring systems, farmer support programmes, veterinary services, agricultural education, and transformation initiatives. Progress has been made in several areas, particularly biosecurity and stakeholder coordination, although challenges remain regarding agricultural colleges, Farmer Production Support Units, Land Bank accountability, and veterinary capacity.

Update On the Foot and Mouth Disease Situation and Mass Vaccination Strategy

Dr Emily Mogajane, Chief Director for Biosecurity Coordination, Department of Agriculture, took Members through the briefing. The Department of Agriculture briefed the Portfolio Committee on Agriculture on the spread of Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD), progress with vaccination efforts, and measures being implemented to contain the outbreak.

Current Situation

- Foot and Mouth Disease outbreaks have been reported in all provinces.

- New cases were reported in:

-- Northern Cape (Kimberley and Hopetown)

-- Limpopo (Blouberg Municipality, Marble Hall, Sekhukhune, and Steilloop)

-- Western Cape (George)

-- KwaZulu-Natal (four new cases since the previous month).

- Limpopo outbreaks were of particular concern due to a high virus load and high mortality rates.

- The Section 10 disease-control scheme remains in force, and preparations for implementation are underway.

Growth in Outbreaks

- Reported outbreaks increased significantly from 932 cases on 2 March 2026 to 2 034 cases on 22 May 2026.

- The provinces with the highest numbers of reported outbreaks were:

-- Free State: 579

-- KwaZulu-Natal: 334

-- North West: 327

-- Gauteng: 274

-- Mpumalanga: 238.

Vaccine Procurement

To strengthen the vaccination campaign, South Africa imported vaccines from international suppliers:

- Biogenesis Bago (Argentina):

-- 2.5 million doses already received.

-- A permit has been requested for an additional 5 million doses.

- Dollvet (Türkiye):

-- 1.5 million doses initially received.

-- Approval granted for the export of 6 million doses.

-- Deliveries of 2 million doses were made on 29 April and 12 May 2026, with another 2 million expected by the end of May.

Vaccination Progress

- A total of 5.23 million vaccine doses had been allocated nationally.

- However, only 3.35 million animals had been vaccinated by 26 May 2026.

- KwaZulu-Natal recorded the highest number of vaccinated animals (918 193), followed by the Free State (515 467) and Eastern Cape (489 979).

Reasons for Slow Vaccination

The Department identified several challenges:

- Initial vaccine allocations to some provinces were too low.

- Shortages of essential resources such as ear tags and syringes.

- Limited travel budgets and kilometre allocations for vaccination teams.

Vaccine Allocation

Additional vaccine allocations were distributed across provinces, including:

- North West: 332 000 doses

- Free State: 266 100 doses

- KwaZulu-Natal: 192 000 doses

- Limpopo: 180 000 doses

- Eastern Cape: 174 350 doses.

Allocation to Industries

Specific vaccine allocations were made to key livestock industries:

- Dairy industry: 100 000 doses

- Stud industry: 50 000 doses

- Pig industry: 150 000 doses.

Dairy Industry Vaccinations

- A total of 634 886 dairy animals had been vaccinated.

- KwaZulu-Natal recorded the highest dairy vaccination uptake (360 159 animals), followed by Eastern Cape (124 303) and Western Cape (98 808).

Expanding Diagnostic Capacity

- Two additional laboratories, in KwaZulu-Natal and the Western Cape, are being added to strengthen testing capacity.

- Discussions are underway regarding workflows and coordination with the Agricultural Research Council.

Employment of Animal Health Technicians

- Budget limitations mean only 150 Animal Health Technicians will be appointed.

- They will be employed as Assistant Agricultural Practitioners and deployed by provinces to support vaccination efforts.

- Red Meat Industry Services has already appointed 20 technicians, with several allocated to the North West and Free State provinces.

The presentation highlights that Foot and Mouth Disease continues to spread across South Africa despite extensive vaccination efforts. While more than five million vaccine doses have been secured and additional diagnostic and personnel capacity is being developed, vaccination progress remains slower than required because of resource and logistical constraints. The Department recommended that the Portfolio Committee note the contents of the report.

Discussion

Mr C Smit (DA) requested clarity on the implications of the recent court judgement relating to Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) and asked the Deputy Minister to explain how the ruling would affect the Department’s operations going forward.

He raised concerns regarding restrictions affecting farmers’ ability to assist with disease control measures, including providing transport for technicians and facilitating the practical training of student veterinary technicians who require field experience, such as administering injections.

Mr Smit questioned what measures the Department was taking to hold provinces accountable for failures in tagging, tracing and movement control. He noted that the Committee had previously requested provincial MECs to appear before Parliament, arguing that while national government develops policy frameworks, implementation largely rests with provincial veterinary services.

He expressed concern that the lifting of certain traceability and movement-control protocols had created a regulatory vacuum, with inconsistent approaches across provinces and limited accountability. He questioned whether the national department should establish uniform protocols to address these gaps.

Mr Smit warned that inadequate movement controls were contributing to the continued spread of FMD and undermining containment efforts. He stated that vaccination alone would not be sufficient without effective control measures and robust animal traceability systems.

He further highlighted reports that some provinces were failing to issue the necessary documentation and identification tags, making it difficult to determine which animals had been vaccinated. This, he argued, could compromise monitoring efforts and reduce the effectiveness of the vaccination programme.

Mr Smit asked the Department to outline its long-term strategy, particularly how it intended to ensure that significant public expenditure on vaccination and disease control would achieve the desired outcome of restoring South Africa’s FMD-free status.

Finally, he sought the Department’s views on whether a large-scale national vaccination programme should be considered as an immediate crisis-management measure to bring the disease under control before pursuing the longer-term objective of regaining World Health FMD-free status. He noted that some stakeholders believed mass vaccination may be necessary given the current scale of the outbreak.

Mr A Trollip (ActionSA) raised several concerns regarding the Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) report. He noted that the disease continued to spread across the country, including in the Western Cape, and expressed concern that incidents were increasing across all nine provinces.

He questioned the apparent non-adherence to disease-control measures by both provincial and national authorities. Referring to information shared by stakeholders, he alleged that ear tagging, reporting and vaccination processes were not being consistently implemented, which, in his view, called into question the reliability of the figures presented to the Committee. He requested an explanation from the Department regarding the reasons for non-compliance in certain provinces.

Mr Trollip acknowledged that Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal appeared to be showing some stability despite being at the centre of the outbreak and asked the Department to explain the factors contributing to this apparent improvement.

He sought clarity on the number of vaccines that had been procured and delivered to South Africa. He noted an apparent discrepancy between figures indicating that approximately nine million vaccine doses had arrived in the country and reports suggesting that 14.5 million doses had been procured.

Mr Trollip expressed concern that the number of vaccines distributed to provinces exceeded the number of vaccines administered to animals. He argued that the data suggested the primary challenge was not vaccine availability, but rather the administration of vaccines. He cited examples where substantial numbers of doses remained unused despite being allocated to provinces. Highlighting the contrast between the Department’s conclusion that vaccine availability remained the main challenge and the reported vaccine stock levels in provinces. In his view, the figures indicated that the greater challenge was ensuring that vaccines reached livestock.

Mr Trollip further referred to challenges identified in the report, including low initial vaccine allocations, shortages of tags and syringes, and limitations on travel distances for officials. He described these constraints as serious shortcomings in the vaccination rollout programme and suggested that they contributed to stakeholder frustration and legal challenges against the Department.

He requested clarity on the role of Red Meat Industry Services (RMIS) in supporting the employment of animal health technicians. Specifically, he asked about the conditions of employment of these technicians, who they were accountable to, and whether a private entity had been authorised to employ animal health technicians on behalf of the Department.

Finally, Mr Trollip asked whether, considering the operational constraints and the apparent surplus of vaccines held by provinces, the Department would consider releasing vaccines directly to the livestock industry and animal owners to facilitate broader vaccination efforts.

Ms Rosemary Capa, Deputy Minister of Agriculture, requested clarity on the structure of questioning during the meeting. She asked that members be allowed to group or separate their questions particularly distinguishing between Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) issues and other matters so that responses could be clearer and easier to follow. She expressed concern that mixed questioning made it difficult to respond accurately and suggested that allowing members to ask questions in structured segments would improve clarity and recall of issues.

The Chairperson agreed with the need for better organisation of questions and proposed that questions be separated into thematic blocks, particularly isolating FMD-related issues from other agenda items such as budget matters. He further requested that the Department respond in a structured manner, addressing FMD questions first before moving on to other topics. The Department should clearly assign responsibility for answering specific questions, especially where technical clarification was required, and that responses should be well organised according to the categories of questions raised.

Mr Z E Mthethwa (MKP) referred to a debate held approximately two weeks earlier, during which he raised concerns about the Department’s approach to budgeting and planning. He argued that the agricultural sector remains characterised by a “parallel” structure that has persisted since 1994. On the one hand, the established commercial farming sector continues to dominate, while on the other, previously disadvantaged communities remain excluded from meaningful participation in the agricultural economy.

He stated that departmental planning and Annual Performance Plans should address both sectors simultaneously by introducing interventions that enable emerging and historically disadvantaged farmers to enter the mainstream agricultural economy. However, after listening to the presentation, he found little evidence of transformative measures. He remarked that the concept of transformation appeared to be lost within the extensive information provided and that previously disadvantaged individuals could not easily identify how the Department’s programmes would benefit them.

Mr Mthethwa expressed concern about the separation of rural development and agriculture functions. He argued that this had created unintended consequences, particularly for emerging farmers. While the Department continued to focus on emerging farmers, it no longer controlled one of the most fundamental requirements for their growth, namely land. Since land reform now falls under a different department, he questioned how the Department could meaningfully report on the progress of emerging farmers. In his view, this arrangement would result in limited growth, transformation and reporting on improvements in the status of emerging farmers, as a key determinant of their success was outside the Department’s mandate.

He further noted that approximately 25 million South Africans live in rural communities under traditional authorities. He argued that this segment of the population was largely absent from departmental reporting because matters relating to traditional authorities often fall under the Department of Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs (CoGTA). He stressed that the issue was not the institution of traditional leadership itself, but rather the conditions experienced by people in rural communities. He questioned how the Department measured the economic participation and development of these communities when they did not appear to form part of its reporting framework.

Mr Mthethwa added that many unemployed black agricultural graduates originated from rural communities. He argued that their challenges were compounded by inadequate infrastructure, limited economic opportunities and continued exclusion from the agricultural economy. Although many had acquired agricultural qualifications, they often returned to unemployment because ownership and participation in the agricultural sector remained concentrated within the established commercial farming sector. As a result, he argued that large sections of the population remained outside meaningful economic participation due to the absence of targeted interventions.

Turning to foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), Mr Mthethwa noted reports that outbreaks had occurred in commercial farming areas. He questioned the source and transmission of the disease and suggested that further investigation was required to determine whether outbreaks were primarily caused by animal movements or whether other factors were contributing to the spread. He said the increasing number of outbreaks made it difficult to understand the exact causes and progression of the disease and called for clearer explanations.

He further observed that emerging farmers often lacked the infrastructure and resources necessary to contain disease outbreaks effectively. Given that certain areas had been identified as epicentres of infection, he suggested that the Department should focus intensive interventions in those areas to reduce the risk of infected animals being moved elsewhere. He warned that farmers who were unable to sell their livestock due to restrictions might be tempted to move animals illegally in search of markets, thereby increasing the risk of further transmission.

Mr Mthethwa stressed that farmers faced severe financial pressures when they were unable to sell livestock while still having to bear feeding and maintenance costs. He therefore called for practical solutions that went beyond vaccination and animal treatment. He argued that the Department should develop mechanisms to assist affected farmers and prevent the unintended consequences of prolonged movement restrictions. He noted concerns around the traceability of livestock and the monitoring of animals sold through auctions, particularly where uncertainty existed regarding their disease status.

The Chairperson warned that Mr Mthethwa risked confusing the Deputy Minister by stating they requested no separate protection. The Chairperson noted that the explanation was becoming confusing, even if the intention was to use the Deputy Minister as an example for clarity.

Mr Mthethwa replied that he assumed the team was present to note all aspects of the discussion. He acknowledged that his explanation might seem confusing but stated the team would capture what they could hear. He maintained that he was abiding by parliamentary rules regarding previously answered questions. He noted he had faced opposition claiming the response did not match his written notes, but concluded that if he was causing confusion, he would stop speaking.

Ms S Davids (ANC) raised concerns regarding the Department’s response to the outbreak of foot-and-mouth disease (FMD). She noted that the Department had secured and imported 5.2 million vaccine doses from Argentina and Türkiye but had vaccinated only 3.3 million animals, representing approximately 64% of the available vaccines.

She questioned what measures the Department had put in place to ensure that the outbreak would be contained within the next two to three months. While the Department had provided explanations relating to logistics, distances and animal identification systems, she expressed concern about the pace of vaccinations. She asked whether the target of vaccinating herds by the end of the year remained achievable and whether the containment of the outbreak was still a realistic objective. The Department’s reliance on logistical explanations raised questions about the urgency with which the vaccination programme was being implemented.

Ms Davids further noted that the presentation did not provide clear weekly vaccination targets for each province, nor did it provide details on vaccination backlogs in areas affected by outbreaks. She requested greater transparency regarding provincial targets, progress and outstanding vaccination requirements.

On intergovernmental coordination and budget implementation, Ms Davids asked what specific measures had been introduced since the Budget Vote to ensure that provinces and municipalities were implementing FMD biosecurity measures consistently across the country.

Referring to the proposed Biosecurity Council, she requested clarity on when the Council would become fully operational, who would serve on it, and what authority it would possess to address and overcome departmental silos.

Regarding budget and resource allocation, Ms Davids referred to concerns previously raised by Members about the Department continuing to function as the sole regulator while struggling to contain disease outbreaks. She asked what steps the Department had taken to address concerns regarding the outsourcing of certain regulatory functions and whether alternative arrangements were being considered to strengthen the country’s biosecurity and disease-control capacity.

Ms Davids further referred to the Committee’s Budgetary Review and Recommendations Report (BRRR), which had highlighted a R2 billion funding gap before the new vaccine production facility became operational. She asked whether the National Treasury had identified funding to address this shortfall and requested a timeline for the completion and operationalisation of the facility. She sought clarity on how and when the facility would reduce South Africa’s reliance on imported vaccines.

Turning to performance management and accountability, Ms Davids noted that the Auditor-General had previously raised concerns regarding the Department’s performance indicators and targets, finding that some targets were not sufficiently measurable or linked to actual performance outcomes. She asked which indicators in the 2025/26 Annual Performance Plan (APP) had been revised to address these concerns and align with SMART principles. She requested specific examples of how these changes will be done and how the revised indicators would improve reporting. She further asked how the revised indicators would respond directly to the shortcomings identified by the Auditor-General.

On executive response and institutional coordination, Ms Davids noted that the BRRR had relied on briefings from the Auditor-General, the Office of the Budget and Treasury (OBT), the National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC), and the Perishable Products Export Control Board (PPECB). She asked what measures the Department had implemented since October 2024 to coordinate the work of these entities and address the governance, performance and accountability gaps identified by the Auditor-General.

Ms N A Ndalane (ANC) asked how Parliament would receive real-time data on foot-and-mouth disease infection numbers, transmission rates, progress in controlling the outbreak, and its economic impact going forward. Which programmes within the Department's budget had been prioritised in response to foot-and-mouth disease being declared a national disaster, and the extent of the funding allocated to these interventions.

Referring to the Budgetary Review and Recommendation Report, the foot-and-mouth disease outbreak, and biosecurity measures, she asked what measurable progress had been made since October 2024 towards eradicating foot-and-mouth disease and how the Department was monitoring vaccination rates across provinces.

On the budget, she asked about the current utilisation rate of the Agricultural Land Holding Account and what specific measures had been implemented to accelerate expenditure from the account.

Dr N Nkabane (ANC) indicated that her first question related to the 2025/26 Budget Vote response, specifically recommendation 6.17, which focused on prioritising farm workers as a category within the Department’s farmer development and support programmes, including skills training through agricultural colleges. She noted that, given South Africa’s high unemployment rate, the role of the Agricultural Sector Education and Training Authority in supporting skills development was critical. She referred to the Department’s response, which highlighted the need for collaboration between the Agricultural Sector Education and Training Authority, the Department of Agriculture and agricultural colleges. She asked what progress had been made in reviewing agricultural college curricula to ensure that they were aligned with the demands and skills requirements of the agricultural sector.

Dr Nkabane recalled previous discussions regarding the reconsideration of the institutional location of agricultural colleges, including proposals for their transfer either to the higher education sector or to the Department of Agriculture. She requested an update on the progress made in processing this reconsideration and indicated that such information would assist the Committee in planning future oversight and monitoring activities.

Her second question related to the foot-and-mouth disease outbreak and the recent court judgement referred to by Mr Smit. She sought clarity from the Deputy Minister and the Department on whether the judgement would be appealed. If not, she asked how the Department intended to integrate private vaccination initiatives into its broader foot-and-mouth disease containment strategy.

Referring to slide eight of the presentation, Dr Nkabane noted that vaccination efforts were reportedly progressing slowly due to shortages of critical materials, including ear tags and syringes. She asked what immediate interventions had been implemented to address these shortages. While the Department had indicated that it was engaging provinces to resolve bottlenecks, she requested further details on the specific measures being taken and the expected timeframe for resolving these challenges.

She referred to slide 13, which mentioned the deployment of animal health technicians. She requested a provincial breakdown of the number of animal health and veterinary professionals that would be deployed to support the vaccination campaign and ensure its effective implementation.

On biosecurity and containment measures, she noted the Department’s indication that the disease was spreading across borders, including references to eSwatini and neighbouring areas. She asked what additional biosecurity enforcement measures were being implemented beyond vaccination, particularly along livestock transport routes, at dip tanks, and in border areas, to prevent further spread of the disease.

Regarding the financial and economic impact of the outbreak, she asked whether the Department had conducted any desktop analysis on the estimated economic impact of the foot-and-mouth disease outbreak for the livestock sector, the broader agricultural industry and agricultural exports. If such assessments had been undertaken, she requested an update on the current findings.

Lastly, she enquired about monitoring, evaluation and long-term preparedness measures. She asked whether mechanisms had been established to strengthen early detection, surveillance and rapid response capacity for future outbreaks. She said that foot-and-mouth disease outbreaks were not new and were likely to occur again in the future. She therefore sought assurance that the Department was strengthening its ability to detect outbreaks early and respond timeously rather than remaining reactive in its approach.

In closing, Dr Nkabane welcomed the presentations and expressed appreciation for learning that approximately 40 million animals had been vaccinated thus far.

The Chairperson said that she had received questions from a member of the public, which she would read as submitted. Before doing so, she raised the issue of agricultural colleges. She said the Committee should receive a presentation from the Departmental official responsible for agricultural colleges. She noted that she had spoken to the official during the previous week and believed the Committee needed a comprehensive briefing on the activities, challenges, and plans relating to agricultural colleges. She said the official should explain what the Department was doing in this area and provide clarity on current developments.

Mr Dipepeneneng Serage, Deputy Director-General (DDG): Agricultural Production, Health, and Food Safety, responded to the question regarding the court case and noted that the Minister, John Steenhuisen, had already issued a public statement on the matter. He explained that one of the key issues raised in the application by Sakeliga, the Southern African Agri Initiative, and Free State Agriculture was the request for farmers to be allowed to procure and administer vaccines privately through qualified veterinary professionals.

Mr Serage stated that the Minister had issued a Routine Vaccination Scheme in the Government Gazette in terms of Section 10 of the Animal Diseases Act, 1994. The scheme provides a legal framework through which farmers may participate in vaccination programmes under prescribed conditions. He explained that Section 10 empowers the Minister to establish vaccination schemes with specific requirements relating to registration, monitoring, record-keeping and compliance. Using Foot-and-Mouth Disease as an example, he noted that where the State is not conducting vaccinations, farmers may be authorised to vaccinate their livestock through the scheme, provided they comply with the stipulated conditions.

Mr Serage said that the scheme effectively enables the activities that the applicants had sought through the court application. He noted that the Department had not yet studied the full judgement, as it had only recently been delivered, but had reviewed the summary. He pointed out that the judgement recognised the applicants' right to legally import vaccines. He said that the operative word was “legally”, which triggers compliance with all applicable regulatory requirements. As such, the court judgement does not exempt applicants from complying with existing legislation governing the importation of biological materials.

He explained that the importation of vaccines remains subject to the provisions of Section 6 of the Animal Diseases Act and the Medicines and Related Substances Act, 1965 (Act 101 of 1965). Any party wishing to import vaccines must first obtain the necessary permits and approvals. He noted that before a permit could be issued, applicants would need to comply with the Medicines and Related Substances Act, which is administered by the Department of Health and regulates the importation of veterinary materials. The Department of Health could not issue the required authorisation without concurrence from the Department of Agriculture, and this demonstrated how the two departments worked together in regulating vaccine imports.

Mr Serage stated that the Minister’s position was correct and consistent with the court judgement because the Section 10 scheme, which had now been gazetted for public consumption, allows commercial and other livestock farmers to identify vaccine manufacturers anywhere in the world. Farmers could source vaccines that match South African circulating strains, provided the vaccines are matched by reputable reference laboratories. Some laboratories use the Pirbright Institute in the United Kingdom, while others use the Botswana Vaccine Institute or local institutions such as the Agricultural Research Council. Once proof is provided that the vaccine is compatible with circulating field strains in South Africa, farmers may apply for permission to import the vaccines. Following the necessary approvals from the relevant departments and the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority, the vaccines may be imported.

For quality and regulatory purposes, whether the vaccine is purchased by government or the private sector, it must be stored at facilities operated by Onderstepoort Biological Products or at Biosafety Level 3 facilities that meet the required standards. Mr Serage further explained that, after importation, provincial departments would oversee the distribution and use of the vaccines. Farmers would be required to maintain detailed records, including animal identification and ear-tagging information, to ensure traceability. This would enable authorities to track animal movements and identify which vaccines had been administered to particular animals.

He stated that, instead of appealing the court judgement, the Department would prefer to work collaboratively with Sakeliga and other agricultural organisations rather than pursue lengthy legal processes. He suggested that the Department engage with these organisations to determine the size and location of their livestock herds so that provincial authorities could allocate resources appropriately and support vaccination efforts.

Mr Serage noted that South Africa’s livestock population was estimated at approximately 14 million animals and that achieving vaccination coverage of at least 80 per cent would require the vaccination of approximately 11.2 million animals. At present, only about 3.8 million animals had been vaccinated, indicating that substantially greater efforts were required. He argued that participation by organised agriculture would assist the Department in increasing vaccination coverage and controlling the spread of Foot-and-Mouth Disease.

Mr Serage added that private sector involvement in vaccine procurement had already been taking place prior to the court application. He recalled that when 990 000 vaccine doses were imported earlier in the year, some doses had been made available to the private sector. The Department’s objective had therefore been to establish a formal system that would allow farmers to undertake vaccination activities while ensuring appropriate oversight and accountability. The Department’s position was not opposed to private vaccination initiatives but was focused on maintaining proper controls, traceability and regulatory compliance across the livestock sector. He noted that the Department had been engaged in mediation and discussions with stakeholders before the court proceedings and believed that the newly established Section 10 scheme now provides the legal mechanism through which farmers can participate in vaccination programmes in a structured and coordinated manner.

He explained that when vaccines were being imported from Botswana, RIMS, a private pharmacy organisation, together with other authorised entities, were permitted to procure vaccines directly. He stated that the Department allowed such entities to purchase vaccines themselves while the State maintained oversight and control over the process.

Mr Serage said that in this instance vaccines were ordered from the Botswana Vaccine Institute, following the established regulatory process, including Section 21 authorisations issued by SAHPRA and Section 6 permits issued by the Department of Agriculture.

He added that this process had been followed consistently in similar cases. He noted that there had been limited understanding of why court action was pursued at the time, as the Department had not been able to prevent the procurement once the required authorisations had been granted.

Mr Serage stated that, in the main, most of the questions raised by Members were complimentary. He indicated that Mr Msomi was on standby to answer questions from Dr Nkabane, particularly those relating to agricultural colleges and developments within that landscape.

On the issue of animals being tagged, in response to a question from Mr Mthethwa, he clarified that this was no longer the position. The requirement was that animals must be tagged and traceable in relation to the vaccines administered to them.

Referring to the question posed by Mr Smit, Mr Serage remarked that it appeared the Mass Vaccination Strategy document approved by Cabinet had not yet been circulated widely.

He explained that this formed part of a ten-year strategy that had been presented and approved, although it was recognised as a very costly strategy. Nevertheless, the Department intended implementing it.

On the lifting of Disease Management Areas, Mr Serage explained that, in terms of the law, when sporadic outbreaks occur, authorities concentrate on the epicentre and a surrounding ten-kilometre radius and declare a Disease Management Area. During that period, animals may not move into or out of the affected area. However, the Department had recently lifted the Disease Management Area in KwaZulu-Natal because it no longer made sense to maintain such restrictions while implementing a mass vaccination programme.

Responding to Mr Trollip’s question regarding ear tags, Mr Serage said this remained a challenge. Members were aware that animal health is a concurrent function shared by national and provincial government. The expectation from national government, after ensuring that the rules and legislation are in place, is that provincial departments should procure ear tags, syringes and other necessary materials and ensure that vaccinations are carried out.

He explained that six per cent of the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme allocation is specifically earmarked for biosecurity. This funding is intended to enable provinces to purchase ear tags and related materials. Consequently, provinces should not cite a lack of funding as an excuse because the allocation is specifically intended to support biosecurity measures. However, he expressed concern that some provinces may be vaccinating animals without properly tagging them. This would create serious traceability challenges because authorities would not be able to determine which animals had been vaccinated and which vaccines had been administered.

Addressing the issue of RIMS appointments, Mr Serage stated that the appointments would occur in the same way as those made by any other company. In response to Mr Trollip’s question, he said the Department could not prevent RIMS from appointing individuals. He noted that the question had two parts. Firstly, whether the Department was involved in deciding who RIMS deploys, and secondly, what control measures exist. He explained that RIMS is a consortium of farmers and allocates personnel to its member farmers according to its own operational requirements. All animal health technicians are registered with the South African Veterinary Council. They are bound by registration requirements, ethical standards and the relevant legislation. It is therefore expected that they understand what may and may not be done in terms of their professional duties. He said the situation was no different from that of private veterinarians. The Department does not determine where such professionals are deployed. Referring to the Chairperson’s question regarding the difference between 16 and 20 appointments, he stated that the Department was not involved in those appointments and had no control over how personnel were recruited or remunerated. The only requirement was that anyone acting as an animal health technician must be registered with the South African Veterinary Council. If they are not registered, they would be acting unlawfully. If they are registered, they are expected to perform their duties in accordance with the law and their registration requirements.

Mr Serage further noted that provincial kilometre limits for officials posed a challenge. On the Biosecurity Council, Mr Serage explained that its mandate was to create additional national capacity in biosecurity. He noted that biosecurity was not generally taught as a standalone discipline at universities. Instead, expertise was acquired through experience in institutions such as the Department, the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), or related research environments. He explained that biosecurity drew together a range of disciplines, including virology and other scientific fields, into a single integrated concept.

He stated that the Department had established a Biosecurity Hub to consolidate this expertise and strengthen national research capacity. Biosecurity, he explained, was fundamentally concerned with preventing biological diseases and pests. He described it as the prevention of diseases and pests before they occurred. Achieving this required surveillance systems and the development of specialist skills, including veterinarians, biologists, virologists, and other experts who formed part of the broader biosecurity framework. The purpose was to ensure that the country developed and retained scientific capacity in this area.

Mr Serage said that the Biosecurity Council's role was advisory only. It did not have executive authority or operational functions. He said that the Department relied on science in carrying out its responsibilities and that its role was to develop legislation and regulations informed by scientific evidence. As scientific knowledge evolved, new innovations would need to be incorporated into the legislative framework. The Council's purpose was therefore to provide scientific advice and guidance.

He explained that members of the Council came from institutions such as the University of Pretoria, the Agricultural Research Council, the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), and entities linked to the Department of Science, Technology and Innovation. Through the University of Pretoria and other institutions, the Department was able to access expertise that government might not otherwise readily obtain. He described the Council as a scientific advisory body that would assist government in developing and refining regulations.

Responding to a question raised by the Chairperson from a member of the public regarding booster vaccinations, Mr Serage described it as a good question but cautioned that it needed to be answered within the correct context. However, he noted that many animals in the feedlot industry did not live beyond 18 to 24 months before being slaughtered.

The need for booster vaccinations depended on factors such as the age at which animals were vaccinated and whether the animals remained in production. Dr Mogajane commented on the Department's plans regarding animal identification and future disease-control measures. He noted that a Members had raised the issue of animal identification and future planning and explained that the Department intended to move towards a compartmentalisation system. He explained that zoning had limitations because the declaration of a disease outbreak in a single area could result in the entire zone losing its status. Compartments, however, offered a more effective approach. The Department's intention was to identify compartments where there was no longer any infection, thereby allowing exports to proceed from those areas. He added that South Africa was currently negotiating health certification arrangements with Jordan for the export of meat from vaccinated animals. He stated that CASP, Ilima/Letsema and similar programmes were repeatedly identified as failing initiatives across different oversight visits. He questioned whether the Committee was simply receiving reports without meaningful accountability, stating that the Department continued to provide forward-looking plans rather than addressing systemic failures identified in previous oversight reports.

He further raised concerns about agricultural colleges, stating that their transfer to higher education had been discussed for many years without resolution. He questioned the effectiveness of blended finance schemes, agro-processing initiatives, and pre-training assessments, asking where measurable success had been achieved.

He referred to entities such as OBP, the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), and the National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC), stating that operational issues such as vacancies and governance failures were repeatedly reported without corrective action. He stated that despite the Committee’s oversight work and formal recommendations, the Department’s responses remained unchanged year after year, with no tangible evidence of implementation or improvement.

He further raised concerns about repeated failures in projects funded through the Land Bank, CASP, Ilima/Letsema, and other programmes. He stated that significant public funds were being invested in projects that subsequently collapsed and questioned why this pattern continued.

He cited examples from oversight visits, including packhouse facilities and irrigation schemes that had become non-functional despite substantial investment. He stated that in some cases, farmers were forced to transport produce over long distances because infrastructure was not operational, and essential services such as electricity and veterinary support were not provided.

He concluded by expressing strong frustration, stating that the Committee was repeatedly encountering the same failures, the same officials, and the same explanations without seeing corrective action. He warned that continued repetition without accountability undermined the oversight role of the Committee and stated that the situation was becoming unacceptable.

The Chairperson recalled that when these matters had previously been discussed, Mr Trollip had written to her, and she had subsequently raised the issues with the Director-General (DG). She noted that, during the week before last, when Mr Trollip was in court and absent from the Committee meeting, she had raised the matters with the Minister. She had complained about all the issues that Members had encountered during oversight visits and expressed the hope that the Minister would be able to respond, as he had indicated at the time that he had not been fully aware of the matters she had raised.

She stressed that the issues currently being raised were already before the Minister. She stated that these were very serious matters and explained that during oversight visits Members often became emotional because they saw firsthand what communities and farmers were experiencing. Members listened directly to affected people and witnessed their circumstances personally.

Referring to an oversight visits in the Eastern Cape, she said that Members had met elderly people who had literally cried before them. She admitted that she had cried with them because she did not know what to do. When people cried before Parliament's representatives, they expected assistance because they had elected Members to represent them. She said it became extremely difficult when people raised concerns, yet there were no responses and no visible changes. She questioned where people's hope would lie if neither Parliament nor the Department addressed the problems that had been repeatedly brought to their attention. Members could only assist by instructing the Department to perform the functions for which it had been appointed.

The Chairperson referred to an example raised by Mr Trollip during an oversight visit in the North West. She said that at one of the farms it felt as though the Committee was attending a funeral. There was only a small tent, a few chairs and a cooler box with water. Members had been shocked because they had driven past productive farms before arriving at land that had not been cultivated and where only a tent had been erected.

The Committee had attempted to assist the farmer concerned. However, despite their efforts, the farmer had not been assisted and had eventually been forced to sell his farm. She explained that Members had tried to stop him from doing so. He had already negotiated a sale, and both the Committee and representatives from the Land Bank had urged him not to proceed, assuring him that assistance would be forthcoming. However, a week later the farmer wrote to her stating that he was still burdened by debts owed to various creditors. She said that the situation had not been the farmer's fault but rather the fault of the Land Bank, which had failed to take account of the farming season and crop cycle.

She added that this had not been an isolated case. Another farmer, a maize producer, had experienced similar problems. When assistance eventually arrived, he was told to plant sunflower instead because the maize season had already passed. She remarked that the farmer did not want to plant sunflower.

Mr Trollip added that the situation had been even worse. The farmer had originally been approved for maize production, while the neighbouring farm had achieved a record harvest. During the oversight visit, Members had driven past a highly productive maize farm. Although the affected farmer had been approved for maize production, the funding had not arrived in time for planting. As a result, he had applied to plant sunflower instead but was refused permission. He was instructed to plant maize long after the appropriate season had passed. Members had seen the resulting crop, which was in a poor condition. He stressed that the farmer was a capable and experienced producer, but the delayed funding had prevented him from planting maize effectively.

The Chairperson said that officials had informed the farmer that the funding could not be redirected to sunflower production because it had originally been approved for maize. She questioned whose fault it was that the maize funding had been approved and released too late.

She then referred to another project which she described as feeling like either a funeral or a party. The project involved a farmer who was supposed to be operating a broiler enterprise. Addressing the DDG directly, she stated that he should attend oversight visits personally rather than sending Ms Zelda. She explained that Ms Zelda accompanied Members regularly but was unable to provide answers to their questions. The Chairperson said that officials appeared to have become accustomed to such situations and no longer reacted appropriately. She compared it to working in a funeral parlour and becoming desensitised to death. She apologised for the comparison but stated that she observed this attitude among some officials.

She reminded officials that the projects visited during oversight were identified by the Department itself. Members did not select them independently but requested the Department to identify projects for inspection. During one visit, the woman responsible arrived dressed as though she were attending a high-level meeting, wearing high heels, while Members themselves had dressed practically for farm visits. The officials provided chairs outside and sat there while Members questioned them, yet no satisfactory answers were forthcoming.

The Chairperson noted that the Land Bank had provided approximately R24 million to the project, in addition to a further R7 million. Despite this investment, there were no chickens on the farm and only a house remained.

She said that Members had questioned the Land Bank representative responsible for project rehabilitation and turnaround interventions. However, his attitude had not been constructive or helpful. She recalled that Mr Trollip had written to her expressing the view that he did not wish to continue seeing such situations for the next three years until 2029, with no visible results from the Department despite continuous reporting and correspondence. She agreed with his sentiment and said she had conveyed the same concerns to the Minister. She asked what Members were expected to do if they were unable to help those they represented.

Mr Serage responded that the matter should perhaps be revisited specifically in relation to the BRRR process. He proposed returning to the Committee with Mr Msomi, together with an action plan and practical solutions.

He acknowledged that government officials often explained challenges rather than solving them, but he felt it was necessary to outline some of the systemic problems. He pointed out that many of the funding arrangements involved conditional grants. As a result, funds passed through numerous institutions before reaching the intended beneficiary. In his view, this complexity was created by government itself. He questioned why a farmer in the North West should have funding routed through the national department, then the Land Bank, before eventually reaching the farmer. These were systemic issues that could be changed.

Mr Serage apologised to Members regarding the report before them and stated that he found its structure unsatisfactory. Instead of grouping issues thematically, the report had lumped numerous matters together. He suggested that future reports should deal with programmes individually. For example, discussions on CASP should focus exclusively on CASP, allowing Members to engage with that programme before moving on to the next issue.

He undertook to improve future reporting and said the Department would revise the report so that it directly addressed the questions raised by Members. He acknowledged that bringing provincial officials had not helped sufficiently because coordination problems persisted. Nevertheless, until the system changed, provincial officials would need to be included. He proposed that, in future, the Department should bring not only provincial departments but the specific directors responsible for the relevant funds. Better preparation would be undertaken to ensure meaningful reporting.

Mr Serage stressed that officials should admit when things had gone wrong rather than attempting to conceal shortcomings. The Department would bring substantive answers, identify the projects concerned and ensure that the responsible officials attended meetings. If necessary, representatives from the Land Bank would be invited so that Members could receive direct answers from the appropriate institutions.

Deputy Minister Capa said she would respond briefly to some of the issues raised. He indicated that his remarks would be political rather than technical in nature and that the Acting DG would address the technical aspects.

Referring to Mr Serage's suggestion that officials should return to the Committee, she proposed that the focus should be on fixing the underlying problems affecting the Portfolio Committee and argued that the Committee's frustrations were often caused by failures in administration.

Turning to the question regarding the effect of the court judgement, he stated that the technical implications would be addressed by the Acting DG. However, she believed that a major problem lay in the Department's communication with the people it served. While individuals could choose their own actions, poor communication frequently contributed to misunderstandings.

DM Capa recalled a case involving a farmer who had received approximately R10 million in support for a farming operation. Despite this assistance, the farmer later appeared on national television and publicly accused politicians and officials of corruption. The Minister said that he had personally visited the farm when she first became Deputy Minister. During the visit, the farmer had attempted to offer him various items, which she declined. Nevertheless, the same individual later petitioned the President's Office, alleging that politicians and officials were robbing him. She stated that the farmer remained on the property to this day.

She argued that there was often a significant gap between beneficiaries and political office-bearers. If politicians had direct access to information showing exactly how much money had been allocated to specific beneficiaries, they would be less dependent on reports from provincial officials and extension officers. Politicians would then be able to monitor expenditure more effectively and ask beneficiaries directly how resources had been used.

DM Capa said that many farmers were not eager to meet politicians, yet such engagement was important because politicians could ask difficult questions regarding the use of public funds. He referred to a previous visit to North West involving farmers and an investment project. Upon observing significant disorder and frustration, he had proposed a follow-up engagement involving the Land Bank and officials responsible for coordinating relations between the Department, the Land Bank and beneficiaries. However, that proposal had never materialised.

She cautioned against interpreting the recent court victory as a reason for complacency. Although the judgement had found in favour of the Department, this did not mean officials should become arrogant. Beneficiaries needed to feel that they had meaningful relationships with the institutions responsible for supporting them.

DM Capa referred to tensions between national, provincial and district government structures. She cited vaccination campaigns as an example. During one visit, she had communicated with traditional leaders and local extension officers. However, information had not reached farmers because officials considered the matter to be the responsibility of another section dealing with biosecurity. She described this as a serious disconnect within government. Nevertheless, when he personally visited the area, farmers attended and approximately 700 animals were vaccinated in a single day.

Regarding animal identification and disease control, the Minister argued that previous systems based on dipping tanks had functioned more effectively because farmers naturally organised themselves around those facilities. She criticised newer approaches that relied on different demarcation systems and argued that the traditional dipping tank model provided a clearer and more practical framework for disease management.

DM Capa proposed that Parliament and the Department hold workshops together. She recalled that such engagements had taken place in the past and had allowed Members and officials to discuss issues openly and improve mutual understanding.

She criticised the fragmented nature of current campaigns and programmes. Disease control, infrastructure development and other agricultural interventions were often treated separately rather than being integrated. Stressed that the Department was not merely a department dealing with Foot and Mouth Disease but a Department of Agriculture responsible for a broad range of issues. Meetings and planning processes should therefore address multiple priorities in a coordinated manner.

DM Capa stated that many challenges arose because communities and stakeholders were not properly consulted or informed about programmes. She believed that district structures, local economic development officials and communities needed to be involved from the outset. He expressed support for the programmes under discussion but stressed that implementation had to improve. She added that he would continue participating actively in field activities and oversight work. Having received training in administering injections, he remarked that he was personally capable of participating in vaccination campaigns if necessary.

Closing remarks

The Chairperson thanked the Deputy Minister for her remarks and noted that the Committee appreciated the fact that he had remained for the discussion. She indicated that a follow-up meeting would be arranged to address the issues raised. The DDG would be informed in advance, and the Committee would attempt to adjust its programme to accommodate the engagement. She said that Members required the information urgently because the presentation that had been made had not satisfied them.

She requested that the Department pay particular attention to the concerns raised by Mr Trollip regarding the packhouse in the Eastern Cape. Members had visited the facility and found the situation distressing. Only one farmer was still using the packhouse, while others had stopped for various reasons. She believed that the Department could intervene and resolve many of the problems. Because the facility was no longer operating effectively, farmers were now paying additional transport costs to move their products elsewhere.

The Chairperson further noted that Mr Trollip had written to the DG on the matter. Although a response had been received, she did not believe it had adequately addressed the concerns. Farmers continued to contact both herself and Mr Trollip regarding the issue. She urged the Department to engage directly with the affected farmers, noting that the Committee had been assisting them for a long time.

She concluded by stating that the Committee would begin considering the North West oversight report the following week.

The meeting was adjourned.
