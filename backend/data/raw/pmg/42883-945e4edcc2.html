Basic Education Budget Vote Report; NSNP roll-out, challenges and review

Basic Education

05 May 2026
Chairperson: Ms Joy Maimela (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

Annual Performance Plan (APP) of Government Departments & Entities 2026/27

The Department of Basic Education (DBE) 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan and Budget covered matters such as learner support, infrastructure, budget allocations, and programme implementation. The meeting focused on persistent systemic challenges observed through oversight visits and departmental reporting, particularly on Learning and Teaching Support Materials (LTSM), school infrastructure, school nutrition, and programme funding constraints.

A key area of discussion for the Committee was the availability and use of textbooks under the LTSM policy. Members raised concerns about widespread textbook sharing among learners, which was confirmed by DBE as a common practice in some provinces, despite there being no formal policy requiring or promoting textbook sharing. DBE clarified that provinces are expected to ensure adequate textbook provision and that current procurement cycles are intended to replace and update textbooks aligned with curriculum requirements over the 2026/27 period.

The Committee also interrogated school infrastructure challenges, particularly the status of conditional assessments being conducted across provinces. Members expressed concern that existing departmental data may not reflect the actual conditions on the ground, citing issues such as incomplete infrastructure projects, poor maintenance, and safety risks in schools. DBE committed to providing written provincial breakdowns of conditional assessment findings and acknowledged the need for stronger oversight and accountability in infrastructure delivery.

A major point of concern was the continued use of temporary container classrooms as permanent infrastructure. The Chairperson highlighted that containers, originally intended as short-term relief measures for overcrowding, have in many cases become long-term classrooms without adequate maintenance or basic facilities such as electricity and ventilation. DBE was requested to provide a comprehensive assessment of the scale of this issue and a clear plan for addressing or replacing such structures.

In its National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) presentation, DBE explained that feeding schemes primarily target Quintile 1–3 schools, with some provinces extending coverage to additional learners using provincial funds. Variations in implementation across provinces were noted, with concerns raised about inconsistent coverage and operational inefficiencies. DBE confirmed that discussions are ongoing about a revised model that decentralises implementation to provinces while maintaining national guidelines.

On budget constraints and reductions, the DBE Chief Financial Officer explained that funding cuts are largely driven by fiscal constraints imposed by National Treasury. It was noted that the education sector has experienced long-term underfunding, requiring difficult prioritisation decisions. Despite this, infrastructure grants remain a key focus, with significant backlogs estimated nationally.

Additional issues included scholar transport challenges, learner safety concerns, overcrowding in schools, and the integration of Grade R practitioners into the formal system, including qualification upgrading. DBE also reaffirmed its commitment to inclusive education, including access for undocumented learners in line with constitutional obligations and court rulings.

In conclusion, the Chairperson emphasised the need for detailed written responses on unresolved issues, particularly infrastructure assessments and container classroom usage. The Committee indicated that a final oversight report will be compiled and shared with members, and that further engagements may be scheduled depending on parliamentary availability.

Meeting report

The Chairperson reflected on the recent oversight visit to Mpumalanga, noting that members felt the three-day visit had been insufficient and that the Committee would consider extending oversight activities during the third term.

The Chairperson informed members that several political parties and Committee members had requested that DBE account for issues reported in the media concerning publishers and the textbook catalogue, which had raised concerns on the credibility of departmental processes. Efforts were underway to secure a full-day meeting to address both the ELRC report and DBE’s response to these concerns.

This meeting on the Budget Review and APP engagement was important as it allows the Committee to assess if the Department plan aligns with the Medium-Term Development Plan, government priorities, and oversight findings. She highlighted the NSNP as one of the country’s most successful programmes, particularly praising the introduction of breakfast meals, which schools reported had improved learner attendance, punctuality, and participation.

Opening remarks by Minister of Education

Minister Siviwe Gwarube welcomed the opportunity to lead her delegation, stating that the meeting focused on two critical matters: the 2026/27 Budget Review and the NSNP. She emphasized that the Budget Review should not be treated merely as a compliance exercise but as an opportunity to determine if Departmental planning, budgeting, and governance are delivering measurable improvements in education outcomes. She noted that DBE’s planning framework is aligned with the National Development Plan, the Medium-Term Development Plan, and Action Plan 2029. Key priorities include strengthening foundational learning, literacy and numeracy, expanding early childhood development, improving learner retention, supporting inclusive education, strengthening teacher capacity, and improving overall system functionality.

The Minister stressed that success should not be measured only by inputs such as plans and budgets, but by outputs and outcomes, including if learners can read for meaning, acquire numeracy skills, remain in school, and learn in safe and inclusive environments. She described the NSNP as one of DBE’s most important interventions, reaching approximately 9.4 million learners in over 20,000 schools, supported by over 61,000 food handlers and more than 2,000 small and medium enterprises. The programme demonstrates the intersection between education, poverty alleviation, and social protection. She concluded by affirming DBE’s commitment to evidence-based, results-driven governance and accountability to parliamentary oversight.

DBE Annual Performance Plan 2026/27

Ms Nozipho Mbonambi, DBE Director for Strategic Planning and Reporting, presented DBE’s 2026/27 APP indicators and targets. She explained that the APP had been reviewed by the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) and the Auditor-General of South Africa (AGSA), with findings relating mainly to strengthening situational analysis, performance explanations, data sources, calculations, and verification methods. She confirmed that all findings had been addressed and the APP resubmitted.

The APP contains 66 indicators across five programmes.
- Programme 1 focuses on governance and administration, including targets for paying invoices within 30 days, resolving misconduct cases, staff capacity building, Treasury reporting, and advisory reports for the Minister.
- Programme 2, the largest programme, focuses on curriculum delivery and includes targets related to technical schools, the Second Chance Matric Programme, learner support programmes, ECD access, curriculum monitoring, mathematics and science performance, vocational and occupational subjects, female participation in technical subjects, literacy and reading strategies, bilingual education, textbook provision, teacher training, and support for learners with disabilities.
- Programme 3 addresses Teacher development and school governance, including SGB effectiveness, school management documents, Funza-Lushaka bursaries, teacher development centres, literacy and numeracy training, post provisioning norms, quality management systems, and the creation of 150,000 jobs through the Basic Education Employment Initiative.
- Programme 4 focuses on examinations and assessments, including test item development, examination papers, school administration systems, learner retention, literacy and numeracy outcomes, monitoring of underperforming schools, and sanitation monitoring.
- Programme 5 covers social support programmes, including NSNP monitoring, HIV/AIDS life skills education, school safety frameworks, social cohesion programmes, GBV awareness, psychosocial support training, and school sport participation.

Department’s Medium-Term Expenditure Framework

Mr Patrick Khunou, Chief Financial Officer, presented DBE’s MTEF budget allocations for the next three years. He explained that Treasury initially provided indicative allocations of approximately R36.3 billion, R36.6 billion, and R38.3 billion, which were later adjusted through increases and reductions. Conditional grants increased substantially, especially for Early Childhood Development. Final allocations amounted to approximately R38.2 billion for 2026/27, R41.1 billion for 2027/28, and R42.5 billion for 2028/29.

Mr Khunou explained that the budget was analysed both by programme and economic classification, including compensation of employees, goods and services, transfers and subsidies, and capital assets. He clarified that although the budget appeared to have declined by approximately R32 million compared to the previous year, the comparison should be contextualized because the previous year’s budget had received additional allocations for ECD and flood relief in KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape. The School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant had been incorporated into the Education Infrastructure Grant, resulting in decreases in capital asset allocations and increases in transfers and subsidies. Earmarked funds had decreased from R3.8 billion to R2.3 billion due to the integration of grants and the conclusion of once-off BEI training allocations. Conditional grants overall increased by approximately R1.4 billion, with notable increases for ECD and the NSNP.

National School Nutrition Programme

Mr Muzi Ndlovu, DBE Director for Health Promotion, presented on the National School Nutrition Programme. He confirmed that the programme continues to reach more than 9.4 million learners in over 20,000 schools and aims to address hunger, malnutrition, learner attendance, concentration, and educational outcomes. He outlined the policies and guidelines governing the programme, including conditional grant frameworks, financial management systems, food specifications, kitchen compliance standards, tuck shop guidelines, and school food garden initiatives.

Mr Ndlovu explained the two procurement models used by provinces. The centralized model, implemented in KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and the Western Cape, involves provincial service providers delivering food directly to schools. The decentralized model, used in provinces such as the Eastern Cape, Free State, Northern Cape, and Northwest, transfers funds directly to schools for procurement. Both models follow the same nutritional guidelines requiring protein, starch, and vegetables in meals.

He reported that the programme employs over 61,000 voluntary food handlers, mostly unemployed parents and community members, who receive a daily stipend of R17.27. Financial performance for the programme stood at approximately 100.9% expenditure in 2025/26, with some provinces slightly under or over-spending due to vacancies and accruals. Additional allocations were made to provinces such as the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga, North-West, and Northern Cape to address previous challenges related to feeding learners during April and to support breakfast meal implementation.

Mr Ndlovu described the monitoring and evaluation systems used by the programme, including national and provincial monitoring visits, annual evaluations, and a monitoring report and response system that assesses meal timing, nutritional balance, and meal quantities. Provinces such as Limpopo performed strongly, while challenges remained in some provinces where schools failed to consistently provide all three food groups.

He further highlighted partnerships with UNICEF, Tiger Brands Foundation, PepsiCo, and Unilever, which support breakfast programmes, technical assistance, and school kitchen renovations. Major challenges identified included poor financial management and stock control, inconsistent food quality, inadequate storage facilities, insufficient training, and poor kitchen infrastructure.

To address these issues, Mr Ndlovu outlined proposed reforms centred around governance and compliance, digital monitoring systems, logistics and warehousing improvements, supply chain integration with smallholder farmers, and institutionalized oversight structures. He concluded that the NSNP remains one of South Africa’s most important social support and education interventions, improving learner health, attendance, concentration, and educational performance while also creating employment opportunities in vulnerable communities.

Discussion

Mr S Mkhize (MKP) welcomed the opportunity to partake in the discussion, noting humorously that the recent oversight visit to Mpumalanga had left many members with flu symptoms due to the cold weather and travel conditions.

He referred to the Minister’s earlier remarks that the APP review process should not merely focus on activities, indicators, and compliance, but should assess alignment with national priorities and educational outcomes. While agreeing with this principle, he argued that DBE’s 2026/27 Annual Performance Plan (APP), particularly the full document rather than the presentation, was “misguided, misplaced, disconnected from reality, and disconnected from itself.”

Mr Mkhize focused extensively on the SWOT analysis contained in the APP. He acknowledged that conducting a SWOT analysis was important but criticized DBE for listing financial controls as both a strength and a weakness. He argued that this contradiction demonstrated a disjointed and poorly thought-out planning process. According to him, weaknesses identified during planning directly affect the credibility of DBE’s strategies and overall plan.

He also criticized DBE for identifying its unqualified audit outcome as a strength while failing to sufficiently acknowledge the seriousness of audit findings and irregular expenditure raised by the Auditor-General. In his view, DBE appeared unwilling to confront its governance and financial management problems honestly.

Mr Mkhize further argued that the APP lacked consistency in distinguishing between weaknesses, risks, and threats. He noted that poor or unavailable planning data appeared as a risk in one section of the APP but not as a weakness in the SWOT analysis, which he viewed as illogical, and indicative of a superficial “box-ticking exercise” rather than a genuine diagnostic process aimed at identifying and fixing problems within DBE.

He also questioned the inclusion of the Government of National Unity (GNU) as an “opportunity” in the APP. He argued that this was contradictory because, in his view, the GNU was simultaneously implementing budget cuts that negatively affected DBE. Instead, he suggested that increased budget allocations should have been identified as opportunities, particularly because many of DBE’s challenges stem from insufficient funding.

Mr Mkhize emphasized that several critical issues should have been explicitly categorized as threats in the APP, including:
lack of planning data,
budget cuts,
deteriorating and inadequate school infrastructure,
shortages of educators,
overcrowded classrooms,
delayed delivery of textbooks and Learning and Teaching Support Materials (LTSM),
and inadequate learner transport systems

He argued that these issues directly undermine DBE’s core mandate of improving educational and pedagogical outcomes and therefore should be treated as central strategic threats.

Turning to DBE’s “Theory of Change”, Mr Mkhize stated that while some elements appeared promising, the framework failed to adequately address consequence management for underperformance, improved data management systems, increased budget allocations, and scholar transport. He stressed that learner transport is a major issue affecting access to education and should have formed part of DBE’s change strategy.

Finally, he raised concerns about infrastructure funding. He noted that DBE itself had estimated a school infrastructure backlog of approximately R140 billion, yet the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework only allocated around R49 billion over three years. He questioned how DBE intended to address the remaining funding gap of roughly R90 billion. Concluding his remarks, Mr Mkhize stated that the APP was fundamentally flawed and required substantial revision to properly align priorities, strategies, risks, and budget realities.

Ms P Mngadi (MKP) noted that DBE’s budget is expected to increase from R38 billion to R42 billion over the medium term but questioned if this represented a real improvement in education funding or merely nominal growth driven by inflation and increasing learner enrolment. She asked if inflation and enrolment pressures had been factored into the projections and requested clarity on the real per-capita spending change year-on-year to better assess the true impact of the increases.

She expressed concern about a projected reduction of approximately R300 million in the Teachers, Education Human Resources and Institutional Development Programme, emphasizing that this programme is central to teacher support, recruitment, and training. She warned that the cuts could negatively affect the quality of teachers entering classrooms, worsen staffing shortages, and weaken the overall quality of education. She highlighted ongoing problems such as overcrowded classrooms, DBE’s failure to fill vacant posts, and the urgent need to strengthen teaching standards in literacy and numeracy at foundation phase level, as well as mathematics and science in intermediate and senior phases. She asked how DBE could justify the reduction and if alternative measures had been explored to avoid such cuts. She also asked how teacher training and development would be protected despite the budget reduction.

Ms Mngadi further raised concerns on the reduction in allocations for payments of capital assets and cuts to the Education Infrastructure Grant. She stated that this effectively meant there would be little or no investment in school infrastructure projects and fewer resources for building or upgrading schools. Referring to a statement made by the Deputy Minister before the Committee on 10 March, she recalled that DBE had acknowledged that accommodating the current influx of learners would require approximately 20 to 30 new schools to be built in the current year alone. She questioned how this need could be reconciled with declining infrastructure expenditure. She argued that insufficient infrastructure investment would keep children out of school because of a lack of classrooms and new schools, while also worsening overcrowding and placing additional strain on existing resources. She stressed that this was especially alarming given DBE’s own estimate of a R140 billion national school infrastructure backlog, which she described as a crisis of significant magnitude. She asked how DBE planned to address the situation.

She also interrogated DBE’s allocations for goods and services. Although the presentation indicated that spending on goods and services would increase marginally over the medium term, she pointed out that the allocation would still remain below the R3 billion allocation of the 2025/26 financial year. Instead, it would fall by approximately R800 million and settle at R2.7 billion in 2027. She requested clarity on what exactly constituted “goods and services” within DBE’s budget and asked which areas would be affected by such a major reduction. She argued that an R800 million decrease was too significant to ignore, even if some recovery was projected later in the medium term, noting that nearly R300 million would still effectively be removed from the item.

Turning to the NSNP, Ms Mngadi asked if DBE had conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis of the centralized and decentralized procurement models to evaluate their cost-benefit ratios, administrative efficiency, effectiveness in achieving programme objectives, and overall performance. She requested the findings of such an analysis and asked if DBE would consider implementing a single unified or standardized model across all provinces based on the results.

She concluded by expressing serious concern that some provinces had failed to provide meals to certain schools despite overspending their allocated budgets. She described this as a major red flag for DBE. Using KwaZulu-Natal as an example, she noted that although it was the largest beneficiary of NSNP funding, its inability to provide meals to even 1% of schools meant that thousands of learners were going hungry. She argued that provincial overspending was not translating into effective service delivery, stating that provinces were exceeding their budgets while still failing to deliver adequate results.

Ms P Xaba-Ntshaba (ANC) began by addressing the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP), expressing concern that DBE appeared to be regressing in its administration of the programme. She stated that this undermined South Africa’s ambition of providing quality fee-free education, particularly in a country where approximately 60% of the population reportedly lives below the poverty line. She emphasized that the issue was especially serious in provinces such as KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, and Limpopo, which are among the most poverty-stricken. Despite this reality, she argued that KwaZulu-Natal continued to demonstrate a high degree of inefficiency in implementing the NSNP, reflecting failures in both policy implementation and service delivery.

She questioned what consequences were imposed on provinces and officials when schools failed to provide meals to learners despite receiving their full budget allocations. She requested that DBE provide a province-by-province breakdown of schools that failed to feed learners for more than five consecutive days during the financial year. She also asked what immediate contingency mechanisms were in place when schools failed to provide meals, especially in poor rural communities where the NSNP often represented the only guaranteed daily meal for learners. In addition, she asked if DBE had assessed the impact of repeated failures to provide meals on learner attendance at schools.

Turning to the APP, Ms Xaba-Ntshaba raised concerns about planning, financial management, and institutional capacity under conditions of limited resources. She asked how DBE intended to ensure that its financial and human resource capacity adequately supported the achievement of programme outcomes while safeguarding against service delivery failures. She further questioned why the target for resolving misconduct cases had remained unchanged since the 2022/23 financial year and requested information on the average number of misconduct cases reported annually.

Mr S Louw (ANC) apologized for not using his video due to network problems He stated that the APP demonstrated alignment with government policies and priorities, particularly in advancing transformation, equity, and redress, while also seeking to remove barriers preventing learners from accessing education. He commended DBE for prioritizing Early Childhood Development (ECD) and acknowledged progress made in foundational literacy and numeracy, inclusive education, and addressing school infrastructure backlogs.

However, he raised several concerns and questions on the APP and Departmental performance. Referring to Programme One, he noted that the number of targets had remained static since the 2022/23 financial year. He argued that this was concerning and referred specifically to Table 11 on page 46, stating that DBE should present information in percentages relative to the total number of cases rather than simply using raw figures. He argued that the current format failed to present a clear and accurate picture of Departmental performance and outcomes.

Turning to Programme Two, and specifically Table 14 on page 51, Indicator 2.110 on the number of schools targeted for monitoring and evaluation in provinces, he argued that DBE needed to demonstrate the degree of impact these monitoring targets would have. He emphasized that Committee members came prepared to engage critically with the information presented and needed more meaningful data to assess Departmental performance.

Mr Louw questioned how DBE would ensure that financial and human resource capacity supported programme outcomes and prevented service delivery failures, especially under current resource constraints. He also asked why the target for resolving misconduct cases had remained unchanged since the 2022 financial year and requested the annual average number of such cases. He stressed the importance of ensuring that the APP reflected the true state of DBE and provided an honest indication of how challenges were being addressed.

He further asked what interventions, improvements, and accountability outcomes were being implemented to strengthen programme performance. Referring to current issues surrounding human resource recruitment irregularities, he asked if ICT systems and data governance reforms would improve data integrity, reliability, and evidence-based decision-making within DBE.

Mr Louw also focused on disparities in education delivery. He asked how DBE verified the accuracy of data used to monitor educational support and interventions, especially in poorer quintile one to three schools, where inequalities and structural challenges were most severe. He requested details on specific interventions being implemented to address disparities between rural and urban schools and asked how these interventions would translate into measurable improvements, particularly in relation to educator shortages and skills development.

He raised concerns about school-related gender-based violence and questioned if there was tangible evidence showing that increased stakeholder engagement and awareness campaigns were successfully reducing incidents of gender-based violence in schools. He also asked how DBE ensured that teaching and learning time was not lost while conducting these interventions.

On infrastructure, he noted that R50.4 billion had been allocated to school infrastructure over the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF) period and asked how many schools were expected to benefit from this funding. He further asked how the incorporation of the School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant into the Education Infrastructure Grant in 2026/27 would affect accountability. Specifically, he wanted to know what mechanisms would ensure provinces completed outstanding backlog projects and how far progress had advanced. He also questioned what measures were in place to prevent under-expenditure and delays in infrastructure projects, noting that infrastructure spending and budgeting remained a major concern for DBE.

Finally, on the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP), he noted that the programme had received R3.9 billion over the medium term. He asked how DBE ensured that meals met required nutritional standards and what monitoring systems were in place to prevent corruption and irregularities in school feeding schemes. He also asked how DBE evaluated the relationship between school nutrition, learner attendance, and academic performance in order to measure the programme’s actual impact. He concluded by acknowledging the growth in the number of learners benefiting from the programme, from 9 million to 9.9 million learners, before yielding the floor to other members.

Mr M Shikwambana (EFF) first addressed the situation of Grade R teachers in the Free State, stating that they had been experiencing serious challenges with DBE. According to him, these educators had been promised absorption into the mainstream education system after completing their Bachelor of Education qualifications in Foundation Phase teaching. However, despite this undertaking, many had still not been absorbed into permanent positions after five years.

He noted that the 2026/27 budget speech presented by the Minister included allocations aimed at equalising and supporting Grade R educators. Despite this, he explained that a task team meeting held in the Free State on 9 April 2026, attended by the Head of Department, Advocate Molokwana, informed educators that there was no allocation available for their absorption. He further stated that the Head of Department claimed this omission had resulted from an oversight by both the MEC and the Minister.

Mr Shikwambana expressed concern that this contradicted the budget and APP discussions before the Committee. He therefore asked the Minister, the Director-General, and DBE to clarify if funding had in fact been allocated for the absorption of qualified Grade R teachers into the mainstream system. He requested clarity not only for the Free State, but for all provinces nationally. Specifically, he asked how much funding had been allocated for this purpose and why there had been a five-year delay in absorbing educators who had complied with Departmental requirements to improve their qualifications to better teach Grade R learners.

Turning to the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP), Mr Shikwambana argued that DBE needed to be honest about the realities on the ground. While the presentation reflected near 100% provision and achievement in school nutrition, he said his oversight visits to provinces such as North-West and Limpopo revealed a different reality.

He explained that in many schools, the number of learners funded under the NSNP was lower than the total number of learners enrolled. As an example, he described a school with 1,500 learners where DBE only catered for 1,200 learners, leaving 300 excluded from the nutrition programme. As a result, schools and principals often had to compromise by reducing portions or stretching meals to include excluded learners who were not formally funded under the programme.

He questioned if DBE’s statistics and reports accurately reflected the true situation in schools. While he accepted that DBE might technically achieve 100% provision for the learners formally allocated funding, he argued that these ignored thousands of learners who were not included in the programme despite being registered at schools and attending classes every day. He highlighted the injustice of some learners receiving meals while others went hungry simply because they were excluded from Departmental allocation.

Mr Shikwambana therefore requested that DBE provide a national account of the number of learners excluded from the NSNP across the country. He also asked what plans DBE had to ensure that every learner enrolled in a public school under DBE would be included in the nutrition programme and receive meals.

Mr L Komane (EFF) spoke in Sepedi (2:16:16 – 2:18:07; 2:19:10 - 2:19:28; 2:25:43 - 2:26:24). He raised concerns about the instability and inconsistency of the scholar transport system, particularly in KwaZulu-Natal. He described the transport system as having “chameleon colours,” meaning that transport is available at certain times and unavailable at others. He referred specifically to AbaQulusi, where the issue of scholar transport suspension had supposedly been resolved the previous year, resulting in children receiving transport again. However, the problem has resurfaced, and he asked what causes this recurring fluctuation in transport services, why DBE fails to anticipate it, and what factors are influencing the repeated suspension and restoration of scholar transport. He emphasized that children in AbaQulusi are again forced to walk long distances to school despite prior interventions.

He then shifted to discussing school gardens and agricultural education. He questioned who developed the section in the presentation that promotes school gardens and agricultural initiatives, saying he appreciated that part because it acknowledged the importance of agriculture in schools. He contrasted this with what he called the attitude of the “outgoing professor,” whom he criticized for failing to recognize the importance of agriculture and school gardens. He praised the person responsible for including gardens in the presentation, saying that individual “is my professor and the real one.” He then asked practical questions about the implementation of school gardens: where the gardens should come from, who should establish and maintain them, how they should operate, and where they should be located. He argued that agriculture is not adequately recognized at lower levels of education and said that if agricultural education had been properly implemented earlier, learners would already be contributing to food security and sustaining themselves through food production. He linked this to concerns raised earlier in the meeting about learners who are excluded from school nutrition allocations, suggesting that school gardens could help address hunger among those learners.

Mr Komane then questioned DBE about the definition and standard of a “school kitchen.” He asked what exactly constitutes a kitchen, what elements a proper kitchen should contain, how a kitchen should look, and where DBE had seen examples of kitchens that meet acceptable standards. He requested that DBE bring photographs of what they classify as proper school kitchens to the Committee for demonstration purposes. He said the condition of school kitchens observed during oversight visits was disgraceful and unacceptable.

He said he could not even identify what he was seeing because the facilities were in such poor condition. He argued that these conditions reflected the historical marginalization and poor living conditions experienced by black communities, describing the cooking areas as humiliating and degrading. He repeated his request for DBE to clearly define what qualifies as a kitchen, describe its required components, explain where proper examples exist, and provide pictures to the Committee.

He also raised concerns about tuck shops, school vendors, and service provider guidelines. He requested that DBE distribute the guidelines directly to schools because, according to him, some principals personally own and operate tuck shops within schools. He questioned if these principals were following Departmental guidelines or merely implementing their own rules for personal benefit while neglecting learners’ interests. He asked DBE to clarify the content of the guidelines and if schools are actually complying with them.

Finally, Mr Komane referred to page 20 of the nutrition programme presentation, which contained what he described as a “beautiful picture” of a school feeding setup. He asked DBE to identify the school shown in the image, specify the province where it is located, and provide its address because the EFF wanted to visit the school themselves. He questioned if the conditions shown in the picture genuinely existed or if the image had been staged to create a misleading impression. He argued that the reality in provinces such as Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal does not resemble the polished image shown in the presentation. According to him, schools in those provinces do not provide meals of the quality displayed in the presentation, particularly meals including meat. He accused DBE of presenting idealized images that do not reflect the actual conditions in schools and suggested that officials may have specially arranged the scene and brought in food purely for the photograph. He insisted that instead of showing “fancy” examples, DBE should present the worst conditions faced by schools so that real problems can be addressed and improved.

Ms C Jordaan (DA) welcomed the alignment of the Annual Performance Plan (APP) with national priorities but raised concerns about implementation, accountability, and if the plans are financially realistic rather than merely aspirational. She stressed that Departmental plans must translate into measurable, real-world outcomes, not just targets on paper.

She supported DBE’s emphasis on literacy, numeracy, and early learning outcomes, particularly efforts to improve reading comprehension by Grade 4, but argued that stronger and more consistent action is needed to address South Africa’s literacy crisis. She also raised concerns about Early Childhood Development (ECD), questioning how DBE will improve quality management and vetting of ECD practitioners, and if similar quality assurance systems exist for educators in higher grades.

She asked if DBE prioritises actual learner outcomes over administrative outputs such as training plans and monitoring visits, and if these activities are genuinely improving education quality. She further questioned if policy expansion, particularly in ECD and STEM education, is backed by adequate funding, or if there is a gap between ambition and fiscal capacity.

On school safety, she raised concern about reported cuts to funding for security personnel (including 274 positions reportedly reduced) and asked how DBE will ensure implementation of the National School Safety Framework and Safe Schools protocols at provincial level, given ongoing safety risks in schools. She questioned how the impact of these safety measures is being measured and enforced.

She raised concerns about teacher registration, vetting, and misconduct management, noting that during oversight visits many schools could not confirm if their educators are properly registered with professional bodies. She warned that gaps in registration and vetting could affect learning outcomes and student safety, especially where there are risks of employing individuals with prior convictions. She asked if DBE would introduce clearer targets and stronger systems for teacher registration, misconduct tracking, and accountability.

On infrastructure, she acknowledged funding allocations for infrastructure backlogs but raised concern about if spending translates into actual improvements at provincial level. She referenced school closures due to poor conditions and asked how DBE measures if infrastructure funding is effectively implemented by provinces, especially where monitoring appears weak.

Turning to the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP), she commended its scale and importance in addressing malnutrition and supporting learning, and acknowledged positive elements such as food handlers, school gardens, and partnerships with communities. However, she raised concerns about reliability, corruption risks, and if every learner consistently receives meals daily. She highlighted that about 24% of schools are not serving all required food groups, with non-compliance reaching up to 33% in the Northern Cape, despite monitoring by 420 officials. She questioned why compliance issues persist despite oversight structures.

She referred to proposed reforms in the presentation such as a national product catalogue, improved supplier monitoring, provincial warehouse networks, and better last-mile delivery systems and asked for clarity on implementation timelines, funding feasibility, and how these systems will work in provinces with decentralized models. She also asked if the expansion of Grade R has been factored into NSNP planning, given increased learner numbers and resource pressure. Finally, she encouraged stronger Departmental support for school-level partnerships, noting that schools that actively engage with local communities often achieve better nutrition outcomes. She asked how DBE can better enable and support such partnerships beyond existing funding allocations and monitoring systems

Ms L Ngobeni (ActionSA) acknowledged the Committee’s general agreement that DBE’s reports and presentations are coherent at a policy level but argued that there are deeper contradictions that raise concerns about quality, implementation, and real impact.

She said that while the Annual Performance Plan (APP) presents clear targets across access, learning outcomes, and Early Childhood Development (ECD), there is a concern about if these targets are meaningful or merely quantitative. In particular, she questioned the target that 90% of learners progress from Grade 1 to Grade 9, asking what happens to the remaining 10% and arguing that the goal should ideally be full retention so that no learner is left behind.

She noted that although the report appears well-structured and aspirational, the real issue is if it reflects quality outcomes. She highlighted what she called contradictions between reported outputs (such as training sessions, monitoring visits, and feeding programmes) and actual learner performance improvements, especially in literacy and numeracy.

On the National School Nutrition Programme, she questioned the assumption that feeding learners directly translates into improved academic performance, arguing that learner achievement depends on multiple factors beyond nutrition. She asked for clarity on how DBE measures the real impact of the NSNP on educational outcomes, rather than simply tracking the number of learners fed (reported at around 9.4–9.5 million).

She also asked about the remaining gap in coverage, noting that if there are about 20,000 schools, it is unclear what happens in the schools not fully captured in the feeding programme, and if those learners are being adequately supported.

On Early Childhood Development (ECD) and infrastructure expansion, she raised concern that the Medium-Term Framework (MTF) shows reductions in capital expenditure while DBE simultaneously aims to expand access and improve infrastructure. She questioned how expansion is feasible under financial constraints affecting the system’s physical and fiscal capacity.

She raised a social protection concern about undocumented children in schools, including children born to South African parents without documentation and children of undocumented foreign parents. She cited a specific case of a child (referred to as “Matzia”) who has been orphaned and lacks documentation, asking what support DBE provides in such situations and how these learners can continue their education beyond matric.

She also raised concerns about learner safety and wellbeing, particularly in relation to drugs in schools, citing examples from the Northern Cape where learners are exposed to substances allegedly sold in shops near schools. She questioned how DBE links issues like drug exposure to learner performance and if these risks are considered alongside interventions like school nutrition programmes.

She supported earlier calls for clearer definitions of what constitutes a proper school kitchen, referencing poor conditions observed during oversight visits, including unhygienic food preparation environments. She asked DBE to define “model kitchens” and set clear infrastructure standards.

Finally, she raised concern about the Lighthouse Publishers matter, referencing past textbook delivery failures (such as the Limpopo textbook crisis), and asked what safeguards are in place to ensure that current investigations do not disrupt textbook distribution and learning. She also concluded by highlighting that many children are still not in school and asked what urgent measures DBE is taking to ensure access for all learners.

The Chairperson raised three main areas of concern focused on textbooks (LTSM), infrastructure data reliability, and the possible centralisation of the school feeding programme.

First, she questioned DBE’s policy on LTSM, specifically if there is an official policy that requires learners to share textbooks. She raised concern that textbook sharing is widespread in Mpumalanga and makes learning more difficult, especially since learners need textbooks for both classroom and home study. She sought clarity on if DBE is aware of this practice and if it is officially sanctioned. She also questioned what is meant by claims that provinces have achieved “100% coverage” of LTSM through top-ups, asking if this includes shared textbooks or if it accurately reflects reality on the ground.

Second, she raised concerns about school infrastructure data. She referred to provincial infrastructure conditional assessments that are meant to provide updated information on school conditions. However, she said the Committee does not trust the current information provided by DBE because it appears outdated. She asked when the updated conditional assessment results from provinces will be made available so that the true scale of infrastructure backlogs and gaps can be properly understood.

Third, she asked if DBE is planning to centralise the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) at national level, where head office would manage procurement and distribution of food to schools. She requested clarity on if this is the intended policy direction and, if so, how it would be implemented. She also asked what the implications would be for small and medium enterprises currently involved in supplying the programme, particularly how many would be affected or potentially lose business opportunities.

Minister's response

The Minister thanked members for their detailed engagement and acknowledged that many of the issues raised are already known to DBE, while also noting that oversight visits by the Committee help provide important on-the-ground insight into the realities facing schools.

On school kitchens and nutrition infrastructure, raised by several members, the Minister admitted that many school kitchens are in poor condition. In many cases, ordinary classrooms have been converted into kitchens. She explained that improving kitchens is part of DBE’s infrastructure priorities under provincial infrastructure grants, but fiscal pressures often force provinces to prioritise classrooms and sanitation projects instead. She added that DBE is interested in partnering with private sector companies to help improve school nutrition kitchens and acknowledged that proper kitchen infrastructure requires greater attention as sanitation backlogs are addressed.

Addressing broader financial pressures, the Minister explained that the education sector has suffered significant underfunding for over a decade due to national budget decisions that prioritised other areas, including state-owned enterprises and competing government priorities. She stated that provinces are now experiencing the cumulative impact of these funding constraints. While acknowledging that some provinces experience mismanagement or poor budgeting, she argued that the main challenge is structural underfunding. She explained that during budget negotiations DBE was required by Treasury to identify areas where spending cuts could be made because government could not fund all requests. Difficult choices therefore had to be made to preserve core programmes such as the National School Nutrition Programme and grants supporting learners with severe disabilities. She stressed that DBE is constantly trying to protect essential educational services within a shrinking fiscal environment.

On Grade R expansion and teacher absorption, in response to concerns about Grade R practitioners in the Free State, the Minister explained that once Grade R became compulsory, DBE conducted an audit and identified more than 7,000 Grade R practitioners nationally. Practitioners were given opportunities to upgrade their qualifications through institutions such as UNISA in order to obtain Bachelor of Education qualifications required for full educator status. However, not all practitioners qualify for university admission. She acknowledged concerns about delays in absorbing qualified Grade R educators in the Free State and stated that DBE would follow up on the matter.

On infrastructure accountability and monitoring, the Minister explained that DBE’s infrastructure unit is strengthening monitoring processes to ensure provinces spend infrastructure grants effectively and do not underspend despite major backlogs. She reiterated that the national infrastructure backlog is estimated at close to R140 billion and stressed that provinces should not underspend on the Education Infrastructure Grant. She said that expenditure performance is regularly presented to provincial MECs through monitoring platforms, and provinces are held accountable if they fail to spend allocated funds appropriately.

The Minister also acknowledged concerns around overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, and difficulties filling vacancies. She explained that educator employment remains largely a provincial responsibility because compensation budgets are managed at provincial level. However, she recently instructed provinces to revise their post-provisioning norms and ensure that funded vacancies are filled, especially where overcrowding and staffing shortages are severe.

Responding to questions about undocumented learners, the Minister stressed that DBE is guided by the Constitution and relevant court rulings, particularly a 2019 judgment affirming the right of all children to access education regardless of documentation status. She highlighted that many undocumented children are in fact South African citizens who lack proper documentation due to difficult family circumstances, such as being orphaned or raised by grandparents. She argued that denying education on the basis of documentation would violate constitutional rights and unfairly punish vulnerable children.

On the relationship between the National School Nutrition Programme and learner performance, the Minister argued that providing breakfast and meals at schools improves punctuality, attendance, and ultimately educational performance because hungry children cannot learn effectively. While acknowledging that educational outcomes are influenced by many factors, she maintained that school nutrition contributes positively to learner participation and attendance. She explained that DBE uses annual performance plan targets to monitor progress and reports back through mechanisms such as budget speeches and parliamentary engagements.

Finally, on concerns about Learning and Teaching Support Material (LTSM), including textbooks, the Minister assured members that textbook provision for the next academic year should not be adversely affected. She stated that DBE would return to Parliament with further updates and feedback on the matter. She then invited the Director-General and Chief Financial Officer to address additional financial and technical questions raised by members.

Department response

Mr Khunou clarified that DBE does not determine its own budget allocations, as these are allocated by National Treasury. He explained that many of the budget reductions highlighted by Members were therefore Treasury-imposed reductions rather than Departmental decisions. He then addressed specific questions relating to Grade R teacher funding and programme budget variances.

On the issue of Grade R teacher allocations, Mr Khunou confirmed that funding had indeed been allocated for the equalisation of salaries for Grade R educators. For the 2026/27 financial year, a total of R53.2 million was allocated nationally, with the Free State receiving R3.5 million. He further explained that three provinces: Mpumalanga, Northern Cape, and North West, did not receive allocations because they failed to submit the required information when DBE requested provinces to identify qualifying teachers in need of salary equalisation support. He emphasised that provinces were expected to provide names and lists of affected teachers rather than simply request funding. Despite this, he noted that the process remained open and discussions with those provinces were ongoing, meaning they could still motivate for funding allocations.

Mr Khunou further outlined projected increases in Grade R funding over the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF). The allocation is expected to increase from R53 million in 2026/27 to R106 million in 2027/28, and then to R181 million in the 2028/29 financial year. This demonstrated government’s intention to progressively expand support for Grade R educator salary equalisation over time.

He also responded to questions about reductions in Departmental programme budgets. Under Programme 3, goods and services experienced a 74% decrease, falling from R500 million to R132 million. He explained that this was mainly due to the once-off R400 million allocations previously used for training under the Basic Education Employment Initiative (BEEI), which had now ended. Under Programme 4, goods and services declined by 25%, decreasing from R520 million to R390 million, largely because portions of the School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant were shifted and reclassified within infrastructure funding arrangements.

In addition, Mr Khunou noted a reduction in transfers to nonprofit institutions under Programme 4. Funding declined significantly because DBE reduced its contribution to the National Education Collaboration Trust (NECT), arguing that the organisation should increasingly return to its original funding model of relying on self-generated and partner funding rather than depending heavily on DBE resources.

Finally, he addressed reductions in capital assets expenditure, explaining that Programme 4 saw a 62% decrease, from approximately R1 billion to R400 million. This was due to the restructuring of the School Infrastructure Backlogs Grant, which would no longer operate as an internal grant administered directly by the DBE, but instead be incorporated into the broader Education Infrastructure Grant managed externally through provincial allocations. Mr Khunou concluded by stating that these explanations covered the major financial variances raised by Members during the meeting.

National Schools Nutrition Programme

Dr Granville Whittle (Deputy Director-General: Social Mobilisation and Support Services) responded primarily to concerns relating to learner well-being and the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP). He clarified that the NSNP is specifically designed to serve learners in quintile 1 to 3 schools, which explains why approximately 9.4 to 9.5 million learners are fed daily out of the broader national learner population of roughly 13.7 million. He explained that some provinces, such as KwaZulu-Natal, have expanded the programme to include additional learners from quintile 4 and 5 schools without receiving additional budget allocations from the national Department. KwaZulu-Natal, for example, added approximately 200,000 learners to the programme, contributing to some of the financial and operational pressures experienced in the province.

Dr Whittle further explained that provinces such as the Western Cape and previously the Northern Cape also extended feeding schemes to quintile 4 and 5 learners but funded these additional learners through their equitable share allocations. He highlighted the Western Cape’s targeted model, where vulnerable learners in higher quintile schools are identified through indicators such as if their families receive child support grants, social grants, or if learners regularly arrive at school without food. These learners are then incorporated into the nutrition programme.

Responding to concerns raised by Members on learners who remain excluded from feeding schemes, Dr Whittle stated that DBE’s policy is to target 100% of learners within qualifying schools. He invited Members to provide details of schools where learners are allegedly excluded so that DBE could investigate. He also noted that even in schools where meals are available, around 10% of qualifying learners do not make use of the meals on a given day, with the percentage rising to approximately 15% in secondary schools. According to him, this is often influenced by the menu and learner preferences.

On food quality, Dr Whittle explained that DBE aims to provide balanced meals that include all three food groups daily. While most provinces currently serve canned pilchards, chicken livers, soya products, and beans as protein sources, he noted that the Eastern Cape is the only province currently serving fresh chicken once a week as part of an approved provincial menu variation. He also acknowledged the importance of plant-based proteins within the programme.

Addressing concerns about school kitchens and infrastructure, Dr Whittle confirmed that all newly built schools are now required to include nutrition centres as part of the official norms and standards for school infrastructure. He also linked the nutrition programme to broader agricultural and economic development goals, explaining that DBE wants the programme to create employment and business opportunities, particularly for SMMEs, youth, women, and persons with disabilities.

In response to questions about the possible centralisation of the NSNP, Dr Whittle clarified that DBE has moved away from the idea of a fully centralised national model. Instead, the current proposal favours provincial implementation guided by nationally agreed specifications and standards. He stated that the revised model has already been presented to Cabinet twice, but Cabinet requested further consultation with provinces, government Departments, and other stakeholders before finalisation. He added that consultations are currently underway following a directive recently signed by the Director-General.

Dr Whittle also highlighted ongoing collaboration with the Department of Agriculture, particularly around expanding the provision of fruit and vegetables within the nutrition programme. He explained that aspects of the proposed model are inspired by Brazil’s school nutrition system, which integrates local agricultural production with school feeding programmes.

On school safety concerns, particularly in Gauteng where security personnel cuts had been raised, Dr Whittle acknowledged that provinces were under financial pressure and could no longer sustain extremely high security expenditure. Gauteng, for instance, reportedly spent around R600 million on school security services in the previous year. He explained that DBE is instead strengthening school safety Committees, partnerships with police stations, and collaborations with law enforcement agencies in high-risk communities.

Finally, Dr Whittle addressed concerns on drugs and learner well-being. He explained that the Minister participates in an inter-ministerial Committee dealing with gender-based violence and drug abuse. DBE is involved in programmes aimed at closing illegal taverns near schools, conducting searches and seizures in partnership with SAPS, and implementing targeted interventions in schools and communities heavily affected by substance abuse.

Learners sharing textbooks

Mr Mathanzima Mweli (Director-General for Basic Education) clarified that DBE does not have a policy allowing learners to share textbooks, although textbook sharing remains common in many schools, particularly below Grade 12. He acknowledged that this practice should not be happening.

Responding to concerns about the textbook catalogue investigation and its possible impact on textbook delivery, Mr Mweli assured the Committee that the process would not negatively affect the procurement and distribution of textbooks. He explained that the sector already has a structured plan with clear milestones guiding provinces on when textbook orders must be placed. The current cycle involves a major overhaul and replacement of outdated textbooks, with provinces given until 2026 and 2027 to procure updated materials aligned with curriculum requirements.

Mr Mweli further noted that the current controversy relates mainly to concerns surrounding one publisher rather than the entire catalogue development process. He emphasized that there is no indication that publishers listed on the catalogue will be unable to supply textbooks. He also stated that DBE has mechanisms to address allegations of irregularities, including appointing independent auditing firms to investigate concerns when necessary. A more detailed report on the matter would be presented to the Portfolio Committee at a later meeting.

Minister Siviwe Gwarube apologized to the Committee after Mr Ramasedi Mafoko, DBE Acting Chief Director: Infrastructure Planning and Delivery, who was expected to respond to the infrastructure-related questions, disconnected from the meeting platform. She invited the Chairperson and members to raise any outstanding infrastructure concerns so that either she or the Director-General could attempt to respond in his absence.

Follow-up questions

The Chairperson reiterated the Committee’s concerns on the ongoing conditional assessments being conducted by provinces on the state of school infrastructure. She explained that members consistently observe severe deterioration in school infrastructure during oversight visits, despite continued reductions in infrastructure budgets. According to the Committee, the situation on the ground suggests that infrastructure needs are worsening while available funding remains inadequate.

The Chairperson emphasized that the conditional assessment reports are essential because they would provide a clear and updated picture of the extent of infrastructure backlogs and the seriousness of conditions facing schools across provinces. She requested clarity on which provinces had completed these assessments and when the Committee could engage both DBE and provincial Departments on the findings.

She further raised concerns about long-standing infrastructure failures, including schools built more than a decade ago that never received occupation certificates, projects abandoned by contractors, and newer infrastructure that has experienced defects such as leaking roofs since the day of handover. The Chairperson stressed that these problems now extend beyond classroom shortages and increasingly pose health and safety risks to learners.

The Committee also highlighted concerns about aging and potentially unsafe school buildings that have not undergone professional structural assessments for several years. The Chairperson noted that the conditional assessments would help determine if school infrastructure remains structurally sound and would enable DBE to identify urgent risks before learners are exposed to dangerous conditions.

Mr Komane (speaks in Sepedi 3:30:36- 3:30:54). Mr Komane commended Minister Siviwe Gwarube for her engagement with the Committee before raising a follow-up concern on scholar transport in the AbaQulusi Municipality in KwaZulu-Natal. He questioned why recurring problems with scholar transport continue to re-emerge despite previous interventions and resolutions. Mr Komane expressed concern that learners are repeatedly left without transport and are forced to walk long distances to school, which negatively affects their safety and well-being.

He further raised concerns about the condition of old mobile classrooms that remain in use at some schools despite being severely dilapidated. Mr Komane warned that these structures have become safety hazards for learners and educators. He specifically referred to conditions at Madiatlali School in the Ephraim Mogale Municipality, emphasizing the urgent need for intervention to address unsafe infrastructure in schools.

The Chairperson urged Committee members to continue raising concerns relating to specific schools, particularly where issues had not yet been adequately addressed. She emphasized the importance of members communicating directly with DBE on individual schools so that DBE could provide targeted and detailed responses rather than only discussing matters in general terms. The Chairperson explained that identifying specific schools would allow the Committee and DBE to track concrete interventions, monitor progress, and receive clear feedback on the measures being implemented to address infrastructure, safety, or service delivery challenges at those schools.

The Minister responded by first noting that the Head of Infrastructure, Mr Mafoko, was no longer available on the platform. She requested permission to submit the conditional assessment reports per province in writing, so that members could receive a clear, documented response as requested. She also agreed with the Chairperson that infrastructure safety is not merely a compliance exercise, but fundamentally about ensuring dignity and safe learning and teaching environments.

On the issue of performance indicators and strategic planning raised by Mr Mkhize, the Minister acknowledged the concern but argued that “two things can be true at the same time” within DBE’s SWOT analysis. She explained that certain areas, such as financial management, may simultaneously reflect both strengths and weaknesses, particularly where improvements are still required despite existing systems.

The Minister also reiterated that there is no additional funding available to expand the budget, emphasizing that the education sector has faced long-term underfunding over approximately a decade. She stated that provinces have had to absorb unfunded mandates, including the expansion of Grade R, without corresponding increases in funding.

She stressed that because of these fiscal constraints, DBE must exercise strict financial prudence. However, she agreed with members that where conditional grants such as infrastructure funding are allocated, there should be no justification for underspending given the scale and urgency of infrastructure backlogs.

Closing Remarks

The Chairperson thanked the Minister and Departmental officials for their responses and reiterated the importance of receiving comprehensive written submissions on outstanding matters. In particular, the Chairperson requested that the written responses must address concerns raised by Mr Komane on the widespread use of container classrooms. It was emphasised that these containers were originally intended as temporary relief structures to address overcrowding but have now become permanent classrooms in many schools across the country. In some instances, entire schools consist solely of container structures. The Chairperson further noted that many of these containers are poorly maintained, sometimes delivered incomplete, and lack essential facilities such as electricity and cooling systems like fans, resulting in difficult learning and teaching conditions, especially during extreme heat. The Chairperson stressed that DBE’s response should clearly outline the scale of the problem as well as the government’s plan to address or phase out these structures.

The Chairperson further indicated that Parliament would communicate details on any follow-up meetings, particularly in relation to the issues that have also been highlighted in the media. It was noted that efforts would be made to finalise a meeting with Mpumalanga before the oversight report is concluded, although scheduling challenges were acknowledged. The Chairperson concluded by stating that a final oversight report would be compiled and shared with members, and formally brought the meeting to a close, thanking all participants.

Meeting adjourned
