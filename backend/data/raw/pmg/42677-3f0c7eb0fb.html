Update on FMD outbreak; ARC, NAMC & PPECB Q1 and 2 2025/26 Performance; with the Minister

Agriculture

24 March 2026
Chairperson: Ms D Pule (ANC)
Share this page:

Meeting Summary

Video

The Portfolio Committee met to receive updates from the Department of Agriculture on the Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) outbreak and mass vaccination strategy, and to consider performance and expenditure reports for Q1 and Q2 from the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), the National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC), and the Perishable Products Export Control Board (PPECB).

The Department reported that FMD outbreaks had been detected in all nine provinces. A total of 2 531 900 vaccine doses had been secured, with 1 179 583 administered. Members raised concerns about discrepancies between national and provincial figures, delays in laboratory confirmations, and challenges posed by communal grazing.

The ARC reported progress toward its target of producing 20 000 vaccine doses per week, currently achieving approximately 15 000 doses. The NAMC presented an unqualified audit opinion with material findings, and reported linking 155 farmers to markets against a target of 60. The PPECB reported a 5.2% increase in agricultural exports, largely driven by citrus volumes, while noting an 80% decline in exports to the Middle East. It had maintained its clean audit status and reported R82 million spent on broad-based black economic empowerment (B-BBEE) procurement.

Members expressed doubts over the reliability of the statistics presented on the FMD outbreak; criticised the delay in mobilising a response to the outbreak; asked whether vaccine doses were being prioritised for large-scale commercial feedlots, or if they were being distributed to support subsistence and smallholder farmers; questioned the Department’s reliance on international institutions for vaccine verification; and called for consideration of some form of compensation for the losses sustained by farmers.

The Chairperson raised concerns regarding the appointment of the new NAMC board, citing Section 4 of the Marketing of Agricultural Products Act. The Minister committed to ensuring compliance with legislative requirements, and to providing a detailed breakdown of the R16 million fund shifts in Gauteng.

Meeting report

Chairperson's opening remarks

The Chairperson, Ms D Pule (ANC), officially opened the meeting and welcomed the Minister of Agriculture, the Director-General, and representatives from various entities. She expressed the Committee's appreciation for the Minister’s consistent attendance, noting that his presence allowed for direct accountability and immediate responses to Members' queries.

She specifically commended the Department for adhering to the Committee's request for weekly Tuesday updates on the FMD outbreaks, highlighting that this consistency was vital for the Committee’s oversight role.

The Chairperson informed the meeting that the Committee had recently concluded an oversight visit to the Free State province. While the Minister had been unable to attend due to prior commitments, the Director-General had joined the sessions virtually to provide necessary technical clarity, particularly regarding FMD protocols in the province. She concluded by stating that the Committee was currently finalising its findings, and that the formal recommendations from the oversight visit would be submitted to Parliament and shared with the Department in due course.

Minister’s remarks

Mr John Steenhuisen, Minister of Agriculture, expressed his appreciation for the invitation to the Committee, noting that his presence facilitates direct accountability and allows for immediate responses to concerns raised by Members. He acknowledged the Committee's recent oversight visit to the Free State, and confirmed that the Department had noted the apologies and participation of officials during that process.

Regarding the FMD crisis, he emphasised that the Department remained committed to the weekly update schedule requested by the Committee. He said that while the spread of the disease to all provinces was a significant challenge, the Department was prioritising the mass vaccination strategy and the procurement of necessary vaccine strains.

In response to concerns regarding the expiration of the Agricultural Research Council (ARC) board's term, the Minister assured the Committee that the Department was aware of the legislative timelines. He committed to meeting all statutory obligations regarding the nomination and appointment process, and promised to inform the Committee in a timely manner when the formal process commences.

Department of Agriculture’s update on FMD

Dr Botlhe Modisane, Chief Director: Animal Production and Health, DoA, provided a status update on the FMD outbreaks and the Department’s mass vaccination strategy. He reported that FMD had now been detected across all nine provinces, with several new and suspect cases identified. Recent developments included a new outbreak confirmed among dairy animals in the Creighton Valley in KwaZulu-Natal (KZN), and persisting cases in the Lehurutshe and Dinokana areas of the North West. In Limpopo, active cases remained in several regions, including Modimolle, Waterberg and Vhembe. He specifically highlighted a strong suspicion of an outbreak at a feedlot in Douglas in the Northern Cape, and a suspect case in Bonnievale in the Western Cape. He noted that while these cases were currently classified as suspect based on clinical symptoms, the Department was awaiting final laboratory results for formal confirmation.

Regarding the mass vaccination strategy, the Department had secured a total of 2 531 900 vaccine doses from the Agricultural Research Council (ARC), Dollvet and Biogenesis Bago. To date, 1 179 583 doses had been administered, leaving a balance of 1 352 317 doses. Specific allocations for recent interventions included 15 000 doses for Creighton Valley, 10 000 doses each for the North West and Limpopo, and 4 000 doses for the suspect case in the Western Cape.

In terms of future procurement, Dr Modisane said the Department was ready to order 1.5 million additional doses from Biogenesis Bago. Furthermore, a South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA) Section 21 permit for two million doses from Dollvet had been secured following confirmation on 18 March 2026 that the vaccines comply with Southern African Territories (SAT) 1, 2, and 3 strains. The Department was currently waiting for SAT 3 testing results from the Pirbright Institute in the United Kingdom to finalise the process. The Department recommended that the Committee note the progress of the vaccination strategy and the current provincial status of the disease.

Discussion

Mr C Smit (DA) noted that while the Department classified certain outbreaks as "highly suspect" based on clinical signs, there was a significant lag in receiving formal laboratory confirmation. He asked for the specific timeline regarding the suspect case in Bonnievale in the Western Cape, specifically inquiring when the suspicion had been first documented, and how soon the 4 000 allocated vaccine doses would actually be administered to the animals.

He raised a sharp technical concern regarding the Department’s reliance on international institutions for vaccine verification. He questioned why the SAT 3 results had to be obtained from the Pirbright Institute in the United Kingdom instead of being processed by South Africa’s local laboratories, such as those at the Agricultural Research Council (ARC). He demanded to know the cost implications of sending samples and seeking confirmation from an overseas facility, and whether this indicated a lack of domestic capacity to handle high-level strain testing.

Further, Mr Smit scrutinised the "Section 21" permit process for the two million doses from Dollvet. He pointed out that although the Department had received confirmation of compliance for SAT 1, 2, and 3 strains on 18 March, the vaccination rollout still seemed to be pending further results. He asked for a clear explanation of the administrative or regulatory bottlenecks that prevented a more rapid deployment of these vaccines once they arrived in the country, arguing that the delay between "suspect" clinical signs and "confirmed" laboratory results created a window of vulnerability for farmers.

Ms L Arries (EFF) began by expressing serious concern regarding the "highly suspect" cases mentioned in the presentation, particularly those in the Northern Cape and Western Cape. She questioned the Department on the precise turnaround time for laboratory results once a sample was collected, arguing that leaving farmers in a state of "suspicion" without confirmation created unnecessary panic and economic paralysis. She demanded to know why, if clinical signs were already visible, it took so long for the Directorate to provide a definitive status that would allow for the immediate commencement of vaccination.

She further scrutinised the Department’s laboratory capacity, asking whether the Onderstepoort Veterinary Research (OVR) facility or any other state laboratories were currently overwhelmed by the fact that the disease was now present in all nine provinces.

She concluded by asking for a detailed breakdown of the 10 000 doses allocated to the North West, specifically inquiring whether these doses were being prioritised for large-scale commercial feedlots, or if they were being distributed to support the subsistence and smallholder farmers in the Lehurutshe and Dinokana areas, who were often the hardest hit by movement restrictions.

Ms N Ndalane (ANC) welcomed the updated report from the Department. She specifically wanted to know how the Department intended to monitor and report on vaccine efficacy. She also asked about the potential side effects of the vaccines being administered, and what mechanisms were in place to track these effects among the livestock populations in the affected provinces.

Inkosi R Cebekhulu (IFP) highlighted the critical issue of land availability for livestock grazing in rural settlements. While travelling to Johannesburg, he had seen large herds of cattle kept in areas with insufficient grazing fields. He expressed concern that many livestock owners in rural areas kept more animals than the land could support, leading to overgrazing and competition for small, overcrowded spaces. He suggested the Department engage directly with these owners on the risks of overstocking. He urged the DoA to coordinate with other government departments on land availability to avoid social clashes. He pointed out that these land pressures had led to challenges during the current crisis, noting instances in some areas where residents resisted taking their livestock for vaccinations due to existing tensions and disputes over grazing territory.

Mr Z Mthethwa (MKP) raised several strategic concerns about the Department's response, specifically the need for an integrated approach with the Department of Land Reform. He argued that access to grazing land and quarantine facilities was a major hurdle for emerging and communal farmers. He proposed that as a matter of strategy, the Department must identify land pockets to allow for the equitable disintegration of the national herd, noting that the current communal grazing setup, where one shepherd collects cattle from multiple households, directly conflicted with outbreak protocols.

He highlighted that while state land existed, it was often "hoarded" or ring-fenced by a few commercial farmers, specifically citing examples in Parys, where SA National Roads Agency Ltd (SANRAL)-owned land was not being redistributed.

On the issue of pig farming, he commented that the protocols used for cattle were not suitable for pigs, which procreate rapidly. He warned that without an exit strategy, quarantine measures would lead to an unmanageable increase in pig numbers.

Mr Mthethwa addressed the lack of confidence in the vaccine within black communities. He explained that deaths occurring after vaccination were often misinterpreted by farmers as being caused by the vaccine itself, rather than side effects on an already compromised immune system. He urged the Department to engage the 5 000 unemployed agricultural practitioners to provide community education and "demystify" these deaths, as these practitioners could explain complex veterinary concepts in a language that local farmers understand.

Mr A Mgxitama (MKP) raised two primary questions regarding the vaccine distribution and the Department’s overall capacity to manage the outbreak. He requested a breakdown of how vaccines were distributed between small-scale and large-scale farmers, noting that in the South African context, this often aligned with racial demographics. He questioned the effectiveness of the current strategy, pointing out that while the national herd consisted of roughly 12 to 15 million head of cattle, the Department had mentioned only one or two million vaccine doses. He asked how the Department intended to cover the total herd to prevent recurring flare-ups.

He also raised his "perennial" concern regarding the penalties for policy transgressions. He argued that existing legislation did not provide effective punishment, and asked if there was any policy thinking directed at strengthening punitive measures to discourage transgressors.

Mr Mgxitama demanded an update on the R16 million allegedly shifted from small-scale farmers in Gauteng to FMD interventions. He said that despite previous assurances that such a shift was not permissible by policy, subsequent presentations had suggested the funds were indeed moved from the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme (CASP). He was in contact with affected farmers who were facing evictions and struggling to produce. He argued that this movement of funds sabotaged black farmers and undermined the land reform programme, and requested a clear update to provide to the "anxious and suffering" farmers in Gauteng.

Mr A Trollip (ActionSA) expressed cautious optimism regarding the Department's claim of having sufficient vaccines. However, he noted this did not align with findings from a recent oversight visit to the Free State, where shortages were reported. He highlighted the distress of farmers with uninfected herds who were unable to access vaccines.

He raised concerns about the stalled arbitration between the Department and litigating organisations, noting that since arbitration had failed, litigation would proceed. He specifically referred to a press statement from Agri Eastern Cape (part of Agri SA) that mirrored concerns raised by the Southern African Agricultural Initiative (SAAI), Sakeliga, and Agri Free State. These concerns included a lack of coordination between the Ministerial Task Team, the Industry Coordinating Committee, and farmers.

Mr Trollip pointed out that the current regulatory framework was outdated, as it was designed for maintaining FMD-free status without vaccination, whereas the new strategy required FMD-free status with vaccination. He argued that the Department was "chasing the disease," rather than staying ahead of it.

Regarding vaccine production and procurement, he requested an update on whether the ARC/OBP was meeting its target of producing 20 000 doses per week by the end of March. He also flagged a discrepancy between Dr Modisane’s oral briefing, stating that Biogenesis vaccines would arrive "any day now,” and the data provided on page 8 of the report, asking for specific clarification on the status of various incoming vaccines.

Mr Trollip addressed the issue of under-declaration. He said that many farmers feared the "zero income" consequences of quarantine, leading to significant under-reporting of outbreaks. He noted that the infection statistics provided by Agri EC were much higher than the Department’s figures, and asked Dr Modisane to reconcile these differences, emphasising that current distribution numbers seemed inadequate to meet the requirements for both infected herds and necessary booster shots.

Dr N Nkabane (ANC) welcomed the update on the distribution strategy, and requested a sense from the team on the feasibility of the six-month booster shot protocol. She noted that a major concern raised during the oversight visit was whether the Department had the capacity to procure enough vaccines to comply with this six-month requirement for affected livestock.

She referred to a study by the National Library of Medicine about the benefits of public-private partnerships (PPPs) in combating FMD. She said that outsourcing vaccine supply chain functions to the private sector could accelerate procurement, distribution, and management. She highlighted that the study, conducted in the Western Cape, showed that such partnerships led to more effective reach and efficient management.

Dr Nkabane emphasised the socio-economic impact of the disease, pointing to the distress and devastation faced by farmers across the country. She asked for an outline of the programmatic steps and processes the Department was currently embarking on to bring the private sector on board, and if lessons from the Western Cape study were being deployed nationally.

Ms Arries noted that the Committee had raised these issues many times before. She highlighted the "cry of the farmers" heard during the previous week’s oversight visit, emphasising that people were losing their livestock, which served as their primary livelihood. Addressing the Minister directly, she asked what compensation these farmers would receive, as many had lost everything. She requested specific information on how much funding was available to compensate farmers who had lost their only source of income due to the outbreak.

The Chairperson raised serious concerns regarding the discrepancy in infection data, noting that while the Department’s presentation cited 316 cases in the Free State, provincial statements indicated the number had already exceeded 400. She argued that these outdated figures suggested a critical failure in laboratory turnaround times, preventing the Committee from receiving a real-time understanding of the outbreak.

She specifically questioned why the Department was not collaborating with other state institutions like the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) to expand laboratory capacity. She emphasised that reaching the 80% vaccination target required a multi-departmental effort, and asked for clarity on why it remained difficult to integrate these other entities under the Department's existing guidelines.

The Chairperson also focused on the role of municipalities in managing communal grazing. She noted that farmers in communal areas found it impossible to separate their herds, which facilitated the continued spread of the disease. She asked the Minister for an update on engagements within the Ministers and Members of the Executive Council (MINMEC), and specifically if there had been discussions with those responsible for municipalities, to find land-use solutions. She stressed that without municipal assistance to provide space for separating animals, the disease could not be effectively contained in commonage areas.

DoA's response

Minister Steenhuisen began by acknowledging the gravity of the concerns raised by the Committee, noting that the FMD outbreak was not merely a technical veterinary challenge, but a national economic emergency. He emphasised that the Department was operating under a shift in strategy, moving from a "FMD-free without vaccination" status to a "FMD-free with vaccination" status, which necessitated massive scaling of resources and coordination.

Addressing concerns about the "lag" in laboratory confirmations, the Minister admitted that the current system was under immense pressure. He explained that while clinical signs allowed the Department to label a case as "highly suspect," international trade protocols and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) standards required rigorous molecular confirmation before a case was officially added to the national tally. This explained why the Department’s numbers might appear lower than those reported by provincial departments or farmers on the ground. The Department reported only "confirmed" cases to maintain the integrity of South Africa’s international veterinary standing.

Regarding the use of other laboratories, he clarified that the issue was not a lack of political will to collaborate, but rather the strict "Biosafety Level 3" (BSL-3) requirements needed to handle the live FMD virus. Most laboratories in the country were not equipped to prevent the accidental release of such a highly contagious pathogen. However, he committed to exploring how the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) or university labs could assist with non-live diagnostic components to speed up the turnaround time.

The Minister also addressed the ongoing litigation, standing by his characterisation of certain litigating parties as "mega-fringe" organisations. He argued that while they represented a vocal segment of commercial agriculture, their legal actions often focused on narrow interests that complicate a coordinated state response. He clarified that the Department was not "anti-farmer," but must prioritise the national herd over individual commercial preferences for specific vaccine brands.

Regarding the Biogenesis vaccines and the Dollvet doses mentioned in the report, the Minister assured the Committee that the "Section 21" permits were a temporary measure to bridge the gap while local production at the Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP) and the ARC scaled up. He confirmed that the target of 20 000 doses per week was being pursued aggressively, but laboratory verification of these batches, specifically ensuring they match the SAT 1, 2, and 3 strains currently circulating, could not be bypassed.

Responding to queries about the challenges of communal grazing, the Minister agreed that the land issue was the "Achilles' heel" of FMD containment. He acknowledged the reality that in a communal setup, a single shepherd often mixed herds from many different households, making "quarantine" almost impossible under current conditions.

He informed the Committee that he had already begun high-level engagements with the Minister of Land Reform and the Minister responsible for Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs. The goal was to identify state-owned "land pockets" or commonages that could be used as temporary quarantine stations. He stressed that FMD was not just a "cow disease," but a "land management disease." He said that without the power of municipalities to enforce "standstill" orders on commonage land, the Department’s veterinary efforts were effectively trying to mop up the floor while the tap was still running.

Minister Steenhuisen also addressed concerns about the perceived bias toward large-scale commercial farmers. He insisted that the vaccination strategy was "risk-based," not based on the race or scale of the farmer. Vaccines were deployed where the risk of spread was highest to protect the export value of the entire industry, which supported thousands of black farm workers and emerging farmers.

On the specific matter of the R16 million allegedly shifted from the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme (CASP) in Gauteng, the Minister promised a detailed written breakdown. He explained that while policy generally prohibited shifting funds meant for farmer support into operational disease control, the sheer scale of the FMD crisis in late 2025 had forced "emergency reprioritisations" at the provincial level. He committed to investigating if these funds could be "replenished" to ensure that small-scale farmers in Gauteng were not sabotaged in their production efforts.

The Minister touched on "side effects" and "vaccine fatigue" among livestock owners. He admitted that the Department had not done enough to communicate the science of the vaccine to rural communities. He acknowledged that when an animal died shortly after being jabbed, often it was because it was already in the incubation phase of the disease, but farmers naturally blamed the vaccine.

He welcomed the suggestion to involve the 5 000 unemployed agricultural practitioners. He suggested that these graduates could serve as "veterinary ambassadors" to go into villages and explain, in local languages, that the vaccine was a shield, not a cure. He noted that the Department was looking at a "stewardship program" to employ these graduates to assist with the massive 80% vaccination target set for the end of the year.

Finally, addressing the question of compensation, the Minister delivered a difficult message. He explained that current Treasury regulations did not provide a "blanket compensation" fund for animals culled or lost due to FMD, as it was classified as a "controlled disease," where the owner shares responsibility for biosecurity. However, he noted that the Department was looking into "socio-economic relief" packages, rather than direct "market-value compensation," to help subsistence farmers who had lost their primary source of protein and income.

He concluded by stating that the Department's primary focus must remain on the 80% coverage to lift movement restrictions as quickly as possible, as the restrictions themselves were the greatest cause of economic distress.

Dr Modisane addressed the technical and operational queries regarding laboratory capacity, vaccine stocks, and the discrepancy in provincial data.

He began by clarifying the confusion regarding the infection numbers in the Free State. He explained that the Department’s official report of 316 cases, compared to the 400-plus reported by provincial officials, was a result of the verification lag. While a farm may be quarantined on suspicion based on clinical symptoms observed by a field technician, the case entered the national database only once the Onderstepoort Veterinary Research (OVR) laboratory confirms the presence of the live virus through molecular testing. He acknowledged that this process was currently a bottleneck, and admitted that the Department was effectively working with yesterday's data to manage today's crisis.

Regarding laboratory capacity, Dr Modisane provided a detailed update on why the Department could not simply use any commercial or university lab. He emphasised that the FMD virus was highly contagious and required BSL-3 containment. Currently, only the OVR and the ARC have the specialised infrastructure to handle the live virus safely. However, he revealed that the Department was in the final stages of auditing two private sector laboratories to see if they could be accredited for diagnostic testing, which would significantly reduce the pressure on the state system and improve turnaround times.

On the issue of vaccine supply, he turned to the data on page 8 of the presentation to reconcile the numbers. He confirmed that while the ARC and OBP had a target of 20 000 doses per week by the end of March, they were currently hovering at approximately 15 000 doses due to consistency challenges in the production of the SAT 3 strain. To bridge this gap, the Department had authorised the Section 21 emergency import of two million doses from Dollvet and an additional consignment from Biogenesis in Argentina. He assured the Committee that these imported vaccines had been tested against South African field strains, and were highly effective.

Dr Modisane also addressed the concern regarding the six-month booster protocol. He explained that for the vaccination strategy to be successful, and for South Africa to regain its FMD-free status with vaccination, livestock in the high-risk red zones must receive a follow-up shot every six months. He admitted that the current distribution of 1.2 million doses was enough only for the primary round in priority areas. However, the procurement pipeline for the second half of 2026 was already being secured to ensure that the immunity wall did not crumble due to missed boosters.

He acknowledged that the fear of zero income from movement restrictions was a massive deterrent for farmers to report signs of the disease. To combat this, he proposed a modified quarantine approach, where clean animals from a restricted farm could potentially be moved directly to an abattoir for controlled slaughter under veterinary supervision. This would provide farmers with an exit strategy and an income stream, encouraging them to report outbreaks early rather than hiding them and risking further spread.

Dr Modisane addressed the gaps regarding booster vaccinations and the financial bottlenecks that had previously hindered supply. He acknowledged that while slide eight outlined the current inventory and incoming shipments, it did not explicitly separate doses intended for primary vaccination from those required for boosters. He clarified that the 1.2 million doses currently being distributed were prioritised for the initial round of vaccinations in high-risk districts. However, the Department was finalising a procurement schedule specifically for the six-month booster cycle to ensure the immunity wall remained intact for animals that had already received their first dose.

Addressing the issue of late payments, he admitted there was a hiatus in production and delivery due to administrative delays in processing invoices for vaccine producers. He explained that these delays were largely due to the transition between financial years, and the surge in emergency procurement requests. To prevent a recurrence, he informed the Committee that the Department had now established a dedicated task team within the finance directorate to fast-track payments. This team was mandated to process invoices for Section 21 imports and local production from the ARC and OBP within 14 days to ensure that the supply chain for the two million Dollvet doses and Biogenesis shipments remained uninterrupted.

Regarding the involvement of the CSIR and other external laboratories, Dr Modisane explained that while the Ministerial Task Team (MTT) had found the ARC to be "over-identified" and capable of handling high volumes, they had indeed identified other labs to assist. However, he highlighted a significant biosecurity hurdle -- FMD was highly infectious, and laboratories must meet Biosecurity Level 3 standards to handle it safely. To manage this risk, all samples must first be processed at the OVR facility for decontamination before being sent to other labs for testing. This protocol ensured the disease was not accidentally spread during the testing process.

Addressing the confusion over infection statistics, Dr Modisane clarified the distinction between "suspected" and "confirmed" cases. He noted that other entities often included "suspicions" in their tallies, whereas the Department strictly reported cases that had been laboratory-confirmed. This explained why the Department’s official figures, such as those for the Western Cape or high-risk areas, may appear lower than reports circulating from other sources.

On the financial front, he provided an update on the R16 million CASP funding issue in Gauteng. Following an internal investigation, he confirmed that the Gauteng Department of Agriculture had not moved funds away from the allocated CASP budget. Instead, they had submitted an additional presentation to request supplementary funding specifically for the FMD response, ensuring that the original support for small-scale farmers remains intact.

Finally, Dr Modisane detailed the current status of vaccine orders. He said the Biogenesis Bago vaccine from Argentina was manufactured and ready for shipment, pending the finalisation of the South African order. While there were potential logistical challenges related to conflict in the Middle East, the Department was also working with Dollvet. Although SAHPRA had approved six million doses for Dollvet, the current agreement was to import these in two million-dose batches. He added that an additional five million doses were being ordered from Biogenesis Bago to ensure a robust national supply.

Mr Dipepeneneng Serage, Deputy Director-General (DDG): Agricultural Production, Biosecurity, and Natural Resources Management, provided a detailed clarification on the R16 million funding controversy and the status of laboratory collaborations.

Addressing the confusion regarding the R16 million in Gauteng, he explained that there was no shifting of funds from earmarked farming activities. Instead, he clarified that within the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme, all provinces were required to allocate 6% of their budget toward biosecurity. The R16 million in question was already part of this pre-existing biosecurity allocation intended for managing outbreaks like FMD. He attributed the misunderstanding to a public statement by the Member of the Executive Council (MEC) which may have unintentionally made it sound as though funds were being diverted from other programmes when in fact, it was a legitimate use of the biosecurity line item.

Regarding the collaboration with the CSIR, he confirmed that high-level meetings between the Department and the CSIR had already taken place, with a follow-up session scheduled for April. While there was a desire to decentralise testing, FMD was a "trade-sensitive" disease that spreads with extreme ease. Unlike COVID-19, where many labs could be repurposed, FMD required specialised handling. He reiterated that samples must first go to the OVR facility for deactivation before they could be safely tested elsewhere. He explained that a mobile laboratory strategy, while effective for human health during COVID-19, was not currently feasible for live FMD samples due to these strict biosecurity protocols.

To address the current testing backlog at the ARC, he revealed an operational intervention involving the temporary relocation of provincial laboratory technologists to Pretoria to bolster the capacity at the OVR. The goal of this surge was to clear the existing backlog within the next five months.

Finally, he addressed the long-term strategy for achieving FMD-free status without vaccination. He said this was a staggered, carefully monitored process. He highlighted that provinces like the Western Cape and Northern Cape were being managed in a way that may allow them to be released for exports earlier than originally imagined. He cautioned against the use of rapid test kits by farmers, noting that all data must be centrally collated to build the international case for South Africa’s eventual freedom from the disease.

Further discussion

The Chairperson raised two critical issues concerning provincial accountability and the accuracy of technical data in the Department’s report, specifically regarding slide nine. Firstly, addressing the CASP, she expressed concern that the Department currently lacked specific targets because it was still waiting for provinces to submit their plans. She pointed out that if provinces were failing to submit these plans, it created a significant risk for farmers. Since CASP funds were held at the provincial level, the lack of coordination meant the Department could not effectively assist farmers or meet its national targets. She questioned how the Department intended to support farmers when provincial participation remained a bottleneck.

Secondly, the Chairperson challenged the conflicting information presented in slide nine of the Department’s report. She noted that while the Minister and officials had spoken optimistically about vaccine arrivals, the written report indicated that results for the Biogenesis Bago vaccine were still outstanding, specifically waiting for SAT 3 matching results from the Pirbright Institute in the United Kingdom. She also highlighted a discrepancy regarding the Dollvet vaccine. While the Committee had been led to believe the process was further along, the report stated that results had not yet been received and that the permit application was only just commencing. She demanded a clear correction and an update on the status of these vaccines, noting that the mixed messages were creating confusion within the Committee. She specifically asked for the status of the Pirbright results, and a timeline for when the outstanding data for both Biogenesis and Dollvet, would be addressed.

Mr Trollip expressed the Committee's frustration regarding the perceived lack of transparency and the "disconnected dots" in the Department's reporting. He pointed out that the Committee was frequently given conflicting justifications for the Department's actions. For instance, officials often cited requirements from the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) to justify certain restrictions, only for the Committee to later find that such specific requirements may not exist. He further highlighted the confusion surrounding the legal status of the vaccines, noting that while the Department had argued they were "unregistered medicines," SAHPRA had already granted Section 21 permits for their emergency importation.

Mr Trollip emphasised that it was nearly impossible for the Committee to perform its oversight role without a clear, step-by-step explanation of the regulatory and logistical process. He specifically requested that the presenter (Dr Modisane) stop glossing over the details, and speak directly to the data presented in the report, which seemed to contradict the verbal updates provided by the Minister.

Minister Steenhuisen provided a firm response to the Committee's concerns, dismissing allegations of "late payments" as a manufactured narrative, and clarifying the current status of vaccine imports. Regarding the perceived confusion on slide nine, he explained that the situation had evolved rapidly in the 24 hours since the presentation was shared. He confirmed that SAHPRA had now officially issued the import permits for the Dollvet vaccine under the Section 21 emergency process. He clarified the regulatory framework, noting that the Department must either register a medicine under Act 36 or, in cases of emergency, utilise the Section 21 application. The Department had ordered six million doses from Dollvet, with the first two million currently being shipped, and the remaining four million to follow in subsequent batches.

On the Biogenesis Bago vaccine, the Minister confirmed an order of 1.5 million doses. He acknowledged that while SAT 3 results from the Pirbright Institute were still awaited, the vaccine had already demonstrated the necessary efficacy during field testing on the ground. He emphasised that all future imported vaccines would be required to go through the Pirbright process, as requested by SAHPRA.

The Minister then addressed the assertions regarding non-payments or late payments to vaccine producers, labelling them as "fake news." He reminded the Committee of the Department's obligations under the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), which prohibits government from paying in advance for products. He explained that payment was released only once the goods had been received, verified against the order, counted, and tested. Specifically regarding Biogenesis Bago, he said the delay was caused by a mismatch between the delivered goods and the invoice provided. Once the invoice was corrected and resubmitted, the Department processed the payment immediately. He stressed that both he and the Director-General (DG), as the accounting officer, had a legal responsibility to comply with the PFMA to avoid adverse findings from the Auditor-General (AG). He concluded by stating that the Department would not bypass these financial controls, despite external pressure or alternative narratives.

Mr Trollip concluded by pressing for a direct answer on the discrepancies in the Department's own report, refusing to accept the Minister's dismissal of the issue. He pointed out that the status of the Biogenesis Bago vaccine -- specifically the "outstanding results" for the SAT 3 strain matching from the Pirbright Institute -- was a matter of record in the document provided to the Committee, not a "figment of anyone's imagination." He insisted on receiving a factual update on when those results were expected. Furthermore, he criticised the Minister for projecting how Members might react to future AG reports, stating that such comments were unhelpful. He emphasised that the Committee’s role was to deal with the "live" information and the specific report currently in front of them, rather than speculative future scenarios.

The Chairperson intervened to address the Minister's tone, requesting that he respond to questions directly without casting aspersions on any Member of the Committee. She noted that while the Minister had not explicitly labelled Members as problematic, his manner of response suggested a desire to discourage questioning, which she emphasised could not happen in a parliamentary setting.

She defended the role of the Committee, stating that Members must feel free to ask questions as part of their constitutional oversight duty. She clarified that their inquiries were driven by a genuine interest in ensuring the Department's work, specifically regarding the FMD crisis, and effectively assisted the farmers and the public whom the Members represent.

The Chairperson firmly addressed the Minister's grievances regarding the Committee's line of questioning, emphasising that the Minister should not "overread" the intent behind the Members' inquiries.

The Minister argued that respect must be mutual, and expressed frustration that the questions implied incompetence or a lack of control over the crisis, but the Chairperson interrupted to clarify that no such accusations had been explicitly made. She instructed the Minister to focus strictly on answering the factual substance of the questions rather than interpreting underlying motives or perceived aspersions. From her perspective, the Members had remained respectful, and the Minister must maintain that same standard of professional respect in return, regardless of his frequent appearances before the Committee.

Minister Steenhuisen continued his point by emphasising that his focus on the PFMA had been a direct response to previous episodes where the Department and the Minister himself had faced intense scrutiny and heavy criticism from the AG for non-compliance. He argued that if he were to bypass these financial regulations now, he would undoubtedly face the same criticism in future oversight sessions.

Addressing the technical concerns one last time, he provided a detailed update on the vaccine procurement process. Regarding the Biogenesis Bago vaccine, he acknowledged that the SAT 3 matching results from the Pirbright Institute were indeed outstanding. He reminded the Committee that they had previously criticised the Department for failing to send strains for matching; having now done so, the Department was simply awaiting those external results. Despite this, he reiterated that the Department was satisfied with the efficacy shown in the field, and had placed an order for 1.5 million doses, which would arrive once the permit was finalised.

Regarding the Dollvet vaccine, the Minister confirmed that an order for six million doses had been placed following the approval of the import permit by SAHPRA within the last 24 hours. These doses were expected to arrive in staggered shipments over the coming months. He added that an additional five million doses from Biogenesis Bago would be secured once the SAT 3 results were formally confirmed by Pirbright. He concluded by offering to have another official provide further explanation if his summary remained unclear, reiterating that the Department was moving as quickly as regulatory and laboratory processes allowed.

The Chairperson concluded the exchange by advising the Minister to prioritise clarity and communication. She said that when the Department’s written reports did not align with the verbal updates, such as the news regarding the SAHPRA permits from the last 24 hours, Members were not only entitled, but obligated, to ask questions. She emphasised that if information was not documented, the Committee could not be expected to know it, and she urged the Minister to respond to these inquiries with respect rather than frustration.

She reiterated that effective communication was essential to ensure that the Department and the Committee did not "miss each other" in the pursuit of understanding how the FMD crisis was being managed.

Director-General's clarifications

Mr Mooketsa Ramasodi, Director-General, DoA, provided essential technical and administrative clarity to the Committee, focusing on the legal specifics of vaccine permits, compensation for farmers, and the broader economic impact of the outbreak.

Addressing the "24-hour" confusion regarding vaccine permits, he explained that two separate legal instruments were at play. While SAHPRA provides authorisation for the medicine, the Department must issue a permit under Section 6 of the Animal Diseases Act to allow for the actual importation. He acknowledged that the distinction between these two legislative requirements, one under SAHPRA’s mandate and one under the Department’s, had likely caused the confusion, and committed to providing a clearer written breakdown for the Committee.

On the pressing issue of farmer compensation, Mr Ramasodi referred to Section 19 of the Animal Diseases Act. He clarified that the current law was highly prescriptive, primarily allowing for compensation only when the state proactively destroys animals to control a disease. Because the current losses fell outside these narrow parameters, the Department was in active discussions with National Treasury to develop a relief framework that addresses the unique circumstances farmers were currently facing.

Regarding biosecurity and the "F-branding" of cattle, the DG noted that South Africa's loss of its FMD-free status necessitated a legal pivot. He revealed that Section 9 of the legislation was currently under consultation to be amended. This change was intended to address the "economic sabotage" mentioned by Members, ensuring that vaccination and branding protocols did not lead to the unfair market exclusion of healthy animals during the recovery phase. He also highlighted the need to revisit the Fencing Act and traditional land management practices to better separate grazing and cultivated areas.

Finally, Mr Ramasodi updated the Committee on the economic assessment of the crisis. He confirmed that the National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC) had presented a preliminary study to the Department just yesterday. This study explored the impact of the outbreak beyond the agricultural sector, analysing its effects on the gross domestic product (GDP), inflation, and other macro-indicators. He promised that once the NAMC and the Bureau for Food and Agricultural Policy (BFAP) finalised their results, a comprehensive presentation on the total economic impact would be brought before the Committee.

Entities' briefings on their Q1 and Q2 performance 

Agricultural Research Council

Ms Joyene Isaacs, Chairperson, Agricultural Research Council (ARC), opened the presentation by framing the FMD crisis as a significant disaster that had underscored the vital importance of agriculture to the entire country. The current debates highlighted how deeply agricultural issues affected the lives of every citizen, a realisation she believed was essential for the sector to embrace.

Reflecting on the timing of the session, Ms Isaacs pointed out that the ARC was nearing the end of its financial year. She suggested that while the current reporting covered the first and second quarters, the next time the Council appeared before the Committee, they would likely be providing the final reports for the year. She also emphasised that the morning’s discussions demonstrated that no agricultural organisation operated in a silo -- the ARC, the Department and other entities, were deeply interdependent and must support one another to meet the sector's requirements.

A key point of clarification made by Ms Isaacs was the distinction between the ARC and Onderstepoort Biological Products (OBP). She observed that the media and public often confused the two because the ARC manages the Onderstepoort Veterinary Research (OVR) facility, which sounds similar to OBP. She stressed that OBP was a separate entity ,and not part of the ARC, asking the Committee to keep this distinction in mind as they reviewed the Council's performance.

Dr Litha Magingxa, President and CEO, Agricultural Research Council (ARC), began his presentation by addressing the technical complexities of resuming local FMD vaccine production after a 20-year hiatus. He clarified that while a breakthrough batch of 12 900 doses was officially handed over to the Minister on 6 February, this was the result of a long-term research process that began in 2010.

He emphasised that although the vaccine was registered as a stock remedy in May 2022, registration did not immediately equate to manufacturing readiness. He explained that scaling up production was not simply a matter of increasing volume -- it involved managing highly sensitive biological processes under strict Level 3 biosecurity protocols. To manage this transition, the ARC had adopted a phased ramp-up strategy.

Starting in March 2026, the ARC had reached its first major milestone of supplying 20 000 monovalent doses per week. These doses focused on the specific SAT 1, SAT 2, or SAT 3 strains currently circulating in the region. He also outlined the medium-term goal -- by 2027, the facility was projected to scale up to 200 000 monovalent doses per week, with an ultimate target of producing 20 million doses annually once the new full-scale factory was operational.

Dr Magingxa also addressed the diagnostic backlog mentioned earlier in the meeting. He confirmed that the ARC was currently relocating lab technologists from provincial departments to the Onderstepoort Veterinary Research (OVR) facility. This surge in personnel was specifically designed to clear the existing testing backlog within five months, ensuring that the "war on FMD" was supported by real-time scientific data.

Mr Abdul Carim, Chief Financial Officer (CFO), ARC, provided a strategic overview of the Council’s financial health, noting that the organisation was currently performing ahead of schedule compared to previous years.

He reported that as of the end of September, external income had exceeded projections, resulting in a better-than-budgeted surplus. The ARC remained on track to achieve its target of R1.1 billion in external revenue for the year. While operational expenditure had lagged slightly due to procurement delays, personnel costs had risen above the initial budget. Mr Carim clarified that this increase was a deliberate result of filling contract positions necessary to meet the deliverables of the Service Level Agreement (SLA) with the Department.

Regarding the balance sheet, he acknowledged that cash flows were currently under some strain. The Council was actively engaging with the Department to resolve outstanding payments related to the SLA, but he assured the Committee that the ARC remained in a healthy position from both a solvency and liquidity perspective.

A significant portion of his report focused on the ARC’s audit turnaround. After seven years, the Council successfully achieved an unqualified audit report last year. To maintain this momentum, it had implemented a robust Audit Improvement Plan (AIP), which included a 100% verification of fixed assets using the new NetTrace and Sage economic recovery programme (ERP) systems, addressing the primary cause of previous qualifications. Mr Carim also noted that the Council was working closely with internal audit to clear matters of emphasis regarding Generally Recognised Accounting Practice (GRAP) implementation and irregular expenditure. To avoid the friction experienced during the last audit cycle, the Council had initiated earlier engagements with the AG's team to ensure a smoother process for the coming year.

National Agricultural Marketing Council

Dr Simphiwe Ngqangweni, CEO, National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC), led the presentation by reflecting on his long history with the organisation and the leadership transitions that had occurred over the last several years. He credited the previous Council for resolving internal challenges, which had allowed the current executive team to focus on its core legislative mandate of providing policy advice and coordinating regulatory measures within the agricultural sector.

He reported that the NAMC had successfully met its performance targets in two programmes, and significantly exceeded them in a third. A major milestone for the organisation was the transition from a qualified audit to an unqualified audit report, an achievement attributed to addressing previous capacity challenges within the finance division. In terms of market access, the Council had facilitated market linkages for 155 farmers, far surpassing the initial goal of 60 for the first two quarters of the 2025/26 financial year. This success was driven by strategic collaborations with both provincial public and private sector partners.

Regarding policy advice, the Council had provided critical analysis on high-impact topics such as rising energy costs, the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA), and the impact of European Union (EU) regulations on the South African citrus industry. Dr Ngqangweni specifically confirmed that the NAMC was currently finalising a study on the economy-wide impact of FMD, examining its broader effects on GDP and inflation. A significant highlight of these advisory services was the Minister’s approval to include indigenous crops and medicinal plants under the Marketing of Agricultural Products Act, a move projected to inject an estimated R12 billion per year into the agricultural economy.

Finally, Dr Ngqangweni commented on the NAMC’s international contributions during the G20 proceedings. The organisation had provided substantial technical input to the Agricultural Working Group and the Food Security Task Force, which had ultimately been adopted into the final declaration by the heads of state.

Ms Lebogang Dire, CFO, NAMC, presented the financial performance and audit recovery status, highlighting a significant transition toward fiscal stability. For the first quarter, the Council had operated with a total available budget of 73% against an allocation of R51.8 million, with actual spending recorded at R14 million. By the second quarter, the budget had been adjusted to R62.4 million following National Treasury's approval to roll over funds committed to previous projects. At the end of that period, the Council had utilised 55% of its resources, leaving an available budget of R34.3 million. Ms Dire noted that by the end of the third quarter, available funds stood at 32%, emphasising that budget monitoring was a continuous daily and weekly process to ensure responsible spending.

A major focus of the presentation was the NAMC’s audit recovery plan. The CFO confirmed that the organisation had successfully moved from a negative audit outcome to an unqualified report. While findings were still present, there had been a substantial reduction in the financial area. As of the end of the second quarter, 62% of audit recommendations had been fully implemented, with the remainder partially addressed. However, over 90% of these recommendations had now been resolved. This progress was verified quarterly by internal auditors and reported to the Audit and Risk Committee to prevent repeat findings in the 2025/2026 financial year.

To secure these gains, the NAMC had already initiated kickoff meetings with external auditors for an interim audit. This early engagement was cited as a key intervention that helped remedy issues before the finalisation of previous reports. Ms Dire also addressed the root causes of prior challenges, noting that while the position of Manager for Financial Reporting had been filled, the Council still struggled to recruit a management accountant due to budget constraints.

Finally, she highlighted the full implementation of a new digital records management system. This system allowed for the digitisation of all records, ensuring that information was easily retrievable for auditors and further strengthening the organisation's move toward a clean audit outcome.

Perishable Products Export Control Board

Mr Lucien Jansen, CEO, Perishable Products Export Control Board (PPECB), provided an overview of the organisation’s performance for the first two quarters of the 2025/2026 financial year, noting a significant growth in South African agricultural exports. He reported that 3.9 million pallets were exported during the period under review, marking a 5.2% increase compared to 2024. This growth was primarily driven by record-breaking citrus exports. The European Union (EU) remained the largest market for South African fruit, accounting for 37% of exports, followed by the Middle East at 16%, and Asia at 15%.

However, he highlighted a sharp decline in trade with the Middle East due to regional instability. He noted that comparing March 2026 to March 2025, there had been a staggering 80% drop in exports to that region. In response, the PPECB had been working closely with the Department of Agriculture to assist exporters in diverting their produce to alternative markets.

Regarding human resources, the CEO explained that the board’s staffing levels fluctuated, based on seasonal demand. During the peak citrus season in the first quarter, the workforce relied heavily on temporary employees, who made up 51% of the 1 241-strong staff complement. As the season tapered off in the second quarter, the reliance shifted back toward permanent staff.

The organisation’s performance ratings had shown steady improvement throughout the year. While the PPECB had achieved a 71% rating in the first quarter, this had risen to 93% by the end of the second quarter. Only one out of 14 key performance indicators remained unfulfilled, which related to a slight shortfall in onboarding customers to the "Intellect" data exchange platform. Mr Jansen expressed confidence that the board would close this gap of four customers and meet all targets by the end of the financial year.

Ms Aneesa Moosa-Camroodien, CFO, PPECB, reported a strong financial performance for the first half of the 2025/26 financial year, driven largely by high citrus export volumes. In the first quarter, the board had generated R210 million in revenue against R174 million in expenditure, resulting in a R36 million surplus. By the end of the second quarter, total income had reached R479 million, representing 101% of the budget. While citrus remained the primary revenue driver, the CFO noted that maise volumes continued to underperform. The total expenditure for the six-month period was R357 million, leaving a half-year surplus of R123 million. Ms Moosa-Camroodien clarified that this surplus was seasonal, and typically normalised during the quieter period following the citrus season, but confirmed that the organisation's financial position remained stable, with no material concerns.

Regarding governance, the CFO announced that the PPECB had achieved a clean audit, noting that an audit improvement plan was therefore unnecessary. While there were limited instances of fruitless, wasteful, and irregular expenditure, she assured the Committee that these had been detected, disclosed, and addressed with corrective controls. No financial losses were incurred from these matters, and the board had since condoned the expenditures following the application of consequence management.

Finally, she provided a closing update on a long-standing penalty matter with the South African Revenue Service (SARS) dating back to July 2024. She explained that the organisation had reached administrative finality after exhausting all appeals and third-party support without resolution. To prevent a recurrence, the PPECB had updated its internal controls to ensure all future payments were scheduled on weekdays, rather than Saturdays, to avoid processing delays. The board had officially condoned the value of the penalty, and the matter was now considered concluded from the organisation's perspective.

Discussion

Mr Trollip raised critical questions regarding the timeline and specifics of the vaccine batches recently distributed by the ARC. He reminded the Committee that during an oversight visit to the Free State, he had inquired about the manufacturing date and batch numbers of the vaccines distributed on 6 February. Specifically, he questioned how the ARC could distribute those vaccines if current manufacturing was still in a ramp-up phase, and he sought confirmation on whether the Council would truly meet the target of 20 000 doses per week.

While commending the ARC for its unqualified audit and successful hosting of the G20, he pointed out a discrepancy in the reporting. He noted that the Council had recorded the production of over 72 000 vaccines against a target of 30 000 and requested clarity on exactly which vaccines those were, given the ongoing shortages.

Turning to the NAMC and the PPECB, Mr Trollip addressed the ongoing trade challenges with the European Union. Despite the EU being South Africa's largest citrus market, he highlighted that it continued to impose prejudicial phytosanitary requirements, particularly concerning False Codling Moth (FCM) and Citrus Black Spot (CBS). He asked both entities for an update on any advancements made in negotiating or resolving these restrictive requirements, which continued to threaten the sustainability of the citrus export industry.

Ms Arries raised several pointed questions regarding underperformance and governance across the ARC, NAMC, and PPECB.

Directing her first set of inquiries to the ARC, she expressed concern over the Smallholder Farmer Development programme, noting that the Council had failed to meet its Quarter 1 target of supporting 1 500 farmers, and did not report any figures for Quarter 2. She requested the actual number of farmers supported to date, an explanation for the reporting gap, and a clear timeline for corrective measures.

She also challenged the ARC on its internal capacity, specifically regarding the high vacancy rates in critical units. She questioned why the Council had not proactively addressed these gaps, particularly as they had led to missing all targets for intellectual property registrations and technology transfers. She sought to understand the root causes behind the failure to register any of the three planned intellectual properties (IPs), or execute the six planned technology transfers.

Turning to the NAMC, Ms Arries requested clarity on the status of the Council itself, noting that the previous term had expired in May 2025, and questioning whether a new Council had been formally appointed and why it was not reflected in the presentation. She also pushed for updates on the NAMC's transition from a purely advisory role to one that implements programmes directly, specifically asking for the current status of the feasibility assessment for a "once-off transformation levy."

Finally, she addressed the PPECB regarding the operational crisis at the Cape Town container terminal. She inquired about the extent of service disruptions to inspection and certification during this period, and asked why there had been reported personnel shortages during the peak export season. The Chairperson eventually intervened to manage the time, leading Ms Arries to agree to submit her remaining detailed questions in writing to allow other Members an opportunity to speak.

Ms Ndalane welcomed the presentations from the three entities before directing specific inquiries toward the ARC and the NAMC. Regarding the ARC, she sought clarity on the specific measures being implemented to address the targets that had been missed during the first quarter. She also requested a firm timeline for the completion of the asset register updates, which had been a point of concern in previous financial reports. Furthermore, given the reported decline in external revenue, she asked for the Council's long-term strategy to ensure its financial sustainability.

Turning to the NAMC, she focused on inter-agency cooperation, asking how the Council collaborates with other entities to support the broader agricultural sector. Specifically, she inquired whether there were any concrete plans to expand the agri-technologist programme to increase technical capacity within the industry.

Mr Mthethwa delivered a critical set of questions to the PPECB and the NAMC, focusing on demographic transformation and the structural "fault lines" exposed by the FMD crisis. He challenged both entities to provide data on the origins of export products, specifically asking if they could distinguish demographic parity in the contributions to the harbour. He argued that FMD had highlighted the neglected issue of land in the agricultural sector, pointing out that effective disease control required quarantine facilities and adequate grazing land. He called on the NAMC to move beyond standard research and advise the Department on restoring the land debate as a fundamental necessity for South African agriculture.

Addressing the ARC, Mr Mthethwa questioned the core objective of their FMD strategy, asking whether they were merely managing the disease or working toward its total eradication. He criticised the ARC for being in the "baby stages" of vaccine production while the economy suffered, and questioned why there had been no early warning signs. Drawing a comparison to seasonal flu projections, he expressed frustration that the Council appeared to be caught off guard and forced into a reactionary position without sufficient local stock.

Finally, he urged the NAMC to adopt a more intentional and measured approach to market access. He noted that during the FMD outbreak, the competition for market space had revealed discriminatory labelling practices that he believed deliberately sabotaged certain demographic quarters. He insisted that these limitations manifested as demographic inequality in the sector, and demanded that the Council address these systemic barriers to ensure all farmers could compete on an even footing.

Mr Mgxitama expressed significant frustration regarding the consistency of the reporting and the scale of the targets set by the agricultural entities. He began by critiquing the last-minute updates to performance data, noting that the Committee had initially been told targets had been missed, only to be informed during the meeting that they had been met. He appealed for complete and accurate documentation to be provided well in advance to avoid "ambushing" Members with shifting information.

A central theme of his critique was what he termed "planning against reality." While the ARC and NAMC had reported exceeding their training and support targets, such as training 609 individuals against a target of 140, Mr Mgxitama argued that these figures were arbitrary and disconnected from the true scale of the sector. He pointed out that with an estimated 2.5 million small-scale farmers in South Africa, setting targets in the dozens or hundreds achieved negligible impact. He questioned the methodology used to determine these values, suggesting they were often set low simply to ensure easy achievement.

Finally, he challenged the PPECB on the economic reality of transformation in the export sector. While the board had reported an impressive 96% broad-based black economic empowerment (B-BBEE) achievement rating, Mr Mgxitama demanded a breakdown in "rands and cents." He specifically asked for a comparison of the actual income generated by large-scale versus small-scale farmers through the export process. He argued that reporting on 110 small-scale certifications was insufficient when the vast majority of the "quantum of money" in the export market remained concentrated, and he called for a more honest reflection of how much black economic empowerment was actually occurring in financial terms.

Ms S Davids (ANC) welcomed the presentations from the three entities, and directed specific operational and strategic questions to each. Addressing the ARC, she inquired about the practical impact of the new ERP system on the Council's day-to-day operations. She sought clarity on the specific strategies being employed to improve collaboration with external stakeholders.

To the PPECB, she focused on the large seasonal workforce. She asked whether there were active skills transfer programmes in place for unskilled seasonal workers, and specifically whether there was a career path or "pipeline" that allowed seasonal employees to transition into permanent roles over time.

Finally, she questioned the NAMC on the global geopolitical landscape. Given the ongoing international conflicts mentioned in their report, she requested a focused assessment of the specific negative and positive impacts these wars were having on South Africa's agricultural markets and food security.

Entities' responses

Agricultural Research Council

Dr Magingxa provided a comprehensive technical and operational rebuttal to the concerns raised by the Committee, specifically clarifying the timeline for vaccine production and internal capacity challenges.

Addressing the confusion surrounding the 6 February vaccine distribution, he explained that vaccine development was a cumulative process. While the specific vaccine had been registered in 2022/23, registration did not equate to immediate deployment. He clarified that the 72 000 doses mentioned in the report were "blood vaccines," and not the new FMD strain-specific doses. The current shift to producing 20 000 doses per week was possible only now because the installation of the mid-scale production system had just been completed. He expressed full confidence in meeting this target moving forward, guided by the Department’s epidemiological data.

Regarding the perceived failure to meet smallholder farmer support targets, the CEO clarified that the targets were not "missed" in the long term, but had been delayed due to the modernisation of the Interis system. This system configuration had initially hindered the recording of services rendered to farmers in Quarter 1, but the ARC had since "caught up," and the figures were now fully reconciled.

Dr Magingxa acknowledged the Council's vacancy constraints, but said it was fast-tracking the hiring of laboratory technologists registered with the South African Veterinary Council (SAVC), with the first ten staff members starting at the end of March to clear the diagnostic backlog. On the lack of intellectual property (IP) registrations and technology transfers, he explained these were subject to external dependencies, including departmental approvals and private sector contract negotiations, which often extended beyond the ARC's internal timelines.

He also confirmed that the "Achilles' heel" of the ARC, the asset register, had been addressed through a 100% verification of over 80 000 lines. This had been achieved using the new NetTrace system, which allowed for real-time tracking of assets across the country, providing the Council with high confidence heading into the next audit cycle.

Responding to the "management vs eradication" debate, Dr Magingxa referred to the WOAH's progressive control pathway. He explained that South Africa was currently pursuing FMD-free status with vaccination as a primary milestone. He cautioned that total eradication (FMD-free without vaccination) was a long-term goal that took countries like Brazil 20 years to achieve, suggesting a realistic ten-year horizon for South Africa’s national strategy.

National Agricultural Marketing Council

Dr Ngqangweni addressed the Committee’s concerns regarding governance, market transformation, and international trade pressures, providing clarity on the Council’s evolving role. He confirmed that a new Council had been formally appointed with effect from 10 March. While he acknowledged the presence of the newly appointed Chairperson at the meeting, he deferred to the Department for the release of the full media statement listing all members.

Regarding the NAMC's shift from a purely advisory body to an implementation agency, Dr Ngqangweni noted that while a review of the Act was underway, the current focus remained on statutory guidelines. He highlighted that at least 20% of industry levies were now mandated for transformation, with efforts being made to augment these funds through public-private partnerships (PPPs) and government programmes like the CASP.

The CEO also detailed the Council's collaborative efforts, specifically a memorandum of understanding (MOU) with the PPECB to synchronise data on certified farmers and facilitate better market access. Responding to concerns about demographic parity, he noted that the value of produce from black participants in commercial value chains had risen from 11% in 2019 to approximately 13% recently. He attributed this progress to the Agriculture and Agro-processing Master Plan (AAMP), and suggested that sustained coordination would likely see this figure continue to climb.

Finally, regarding the impact of global conflicts and trade barriers, Dr Ngqangweni said that while a formal study on the war's impact was still in the pipeline, the immediate effects were evident in rising oil prices and input costs, which drive food inflation. On the matter of EU trade restrictions and phytosanitary disputes, he deferred to the regulatory expertise of the PPECB to provide a more technical response.

Perishable Products Export Control Board

Mr Jansen concluded the session by addressing operational challenges at the Cape Town terminal and the board's efforts toward transformation and skills development.

Regarding the disruptions at the Cape Town Container Terminal, he said that while severe winds had caused significant issues from December through February, the PPECB had played a central role in the coordination team. By providing real-time information to Transnet on booked loadings, the board had helped to improve logistical planning during the crisis.

Addressing allegations of staff shortages during the peak season, he said that despite reaching out to industry bodies and clients in the Port Elizabeth (Gqeberha) region to investigate specific complaints, no formal details of missing personnel had been provided. He reaffirmed that client satisfaction remained the board's primary objective.

Mr Jansen also highlighted the success of the Agricultural Export Technologist Programme, which had grown from 20 students in 2008 to 50 per year. He said most graduates were either absorbed into the PPECB as permanent staff, or employed by the broader industry. On the transition of seasonal workers, he explained that the board reviewed its permanent staff baseline annually against export volumes. This process allowed many temporary employees to be appointed to permanent positions each year, while others benefited from skills transfer that made them highly sought after by the private sector.

On the issue of transformation and data, the CEO reported that the PPECB had spent R82 million on procurement from B-BBEE-registered suppliers by the end of the second quarter. While the board did not currently mandate that exporters disclose their farmer category, Mr Jansen committed to collaborating with the NAMC and the Department of Agriculture to better trace the demographic origins of export products, using Product Unit Codes. He concluded by noting that the board remained reliant on provincial departments to identify smallholder farmers for training and certification support.

Department of Agriculture

Dr Serage addressed the ongoing phytosanitary disputes with the EU regarding Citrus Black Spot (CBS) and False Codling Moth (FCM). He confirmed that as of November 2025, the matter remained at the dispute resolution stage within the World Trade Organisation (WTO) framework. South Africa had requested the establishment of independent technical panels to adjudicate the case, but the process was moving slowly. While the CBS dispute had persisted for over 20 years, and the FCM regulations for nearly ten, there had been no further advancement in reaching a final resolution beyond the appointment of independent advisors.

Despite the slow legal pace, Dr Serage highlighted a key point of South Africa's defence -- even with decades of citrus exports to the EU, and various interceptions, neither the CBS nor FCM had established itself within the EU. He argued that this served as empirical proof that South African fruit did not act as a pathway for these pests and diseases, supporting the government's stance that the EU's measures were unscientific and unnecessarily restrictive.

Chairperson’s concluding remarks

The Chairperson concluded the meeting by thanking the Minister, the representatives from the Department, ARC, NAMC, and PPECB, while raising a significant procedural concern regarding the appointment of board members. She specifically addressed the Minister regarding the newly appointed NAMC board. Citing Section 4 of the Marketing of Agricultural Products Act (Act 47 of 1996), she reminded the Minister that the legislation mandated parliamentary committees to be informed and invited to submit nominations when vacancies arise. She expressed disappointment that the Committee had not been formally notified of the new appointments, noting it was "awkward" to meet the new NAMC Chairperson without a prior introduction.

Looking ahead, the Chairperson issued a formal reminder regarding the Agricultural Research Council board, whose term was set to lapse mid-2026 (likely between May and July). The Minister was urged to initiate the nomination process transparently, and inform the Committee in time to ensure legislative compliance and allow for the encouragement of diverse candidates.

Minister Steenhuisen acknowledged the Chairperson's concerns, and committed to meeting all future legislative obligations regarding board appointments.

Due to changes in the parliamentary programme, the meeting originally scheduled for 31 March would be rescheduled.

The meeting was adjourned.
